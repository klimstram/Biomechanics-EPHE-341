Virtual Muscle Lab

The Virtual Muscle Lab, built by Jim Martin at the University of Utah with Jenna Link and Marc Klimstra, running here in the course site. Read the table from the bottom up. Force–velocity alone would give about 33 J a cycle. Add force–length and it drops. Add the real switching times instead and it drops further. Both together is what the muscle actually does. At 2 Hz the timing costs about three times what the length–tension relation costs, which is the opposite of what most people guess. Then take the cycle frequency to 4 Hz and read it again — the real muscle goes negative while the theoretical one is still making work.
The same muscle model, driven by a real person instead of a sine wave. The kinematics are measured: 31 markers on an Optotrak Certus, one rider on a treadmill at 20 km/h in gear 3, 82 rpm. Eighty-two pedal revolutions averaged into one stroke. The knee swings 72 to 131 degrees and the hip 83 to 119. What is NOT measured is force — this dataset has no pedal forces and no EMG — so the force is the page-one model driven by the measured length. Say that out loud; it is the point, not a disclaimer. Start on Vasti. One joint, so the length trace is just the knee angle upside down: longest at top dead centre, shortest at the bottom. About 47 mm of excursion. Then switch to Hamstrings and watch the excursion collapse to about 24 mm. Nothing got smaller — the hip and the knee are both extending through the down-stroke, and for a two-joint muscle those cancel. That is why a biarticular muscle can make force through a big joint excursion while barely changing length, which is the cheapest way to make force there is. Then move Switch on and Switch off and watch the loop open, close and flip over.
And now the same stroke with nothing modelled in it. Three instruments: an ergometer reading pedal torque every two degrees of crank, motion capture at 100 Hz, and surface EMG at 2148 Hz. One participant, 109 steady revolutions averaged. The honest bit first: these are three separate machines with three clocks. They were lined up by matching the sequence of revolution durations between the ergometer and the motion capture — the two agree at r = 0.98 with 16 ms of scatter and no drift over two minutes. The check that it worked is on the slide: integrate the measured torque over the crank and you get 162 W; the ergometer's own display said 161 W. Half a per cent apart, from two completely different routes. The torque panel is the work loop with the model taken out. Shaded green is work done, shaded red is work absorbed — and there IS red, late in the stroke, where the leg is being driven round by the other one. That is the bit riders do not believe. Then the EMG. Vastus lateralis peaks at 97 per cent — before top dead centre, not after it. The muscle switches on while it is still being lengthened, so that force is already there when the crank arrives where it can be used. Hamstrings peak at 22 per cent, gluteus maximus at 6. Put the dashed length trace beside it and the question from page one answers itself: is the muscle on while it is shortening? For the vasti, yes, almost perfectly. That is a work loop drawn by a person rather than by a slider.

About the Virtual Muscle Lab

Developed by Jim Martin, Jenna Link and Marc Klimstra — University of Utah and University of Victoria.

A Thelen-type muscle model driven through a sinusoidal length cycle. The same cycle is computed under four conditions so you can see what each property costs: force–velocity alone, plus force–length, plus the real time it takes to switch on and off, and all three together.

This is the EPHE 341 build, which runs entirely in the browser. The original is at jimmartinutah.github.io/VML.
