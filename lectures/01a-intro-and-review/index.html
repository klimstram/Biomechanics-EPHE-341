Introduction

What is biomechanics?

The study of the effects of internal and external forces on the human body in movement and at rest.

Bio — any biological system.

Mechanics — the branch of the physical sciences concerned with the state of rest or motion of bodies subjected to the action of forces. Rigid body, fluid, deformable body.

The word to notice is internal. External forces can be measured — you stand on a plate and read the number. Internal ones cannot, and they are the ones that decide whether a tendon tears or a hip replacement lasts.

Almost everything this field knows about what the inside of a body is doing is calculated, not measured. The last three lectures of this course are how that calculation works.

Define it, then immediately say why it is not obvious. The internal/external split is the thing to plant on day one, because the entire arc of the course runs along it: measure the outside, compute the inside. Lectures 19 and 20 are where that gets cashed out, and it helps enormously if they have been waiting for it since week one.

Introduction

Qualitative and quantitative

Qualitative — describing movement without numbers. A coach watching a stride, a clinician watching a gait. That is EPHE 201.

Quantitative — putting numbers on it. That is this course.

Neither is the better one. A clinician has fifteen minutes and needs a judgement; a researcher testing a new rehabilitation protocol needs a measurement. Each answers questions the other cannot.

What the quantitative approach buys is the ability to be wrong in a checkable way — which is the whole reason this course spends so much time on units and sanity checks.

His slide 5 and its speaker note. The note is good and worth reading almost verbatim: both approaches are valuable, with different ranges of application and limitations. Add the bit his slide does not say: the reason to put numbers on something is that numbers can be checked. A qualitative judgement that is wrong is hard to catch; a number that is wrong fails a units check or a sanity check. That framing makes the rest of the lecture feel like a toolkit rather than a hurdle.

Introduction

The four words you will hear all term

Statics — bodies at rest, or moving at constant velocity, with balanced forces acting. Lecture 19.

Dynamics — the relationship between motion and the forces that cause it. Lecture 20.

Kinematics — the spatial and temporal components of motion: position, velocity, acceleration, with no regard to force. The description of movement.

Kinetics — bodies in motion with respect to mass, force and energy. The causes of movement.

Kinesis, from the Ancient Greek, is motion — and kineo is to put in motion. Kinematics describes; kinetics explains. Half the course is each.

His slides 6, 7 and 8 in one. The kinematics/kinetics distinction is the one they will get wrong on the midterm, so land it with an example rather than a definition: a video of a jump is kinematics; the force plate under it is kinetics. Same jump, two measurements, and you need both to say anything useful. Statics and dynamics are the other axis, and it is worth saying that constant velocity counts as statics. That surprises people.

What it is for

To measure or predict internal and external forces, in:

Divider. The word to use is fluency, not difficulty.

Basic Math

Three properties, and everything else

Commutative — order does not matter.

a + b = b + a   a × b = b × a

Associative — grouping does not matter.

(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)

Distributive — a multiplier outside a bracket reaches every term inside it.

a(b + c) = a·b + a·c

Every one of the sixteen theorems on his slide 24, and every step of every rearrangement you will do this term, is one of these three applied once.

His slides 20 to 23. Resist the urge to rush. The distributive property is the one that breaks: students multiply the first term in the bracket and forget the second, and it costs them in every force-resolution problem for the rest of the term. Note that commutativity is where subtraction and division are NOT included, which is worth saying because a − b ≠ b − a is the source of half the sign errors in the course.

Basic Math

Three kinds of equation

One unknown. Isolate it. Whatever you do to one side you do to the other.

Two unknowns. You need two equations. Isolate a variable in one, substitute it into the other, solve, then back-substitute.

A quadratic. Anything of the form ax² + bx + c = 0, which has two answers.

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a

Quadratics turn up in this course the moment anything is thrown: a projectile is at a given height twice, once going up and once coming down, and the two roots are those two times.

His slides 28, 29 and 30 set up. The projectile connection is worth making now rather than in week eight. It explains why a quadratic has two roots in a way that "that is what the formula gives" never will: the ball really is at two metres twice. It also explains why you cannot just take the positive root and go home — sometimes both are physical.

Trigonometry

The part of the review that you will use every single week, because almost every force in this course arrives at an angle.

Divider, and a justification. Forces are vectors, vectors have directions, and the only way to add two of them is to break them into components. That is trigonometry, every week, from lecture 4 onward.

Trigonometry

Eight things about lines and triangles

  • Two lines are perpendicular when they meet at 90°.
  • Parallel lines never intersect.
  • Where two lines cross, opposing angles are equal.
  • The angles around an intersection sum to 360°.
  • The angles along one line at an intersection sum to 180°.
  • A line crossing two parallels makes a Z, and the corresponding angles are equal.
  • Three non-parallel lines make a triangle.
  • A triangle’s interior angles sum to 180°.

The Z rule and the 180° rule between them are how you find an angle you were never given — which is most of what free body diagrams ask you to do.

His slides 32 and 33. All eight are correct. Dwell on the Z. When a limb is at an angle and a force is vertical, the angle you need is almost never the one marked on the figure; it is a corresponding angle two steps away. Students who know the Z pattern find it in seconds and students who do not guess.

Trigonometry

Where sine and cosine come from

His slide 34 prints the three graphs and says they can be memorised. They can, but there is no need: they are one point going round one circle. Press Go round and let it run a full turn before saying anything. The height of the point is the sine. Its sideways distance is the cosine. The tangent is one divided by the other, which is why it blows up at 90 degrees — the radius is vertical and there is no sideways distance left to divide by. Then press the special angles. His slide 35 is a table of five rows to memorise; those five rows are five places on this circle, and the table lights up as you pass each one. That is a much better thing to carry into an exam than a memorised table.

Trigonometry

Three relations, and when to use each

Right-angle triangle.

sinθ = opposite / hypotenuse

cosθ = adjacent / hypotenuse

tanθ = opposite / adjacent

and Pythagoras: hypotenuse² = adjacent² + opposite².

Any triangle.

a / sinA = b / sinB = c / sinC

a² = b² + c² − 2bc·cosA

Which one you use is decided by what you were given, not by what you want. Two angles and a side → the sine law. Two sides and the angle between them → the cosine law.

His slides 34 and 36. The callout is the thing to teach. Students learn three boxes and then freeze because they do not know which box to open. The rule is mechanical: look at what you were given, count sides and angles, and the law picks itself. Say also that the right-angle formulas are not a third thing — they are the sine and cosine laws with one angle at 90 degrees. The next figure shows that happening.

Trigonometry

One triangle, all three relations at once

This is the figure to spend time on. Drag a corner and let them watch. Three things happen at once and all three are worth pointing at. The angles always sum to 180. The three sine-law ratios stay equal to each other while every one of them changes. And the cosine law keeps reproducing the side you can measure. Then press Make it right-angled and switch to the right-angle tab. The cosine law's last term is 2ab·cos 90°, which is zero, so it has just turned into Pythagoras in front of them. The right-angle functions are not a separate rule to learn; they are this triangle with one corner square. If a student asks which law to use for the rest of their life, the answer is in the first sentence of the readout.

Planes and Axes

The three planes

Sagittal — divides the body into left and right. Most of walking happens here, which is why most of this course is two-dimensional.

Frontal — divides front from back.

Transverse — divides top from bottom.

An axis is the straight line where two planes intersect — and it is also the line a body rotates about.

Those two sentences describe the same line, and that is not a coincidence. A joint that moves in one plane rotates about the axis perpendicular to it. Flexing your knee is movement in the sagittal plane about a left–right axis.

His slides 39 and 40. The plane/axis duality is the thing students find slippery, and the callout is the way through it: the axis a joint turns about is perpendicular to the plane the movement happens in. Say it with your own knee. Also flag why the sagittal plane gets all the attention: walking, running, jumping and lifting are all overwhelmingly sagittal, which is what makes two-dimensional analysis useful rather than lazy.

Planes and Axes

And the three axes they make

  • sagittal ∩ frontal = the z axis — distance along it is up and down;
  • frontal ∩ transverse = the y axis — left and right;
  • sagittal ∩ transverse = the x axis — front and back.

That is the convention every figure in this course uses, and it is the convention the motion-capture system in the lab writes its files in. When you open a data file in week ten, these are the columns.

Every number you will ever read off a marker, a force plate or an accelerometer is a distance or a force along one of these three lines.

His slides 41 to 46, which are six slides of one picture being annotated. Worth saying that this is not an arbitrary classroom convention: it is what comes out of the lab's own system, and the walking capture in the next figure is in exactly this frame. That is a small thing that makes the data feel less like homework.

What to take away

  • Biomechanics measures the outside of a body and computes the inside.
