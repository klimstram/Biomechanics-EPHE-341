Introduction

What is biomechanics?

The study of the effects of internal and external forces on the human body in movement and at rest.

Bio — any biological system.

Mechanics — the branch of the physical sciences concerned with the state of rest or motion of bodies subjected to the action of forces. Rigid body, fluid, deformable body.

The word to notice is internal. External forces can be measured — you stand on a plate and read the number. Internal ones cannot, and they are the ones that decide whether a tendon tears or a hip replacement lasts.

Almost everything this field knows about what the inside of a body is doing is calculated, not measured. The last three lectures of this course are how that calculation works.

Define it, then immediately say why it is not obvious. The internal/external split is the thing to plant on day one, because the entire arc of the course runs along it: measure the outside, compute the inside. Lectures 19 and 20 are where that gets cashed out, and it helps enormously if they have been waiting for it since week one.

Introduction

Qualitative and quantitative biomechanics

Qualitative — describing movement without numbers. A coach watching a stride, a clinician watching a gait. That is EPHE 201.

Quantitative — putting numbers on it. That is EPHE 341.

Neither is the better one. A clinician has fifteen minutes and needs a judgement; a researcher testing a new rehabilitation protocol needs a measurement. Each answers questions the other cannot.

What the quantitative approach buys is the ability to be wrong in a checkable way — which is the whole reason this course spends so much time on units and sanity checks.

His slide 5 and its speaker note. The note is good and worth reading almost verbatim: both approaches are valuable, with different ranges of application and limitations. Add the bit his slide does not say: the reason to put numbers on something is that numbers can be checked. A qualitative judgement that is wrong is hard to catch; a number that is wrong fails a units check or a sanity check.

Introduction

Statics and dynamics

Statics — the branch of physical science concerned with bodies, static or at rest, upon which balanced forces act.

Static equilibrium — we will be able to quantify forces and torques for objects in static equilibrium. Lecture 19.

Dynamics — the study of the relationship between motion and the forces affecting motion. It is the combined study of kinetics and kinematics. Lecture 20.

Constant velocity counts as statics. That surprises people, and it is the single most useful thing on this slide: the forces balance whenever the acceleration is zero, moving or not.

His slide 6, kept as its own slide because the pair is an axis the whole course runs along. The constant-velocity point is worth labouring. Students hear "statics" and picture something nailed down.

Introduction

What is kinematics?

The area of study that examines the spatial and temporal components of motion — position, velocity, acceleration — without regard to force.

Measures of movement.

Kinesis, from the Ancient Greek, is motion or movement; kineo is to move, to put in motion.

A video of a jump is kinematics. It tells you how high he went and how fast he was travelling when he left the floor, and nothing at all about why.

His slide 7. The example to use is the one they will meet in week seven: the same jump, filmed and on a force plate. The film is kinematics, the plate is kinetics, and you need both to say anything useful.

Introduction

What is kinetics?

The study of bodies in motion with respect to mass, force and energy.

Measures of the causes and effects of movement.

Kinematics describes; kinetics explains. Roughly half this course is each, and the two meet in the last three lectures, where measured motion and a measured force are put together to work out what the joints were doing.

His slide 8. The kinematics/kinetics distinction is the one they will get wrong on the midterm, so land it with an example rather than a definition.

How This Course Works

Basic problem solving in biomechanics

  1. Read the problem carefully to understand what is asked.
  2. Write down what is to be determined.
  3. When helpful, draw a diagram.
  4. Identify and write down helpful formulas related to the variable that is to be determined.
  5. Write down all the given information relevant to those formulas.
  6. Select the formula(s) that contain the given variables and the unknown variable. If a workable formula cannot be found, it may be necessary to return to step 1 for more analysis of the given data.
  7. If a workable formula can be found, write it out and substitute the known quantities.
  8. Use basic math (algebra) to solve for the unknown variable.
  9. Check to see if the answer makes sense.
  10. Box in the answer and check the units.
His slide 16, his wording, his numbering. Read as a list these sound like the kind of advice nobody follows. The next slide runs them once on a real problem, and two of them turn out to be doing all the work.

Basic Math

Properties of real numbers

Quite simply, real numbers are all the numbers that we will deal with in biomechanics, and exist on a continuum from −∞ to +∞.

We can add, subtract, multiply and divide these numbers.

  • Commutative properties
  • Associative properties
  • Distributive properties
His slide 20, as the heading for the three that follow.

Basic Math

Commutative properties

Changing the order of the operands does not change the end result. Said another way: in situations of strict addition or multiplication, number order is not important.

Addition

a + b = b + a

Multiplication

a × b = b × a

Notice what is not on the list: subtraction and division. a − b ≠ b − a, and that is the source of half the sign errors in the course.

His slide 21. The subtraction point is the one worth adding to his slide, and it costs ten seconds.

Basic Math

Associative properties

Within an expression containing two or more occurrences in a row of the same operator, the order in which the operations are performed does not matter as long as the sequence of the operands is not changed. Or whichever way the numbers are grouped to perform the operation, the answer will still be the same.

Addition

(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)

Multiplication

(a × b) × c = a × (b × c)

His slide 22, his wording.

Basic Math

Distributive property

If a number is multiplied by numbers in brackets, we need to multiply each number in the bracket by the number outside of the brackets.

a(b + c) = (a × b) + (a × c)

This is the one that breaks. Students multiply the first term in the bracket and forget the second, and it costs them in every force-resolution problem for the rest of the term.

His slide 23. Resist the urge to rush it.

Basic Math

Solving equations for one unknown

When you encounter an equation with a variable of unknown value, the following procedure can be used to solve for the unknown value.

  1. Isolate the variable by manipulating the equation. Rule: whatever you do to one side of the equation, you must do to the other side. Use the algebraic properties from the previous pages.
  2. Solve for the unknown value.

Example: (3x + 7)/11 = 2, find x

A. multiply both sides by 11   3x + 7 = 2(11)

B. subtract 7 from both sides   3x = 2(11) − 7

C. divide both sides by 3   x = (2(11) − 7)/3

x = (22 − 7)/3 = 15/3 = 5

His slide 28, his example, his three lettered steps.

Basic Math

Solving equations for multiple unknowns

When you have an equation with two or more variables of unknown value, you need a number of equations equal to the number of unknown values, each containing the variables.

  1. Isolate one variable by manipulating the equation.
  2. Substitute the term in place of the isolated variable into a second equation.
  3. Solve for the other unknown value.
  4. Back-substitute the value of the second variable into the first equation to solve for the first variable.

Example: 1) x + y = 3   2) 2x + y = 4, find x and y

1. isolate   x = 3 − y

2. substitute into 2)   2(3 − y) + y = 4

3. solve   6 − 2y + y = 4  →  y = 2

4. back-substitute   x + 2 = 3  →  x = 1

His slide 29, his example. This is the shape of every statics problem in lecture 19: three equations, three unknowns, and you never solve them simultaneously — you pick the one that has only one unknown in it and work from there.

Basic Math

Solving quadratic equations

When you have an equation in the form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a, b and c are real numbers, it is known as a quadratic equation, and once solved will yield two values for x.

This becomes clear if you graph a quadratic equation: the curve crosses the x-axis twice.

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a

His worked case is y(f) = −2x² + 5x − 2, which crosses the axis at x = ½ and x = 2.

Quadratics turn up the moment anything is thrown: a projectile is at a given height twice, once going up and once coming down, and the two roots are those two times. That is why you cannot take the positive root and go home.

His slide 30. The projectile connection is worth making now rather than in week eight. It explains why a quadratic has two roots in a way that "that is what the formula gives" never will: the ball really is at two metres twice. Note the concluding line on his slide reads "x = ½ and x = 1". The next slide is where that gets looked at.

Trigonometry

Basic geometric laws

  1. Two lines are perpendicular when they are positioned 90° to each other.
  2. When two lines are parallel they will never intersect.
  3. When two lines intersect the opposing angles are the same.
  4. When two lines intersect, the sum of the angles created by the intersection will equal 360°.
  5. When two lines intersect, the sum of the angles along one line created by the intersection will equal 180°.
His slide 32, laws 1 to 5, his numbering. All five are correct.

Trigonometry

Basic geometric laws, continued

  1. When a line intersects two parallel lines a Z pattern forms and the corresponding angles are shown below.
  2. The intersection of three non-parallel lines will make a triangle.
  3. The sum of the interior angles of a triangle will always equal 180°.

The Z rule and the 180° rule between them are how you find an angle you were never given — which is most of what free body diagrams ask you to do.

His slide 33, laws 6 to 8. Dwell on the Z. When a limb is at an angle and a force is vertical, the angle you need is almost never the one marked on the figure; it is a corresponding angle two steps away. Students who know the Z pattern find it in seconds and students who do not guess.

Trigonometry

Trigonometric functions of a non right angle triangle

The trigonometric functions for any triangle are as follows:

α + β + γ = 180°

Cosine law

a² = b² + c² − 2bc·cosα

b² = a² + c² − 2ac·cosβ

c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cosγ

Sine law

a/sinα = b/sinβ = c/sinγ

These functions are true for all triangles. They are best memorised and used at all times when attempting to solve for unknown values of a triangle.

Which one you use is decided by what you were given, not by what you want. Two angles and a side → the sine law. Two sides and the angle between them → the cosine law.

His slide 36, his wording. The callout is the thing to teach. Students learn three boxes and then freeze because they do not know which box to open. The rule is mechanical: look at what you were given, count sides and angles, and the law picks itself. The diagrams and description that follow are on McGinnis pp. 158–171.

His slide 39, including his Table 6.1 and his pointer to the text. Flag why the sagittal plane gets all the attention: walking, running, jumping and lifting are all overwhelmingly sagittal, which is what makes two-dimensional analysis useful rather than lazy.

Planes and Axes

Axis system

An axis is the straight line where two planes intersect. The distance an object is along a certain axis is an important reference position.

An axis is also a line about which an object may rotate.

Those two sentences describe the same line, and that is not a coincidence. A joint that moves in one plane rotates about the axis perpendicular to it.

Flexing your knee is movement in the sagittal plane about a left–right axis. Say it with your own knee.

His slide 40, both of his definitions. The plane/axis duality is the thing students find slippery, and the callout is the way through it: the axis a joint turns about is perpendicular to the plane the movement happens in.

Planes and Axes

The three axes, one at a time

  • The line of intersection of the sagittal and frontal planes makes the z-axis, or vertical axis. Distance along this axis is a measure of up and down.
  • The line of intersection of the frontal and transverse planes is the y-axis. Distance along it is a measure of left and right.
  • The line of intersection of the sagittal and transverse planes is the x-axis. Distance along it is a measure of front and back.

That is the convention every figure in this course uses, and it is the convention the motion-capture system in the lab writes its files in. When you open a data file in week ten, these are the columns.

Every number you will ever read off a marker, a force plate or an accelerometer is a distance or a force along one of these three lines.

Planes and Axes

Joint axes move with the limb

Joint axes will move when the limbs are in a different anatomical location.

It is important to have a basic understanding of anatomy to know how to determine the movement of body and limb with respect to joint axes and anatomical planes.

Imagining a wheel helps: the plane of the movement is the plane of the wheel, and the axis is the hub. Raise your arm out to the side and the elbow’s wheel has turned with it — the same joint, the same flexion, a different plane.

His slide 47, and the bicycle-wheel analogy his figure uses. Do it with your own arm in front of them. Flex the elbow with the arm hanging: sagittal. Abduct the shoulder to 90 degrees and flex the elbow again: the same joint action, now in the transverse plane. The axis went with the limb.

Planes and Axes

And most real movement is in none of them

While it is important to know the basic anatomical planes, when someone moves, movement may occur in a different plane about a different axis.

A golf swing is the standard example: the club travels in a plane that is neither sagittal nor frontal nor transverse, tilted somewhere between them, and the shoulders rotate about an axis perpendicular to that plane.

The three cardinal planes are a vocabulary, not a claim about what bodies do. Naming a diagonal movement means naming its components in the three planes you already have words for.

His slide 48. This is the slide that stops the planes feeling like a tidy lie. Nothing outside a textbook moves in one cardinal plane. What the planes buy is a way to decompose a movement that does not.

Planes and Axes

And the plane you watch from changes what you see

Viewing joint movement from different planes will give you a different perspective of the movement.

A shoulder abduction filmed from the front is a clean arc. The same abduction filmed from the side is a limb that barely moves. Neither camera is lying; each one is projecting a three-dimensional movement onto its own plane.

This is the reason every two-dimensional analysis in this course states which plane it is in — and the reason the last three lectures keep asking how much of the movement actually stayed in that plane.

His slide 49. Connect it forwards. In lecture 20 there is a measured jump where the ground force leaves the sagittal plane by a third of a degree, which is what makes a planar analysis honest for that movement. Say that the question gets asked properly later; this is where it gets raised.

Flexion and extension are the pair they already know; hyperextension is the one they will argue about, and it is worth saying that it means movement past the anatomical position rather than anything pathological. The ankle gets its own two words because it is the one joint where "flexion" is ambiguous, which is exactly why dorsiflexion and plantar flexion exist.

Planes and Axes

Frontal plane joint actions

Movement in the frontal plane, about an anteroposterior (front–back) axis:

  • abduction and adduction
  • ulnar and radial deviation
  • inversion and eversion
  • elevation and depression
  • upward and downward rotation
  • lateral flexion to the left and right
His slides 55 and 56. Abduction and adduction are the headline pair; the other four rows are the joints that have their own names for the same idea. Ulnar and radial deviation is the wrist, inversion and eversion the foot, elevation and depression the shoulder girdle.

Planes and Axes

Transverse plane joint actions

Movement in the transverse plane, about a longitudinal (up–down) axis:

  • internal and external rotation
  • pronation and supination
  • horizontal abduction and adduction
  • rotation to the right and to the left
His slides 57 and 58. Pronation and supination of the forearm is the one worth demonstrating, because the axis runs down the forearm itself rather than through a joint you can point at. Horizontal abduction and adduction is the odd one: it only exists once the shoulder is already at 90 degrees, which is the wheel from slide 47 having been turned.

What to take away

  • Biomechanics measures the outside of a body and computes the inside.
  • Kinematics describes motion; kinetics explains it. You need both.
  • Which trigonometric law you use is decided by what you were given.
  • An axis is where two planes meet, and it is the line a joint turns about.
  • The algebra is not the hard part of this course, but it is the part that costs marks. Fix it now.
  • Draw the answer. Both of the errors in this lecture would have been caught by one sketch.
