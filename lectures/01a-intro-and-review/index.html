Introduction

What is Biomechanics?

The study of the effects of internal and external forces on the human body in movement and rest.

Bio — any biological system.

Mechanics (rigid body, fluid, deformable body) — the branch of the physical sciences concerned with the state of rest or motion of bodies that are subjected to the action of forces.

Source: One stride, SUSU-30, Walking1-2 — SUSU youth motion dataset · UVic · CC BY 4.0

The wording is the lecture's; the stride is a real one, playing. One measured stride on a loop, and both halves of the definition are on it. The blue arrow and the top graph are the ground reaction the force plate actually recorded, drawn from the centre of pressure the plate actually reported. The red line and the bottom graph are the Achilles pull that must be there to hold that force at that distance from the ankle — computed on every frame from the force and the ankle position, and measured by nothing. The two graphs are on the same newton scale on purpose. Point at the dashed body-weight line on each. The ground reaction barely clears it, peaking at about one and a quarter body weights in ordinary walking. The Achilles runs to nearly three. The inside of the body carries far larger forces than the outside of it, which is most of the reason this subject exists. The Achilles curve sits at zero for the first part of stance. That is not an error: at heel strike the moment at the ankle is a dorsiflexor one, so the muscles on the front of the shin are doing that work, and a tendon can pull but cannot push. The 50 mm moment arm is a textbook figure, not a measurement. Say so if anyone asks; it is a good question.

Introduction

Qualitative and Quantitative Biomechanics?

Qualitative — describing movement without numbers. EPHE 201.

Quantitative — putting numbers on it. EPHE 341.

Neither is the better one. A clinician has fifteen minutes and needs a judgement; a researcher testing a protocol needs a measurement.

The same stride either way — the difference is whether you leave with a description or a number.

Source: OpenSim, full-body model

Source slide 5 and its speaker note: both approaches are valuable, with different ranges of application and limitations. The thing to add, which the source slide does not say: the reason to put numbers on something is that numbers can be checked. A qualitative judgement that is wrong is hard to catch; a number that is wrong fails a units check.

Introduction

Statics and dynamics

Statics — bodies upon which balanced forces act. We will quantify forces and torques for objects in static equilibrium. Lecture 19.   Dynamics — the relationship between motion and the forces affecting it; kinetics and kinematics together. Lecture 20.

Constant velocity counts as statics — what makes it statics is that the forces sum to zero, which they can do while the body is moving.

Source slide 6, kept as its own slide because the pair is an axis the whole course runs along. Say the law the right way round and keep saying it. Forces never cancel and never disappear; they add, as vectors, always. Statics is the case where that sum happens to be zero and dynamics is the case where it is not — and in the second case the sum is not merely "left over", it IS ma. The two panels differ in one thing only: the length of one arrow. The constant-velocity point is worth labouring. Students hear "statics" and picture something nailed down. Point at the left block and say it could be sliding across the floor at two metres a second and nothing on the diagram would change.

Introduction

What is Kinematics?

Area of study that examines the spatial and temporal components of motion (position, velocity, acceleration) without regard to force. Measures of movement. (Kinesis, from Ancient Greek, “motion, movement”; kineo, “to move, to put in motion”.)

Source: SUSU-46, Vertical Jump (Single) 1 — SUSU youth motion dataset · UVic · CC BY 4.0 · segment parameters: Winter (2009)

Source slide 7, its wording kept, and a measured jump rather than a drawing. SUSU-46, one vertical jump, eighty-three markers at ninety hertz. Press Play and let it run once before saying anything. Then stop it at the bottom of the dip and ask what the centre of mass is doing: it is momentarily still, and the acceleration is at its largest. That pair — zero velocity, maximum acceleration — is the one students will not believe until they see it on a real trace. Everything on this slide came from the markers. The centre of mass is computed from the segment positions with Winter's table, not from the force plate, which matters because the next slide is going to check one against the other. Say that the figure goes up and that nothing here says why. The acceleration trace reads about minus 9.7 through the whole flight phase, which is gravity, measured, by this subject, in this room.

Introduction

What is Kinetics?

The study of bodies in motion with respect to mass, force, and energy — measures of the causes and effects of movement.

Source: SUSU-46, Vertical Jump (Single) 1 — SUSU youth motion dataset · UVic · CC BY 4.0 · segment parameters: Winter (2009)

Source slide 8, its wording kept, and the same jump as the slide before — same subject, same trial, same clock. Flip back and forth between the two rather than reading two definitions. Same body, same instants; one slide has the motion and the other has what caused it. Kinematics describes, kinetics explains, and this is the distinction they get wrong on the midterm. The arrow comes out of the floor because that is where the measurement is made. Watch where it starts: the centre of pressure walks from the heel to the toe through the push, which is not something anyone guesses. The reason both traces are on this slide is that the second one is also on the slide before. The acceleration was computed from the markers; the force was measured by the plates; divide the net force by the body mass and you get the same curve. Over the push the two agree at r = 0.998. There is a second version of the same check that is worth doing out loud, because it is the impulse-momentum theorem and it arrives in lecture 7. Integrate the force above body weight from the start of the dip to take-off, divide by the body mass, and you get 2.31 metres per second. Differentiate the markers at take-off and you get 2.33. Two instruments that share nothing, agreeing to within a per cent about one event. That is the sentence the whole course is built on.

How This Course Works

Basic problem solving in biomechanics

  1. Read the problem carefully to understand what is asked.
  2. Write down what is to be determined.
  3. When helpful, draw a diagram.
  4. Identify and write down helpful formulas related to the variable that is to be determined.
  5. Write down all the given information relevant to those formulas.
  6. Select the formula(s) that contain the given variables and the unknown variable. If a workable formula cannot be found, it may be necessary to return to step 1 for more analysis of the given data.
  7. If a workable formula can be found, write it out and substitute the known quantities.
  8. Use basic math (algebra) to solve for the unknown variable.
  9. Check to see if the answer makes sense.
  10. Box in the answer and check the units.
Source slide 16, its wording, its numbering. Read as a list these sound like the kind of advice nobody follows. The next slide runs them once on a real problem, and two of them turn out to be doing all the work.

Basic Math

Properties of real numbers

Quite simply, real numbers are all the numbers that we will deal with in biomechanics, and exist on a continuum from −∞ to +∞.

We can add, subtract, multiply and divide these numbers.

  • Commutative properties
  • Associative properties
  • Distributive properties
Source slide 20, as the heading for the three that follow.

Basic Math

Commutative properties

Changing the order of the operands does not change the end result. Said another way: in situations of strict addition or multiplication, number order is not important.

Addition

a + b = b + a

Multiplication

a × b = b × a

Notice what is not on the list: subtraction and division. a − b ≠ b − a, and that is the source of half the sign errors in the course.

Source slide 21. The subtraction point is the one worth adding to the slide, and it costs ten seconds.

Basic Math

Associative properties

Within an expression containing two or more occurrences in a row of the same operator, the order in which the operations are performed does not matter as long as the sequence of the operands is not changed. Or whichever way the numbers are grouped to perform the operation, the answer will still be the same.

Addition

(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)

Multiplication

(a × b) × c = a × (b × c)

Source slide 22, its wording kept.

Basic Math

Distributive property

If a number is multiplied by numbers in brackets, we need to multiply each number in the bracket by the number outside of the brackets.

a(b + c) = (a × b) + (a × c)

This is the one that breaks. Students multiply the first term in the bracket and forget the second, and it costs them in every force-resolution problem for the rest of the term.

Source slide 23. Resist the urge to rush it.

Basic Math

Solving equations for one unknown

When you encounter an equation with a variable of unknown value, the following procedure can be used to solve for the unknown value.

  1. Isolate the variable by manipulating the equation. Rule: whatever you do to one side of the equation, you must do to the other side. Use the algebraic properties from the previous pages.
  2. Solve for the unknown value.

Example: (3x + 7)/11 = 2, find x

A. multiply both sides by 11   3x + 7 = 2(11)

B. subtract 7 from both sides   3x = 2(11) − 7

C. divide both sides by 3   x = (2(11) − 7)/3

x = (22 − 7)/3 = 15/3 = 5

Source slide 28, its example, its three lettered steps.

Basic Math

Solving equations for multiple unknowns

You need a number of equations equal to the number of unknown values, each containing the variables.

Example: 1) x + y = 3   2) 2x + y = 4, find x and y

1. isolate   x = 3 − y

2. substitute into 2)   2(3 − y) + y = 4

3. solve   6 − 2y + y = 4  →  y = 2

4. back-substitute   x + 2 = 3  →  x = 1

two lines, one crossing — that is what solving them together means

Source slide 29, its example. This is the shape of every statics problem in lecture 19: three equations, three unknowns, and you never solve them simultaneously — you pick the one that has only one unknown in it and work from there.

Basic Math

Solving quadratic equations

An equation of the form ax² + bx + c = 0 is a quadratic, and once solved yields two values for x.

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a

Quadratics turn up the moment anything is thrown: a projectile is at a given height twice, once going up and once coming down, and the two roots are those two times. That is why you cannot take the positive root and go home.

the worked case, drawn: it crosses at ½ and at 2

Source slide 30. The projectile connection is worth making now rather than in week eight. It explains why a quadratic has two roots in a way that "that is what the formula gives" never will: the ball really is at two metres twice. Note the concluding line on the slide reads "x = ½ and x = 1". The next slide is where that gets looked at.

Trigonometry

Trigonometry

Basic geometric laws

  1. Two lines are perpendicular when they are positioned 90° to each other.
  2. When two lines are parallel they will never intersect.
  3. When two lines intersect the opposing angles are the same.
  4. When two lines intersect, the sum of the angles created by the intersection will equal 360°.
  5. When two lines intersect, the sum of the angles along one line created by the intersection will equal 180°.
Source slide 32, laws 1 to 5, its numbering. All five are correct. Read them off the pictures rather than off the list. Numbers three, four and five are all the same crossing drawn three ways, and saying that out loud saves them memorising three rules.

Trigonometry

Basic geometric laws, continued

  1. When a line intersects two parallel lines a Z pattern forms and the corresponding angles are shown below.
  2. The intersection of three non-parallel lines will make a triangle.
  3. The sum of the interior angles of a triangle will always equal 180°.

The Z rule and the 180° rule between them are how you find an angle you were never given — which is most of what free body diagrams ask you to do.

Source slide 33, laws 6 to 8. Dwell on the Z. When a limb is at an angle and a force is vertical, the angle you need is almost never the one marked on the figure; it is a corresponding angle two steps away. Students who know the Z pattern find it in seconds and students who do not guess.

sinθ = opposite / hypotenuse

tanθ = opposite / adjacent

hypotenuse² = adjacent² + opposite²

Trigonometry

Trigonometric functions of a non right angle triangle

The trigonometric functions for any triangle are as follows:

α + β + γ = 180°

Cosine law

a² = b² + c² − 2bc·cosα

b² = a² + c² − 2ac·cosβ

c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cosγ

Sine law

a/sinα = b/sinβ = c/sinγ

Which law you use is decided by what you were given. Two angles and a side → the sine law. Two sides and the angle between them → the cosine law.

Source slide 36, its wording kept. The callout is the thing to teach. Students learn three boxes and then freeze because they do not know which box to open. The rule is mechanical: look at what you were given, count sides and angles, and the law picks itself. Anatomical planes, axis and position

Three planes, three axes, and a vocabulary for saying which way a limb went.

Divider. The point of this section is that "forward" is not a direction until you have said forward relative to what.

Planes and Axes

Planes and axis system

The planes and axis system will be necessary for describing movement in 2D and 3D. Sagittal divides left from right — most of walking happens here, which is why most of this course is two-dimensional. Frontal divides front from back. Transverse divides top from bottom.

Source: character rig: Mixamo (Adobe)

Each plane turns about the axis perpendicular to it. McGinnis pp. 158–171.

Source slide 39, including Table 6.1 and the pointer to the text. Flag why the sagittal plane gets all the attention: walking, running, jumping and lifting are all overwhelmingly sagittal, which is what makes two-dimensional analysis useful rather than lazy.

Planes and Axes

Axis system

An axis is the straight line where two planes intersect. The distance an object is along a certain axis is an important reference position.

An axis is also a line about which an object may rotate.

Those two sentences describe the same line, and that is not a coincidence. A joint that moves in one plane rotates about the axis perpendicular to it.

Flexing your knee is movement in the sagittal plane about a left–right axis. Say it with your own knee.

Source slide 40, both of its definitions. The plane/axis duality is the thing students find slippery, and the callout is the way through it: the axis a joint turns about is perpendicular to the plane the movement happens in.

Planes and Axes

The three axes, one at a time

  • The line of intersection of the sagittal and frontal planes makes the z-axis, or vertical axis. Distance along this axis is a measure of up and down.
  • The line of intersection of the frontal and transverse planes is the y-axis. Distance along it is a measure of left and right.
  • The line of intersection of the sagittal and transverse planes is the x-axis. Distance along it is a measure of front and back.

That is the convention every figure in this course uses, and it is the convention the motion-capture system in the lab writes its files in. When you open a data file in week ten, these are the columns.

Every number you will ever read off a marker, a force plate or an accelerometer is a distance or a force along one of these three lines.

Source slides 41 to 46, which are six slides of one picture being annotated one axis at a time. The figure on the next slide is that build-up, and you can do it live. Worth saying that this is not an arbitrary classroom convention: it is what comes out of the lab's own system, and the walking capture in the next figure is in exactly this frame.

Joint axes move with the limb

Joint axes will move when the limbs are in a different anatomical location. It is important to have a basic understanding of anatomy to know how to determine the movement of body and limb with respect to joint axes and anatomical planes.

Planes and Axes

And most real movement is in none of them

While it is important to know the basic anatomical planes, when someone moves, movement may occur in a different plane about a different axis. The three cardinal planes are a vocabulary, not a claim about what bodies do.

Source: character rig: Mixamo (Adobe)

Source slide 48. This is the slide that stops the planes feeling like a tidy lie. Nothing outside a textbook moves in one cardinal plane. What the planes buy is a way to decompose a movement that does not. The arm movement drawn here is the one a physiotherapist would call a diagonal pattern, and it was chosen so that the three shares come out equal: ninety degrees of flexion, ninety of adduction, ninety of internal rotation. There is no plane you can point at and call it the plane the movement is really in. Ask the room which one it is before you show the list.

And the plane you watch from changes what you see

Viewing joint movement from different planes will give you a different perspective of the movement. One shoulder, one 90° abduction, two cameras.

Source: character rig: Mixamo (Adobe)

Neither camera is lying; each is projecting a three-dimensional movement onto its own plane. The side camera reports a movement the shoulder cannot make. That is why every two-dimensional analysis in this course states which plane it is in.

Source slide 49. The side camera is the point, and it is worse than "you see less". It reports the hand travelling a hundred and eighty degrees straight up through the shoulder, which is a movement no shoulder makes, and the arm appears to shorten to just over a quarter of its length on the way. Both numbers are measured off the figures on the slide. Connect it forwards. In lecture 20 there is a measured jump where the ground force leaves the sagittal plane by a third of a degree, which is what makes a planar analysis honest for that movement. Say that the question gets asked properly later; this is where it gets raised.

Planes and Axes

Sagittal plane joint actions

Movement in the sagittal plane, about a transverse (left–right) axis — flexion, extension, hyperextension, and at the ankle dorsiflexion and plantar flexion.

At the wrist, elbow, shoulder, hip, knee, trunk, neck and ankle. Flexion closes the angle at a joint, extension opens it, and hyperextension carries it past the anatomical position.

Source: character rig: Mixamo (Adobe)

Source slides 50 to 54, which are five plates of the same list. Have them do each one as you name it. Flexion and extension are the pair they already know; hyperextension is the one they will argue about, and it is worth saying that it means movement past the anatomical position rather than anything pathological. The ankle gets its own two words because it is the one joint where "flexion" is ambiguous, which is exactly why dorsiflexion and plantar flexion exist.

Planes and Axes

Frontal plane joint actions

Movement in the frontal plane, about an anteroposterior (front–back) axis — abduction away from the midline and adduction back to it.

The same idea has its own name at several joints: ulnar and radial deviation at the wrist, inversion and eversion at the foot, elevation and depression and upward and downward rotation at the shoulder girdle, and lateral flexion of the trunk and neck.

Source: character rig: Mixamo (Adobe)

Source slides 55 and 56. Abduction and adduction are the headline pair; the other four rows are the joints that have their own names for the same idea. Ulnar and radial deviation is the wrist, inversion and eversion the foot, elevation and depression the shoulder girdle.

Planes and Axes

Transverse plane joint actions

Movement in the transverse plane, about a longitudinal (up–down) axis — everything that twists rather than swings.

Internal and external rotation at the shoulder and hip, pronation and supination of the forearm, horizontal abduction and adduction once the shoulder is already raised to 90°, and rotation of the trunk and neck to the right and left.

Source: character rig: Mixamo (Adobe)

Source slides 57 and 58. Pronation and supination of the forearm is the one worth demonstrating, because the axis runs down the forearm itself rather than through a joint you can point at. Horizontal abduction and adduction is the odd one: it only exists once the shoulder is already at 90 degrees, which is the wheel from slide 47 having been turned.

What to take away

  • Biomechanics measures the outside of a body and computes the inside.
  • Kinematics describes motion; kinetics explains it. You need both.
  • Which trigonometric law you use is decided by what you were given.
  • An axis is where two planes meet, and it is the line a joint turns about.
  • The algebra is not the hard part of this course, but it is the part that costs marks. Fix it now.
  • Draw the answer. Both of the errors in this lecture would have been caught by one sketch.
The six lines. If you say one of them twice, say the last. Both mistakes in fifty-nine slides — a quadratic's roots and a triangle's side — were visible in a drawing that was already on the slide. That is not a coincidence about this deck; it is how arithmetic errors behave.
