Linear Kinetics

Kinetics:

The concepts of mass, force, and energy as they affect motion.

Newton's 1st Law : Law of Inertia

An object at rest tends to stay at rest and an object in uniform motion tends to stay in uniform motion unless acted upon by a net external force.

  • uniform = unchanging = consistent
  • Net = total after all forces are added together

What is Inertia?

  • derived from the latin for "idleness" or "laziness"
  • resistance to a change in state of motion

Mass quantifies inertia. Inertia is not a separate property you measure on its own — the kilogram is the measure of it. Double the mass and you have doubled the resistance to any change in the state of motion.

More mass, more inertia — harder to start, harder to stop, harder to turn.

Static equilibrium

An object is at rest when the net forces acting on it sum to zero.

  • There is no motion
  • Gravity is acting downward
  • The ground contact force is acting up
  • The net vertical force is zero

ΣFz = 0    ΣFx = 0    ΣFy = 0

This individual is in static equilibrium. Is there such a thing as dynamic equilibrium?

Static equilibrium is not one equation, it is three: the forces have to sum to zero along every axis independently. Standing still, only two forces are in play — the weight the Earth pulls down with, and the ground contact force pushing up — and they are equal and opposite, so all three sums are zero at once.

Dynamic equilibrium

When is an object in uniform motion? This is Dynamic Equilibrium.

  • Uniform motion means constant velocity.
  • An object will maintain its velocity unless accelerated or decelerated.
  • Net forces are equal to zero but an object is in motion
The force diagram for a body gliding at constant velocity is identical to the one for a body standing still. That is the whole of the first law: nothing in the forces tells you which of the two you are looking at. Unbalance the drive against the resistance and the velocity trace stops being flat — that is the only thing a net force ever does.

Momentum

The momentum of an object can be thought of as the tendency for an object to resist change in motion and continue to move in its direction of travel.

Momentum

Momentum is related to inertia but not the same. Inertia is an object’s tendency to resist change in the state of motion (at motion and rest). However during motion an object has momentum.

That is, an object resists its change in state of motion and this resistance is related to its mass (inertia) and velocity.

P = mv

where P is the object’s linear momentum, m is the object’s mass and v is the object’s velocity. SI units: kg·m/s

Remember that velocity is a vector quantity, so momentum will have a magnitude and direction.

Momentum

Understanding Momentum is really important when dealing with things that collide.

  • Baseball
  • Soccer
  • Football players

The outcome of collisions during these sports can be explained by quantifying the momentum of the system.

Law of Conservation of Momentum

In the absence of external forces, the total momentum of a given system is conserved.

Pbefore = Pafter   or   (mv)before = (mv)after

The law of conservation of momentum helps us understand collisions — the sum of the momentum before the collision must equal the sum of the momentum after the collision.

Collisions range between elastic and inelastic collisions:

  • Elastic collisions — objects rebound off of each other
  • Inelastic collisions — objects stick together and usually become distorted and generate heat

Conserved means the total is the same number before and after — not that each object keeps what it had. The collision redistributes momentum; it does not create or destroy any.

mAvAi + mBvBi  =  mAvAf + mBvBf

Coefficient of restitution

Most collisions are neither perfectly elastic nor perfectly inelastic but somewhere in between.

The coefficient of restitution is a means of quantifying how elastic the collisions are.

Coefficient of restitution

The coefficient of restitution is the absolute value of the ratio of the velocity of separation to the velocity of approach.

The velocity of approach is the difference between the two velocities just before the collision — how fast they are closing.

The velocity of separation is the difference just after it — how fast they are moving apart.

Coefficient of restitution

e = velocity of separation / velocity of approach

= (VBf – VAf) / (VAi – VBi)

Where VBf = final velocity of object B, VAf = final velocity of object A, VAi = initial velocity of object A, VBi = initial velocity of object B.

  • The coefficient of restitution is a ratio and therefore has no units.
  • For perfectly elastic collision the COR is 1
  • For perfectly inelastic collisions the COR is 0

Most rules for ball sports directly or indirectly specify what the coefficient of restitution must be for the ball on the specific playing surface or implement.

e  =  vBf − vAf vAi − vBi

velocity of separation over velocity of approach

e = 1  perfectly elastic — they separate as fast as they approached
0 < e < 1  real collisions — some kinetic energy is lost
e = 0  perfectly inelastic — they do not separate at all

Coefficient of restitution

The coefficient of restitution is affected by the nature of both objects. For ball sports the coefficient of restitution can be figured out using a simple equation.

If a ball is dropped from a specific height onto a fixed surface, then the height of the rebound and the drop height can be used to determine the coefficient of restitution. This is a measure of the bounciness of the ball and the implement or surface it strikes will greatly affect the outcome.

e  =  √ bounce heightdrop height

Elastic collisions

An elastic collision occurs when the two objects collide and then bounce off one another.

A collision between two pool or snooker balls is a good example of an almost totally elastic collision. The objects collide and then seem to bounce off of each other and move separately.

In a perfectly elastic collision, in a straight line, each object transfers all of its momentum to the other object.

mAvA initial + mBvB initial = mAvA final + mBvB final

Equal masses, B at rest, e = 1: A stops dead and B leaves with everything A had. That is the extreme case of the sentence on the slide — each object transfers all of its momentum to the other — and it only comes out that cleanly when the masses are equal.

Elastic collisions

Equal masses, B at rest

Since m is the same we can take it out of the equation. Also assume v = 1 m/s and vB initial is zero.

mAvA initial + mBvB initial = mAvA final + mBvB final

(1 m/s) + 0 m/s = vA final + vB final

vA final = 1 − vB final

One equation, two unknowns. It cannot be solved yet — that is the whole reason the coefficient of restitution has to come into it on the next slide.

Elastic collisions

Equal masses, B at rest — solution

Also we know that the coefficient of restitution is 1.

e = (vBf – vAf) / (vAi – vBi)

1 = (vBf – (1 − vB final)) / ((1 m/s) – 0 m/s)

1 = −1 + 2vBf

VBf = 1 m/s

Sub VBf back into equation 1:   vA final = 1 – 1 = 0 m/s

A stops dead and B leaves with everything A had. Equal masses and a perfectly elastic collision is the one case where the transfer is total.

Elastic collisions

Equal masses, head on

Since m is the same we can take it out of the equation. Also assume v = 1 m/s, with B coming the other way at 0.5 m/s.

mAvA initial + mBvB initial = mAvA final + mBvB final

1 m/s − 0.5 m/s = vA final + vB final

vA final = 0.5 − vB final

Sign matters from here on. B is travelling the other way, so its velocity goes into the sum as a negative number, and the total momentum of the pair is 0.5 kg·m/s rather than 1.5.

Elastic collisions

Equal masses, head on — solution

Also we know that the coefficient of restitution is 1.

e = (vBf – vAf) / (vAi – vBi)

1 = (vBf – (0.5 − vB final)) / ((1 m/s) – (−0.5 m/s))

1 = (−0.5 + 2vBf) / 1.5

1.5 = −0.5 + 2vBf  →  VBf = 1 m/s

vA final = 0.5 – 1 = − 0.5 m/s

Equal masses and e = 1: they swap velocities exactly. A arrived at 1 and leaves at −0.5; B arrived at −0.5 and leaves at 1.

Elastic collisions

Different masses

Assume that m = 1 kg and v = 1 m/s. A is twice the mass of B and they are coming straight at each other.

mAvA initial + mBvB initial = mAvA final + mBvB final

(2 kg)(1 m/s) + (1 kg)(−1 m/s) = (2 kg)vA final + (1 kg)vB final

1 = 2vA final + vB final

vB final = 1 – 2vA final

The masses no longer cancel, so they stay in the equation and the coefficients on the two unknowns are different.

Elastic collisions

Different masses — solution

Also we know that the coefficient of restitution is 1.

e = (vBf – vAf) / (vAi – vBi)

1 = ((1 – 2vA final) − vAf) / ((1 m/s) – (−1 m/s))

1 = (1 – 3vAf) / 2

VAf = − 0.33 m/s

vB final = 1 – 2(−0.33 m/s) = 1.66 m/s

The heavy one is turned around and barely moves; the light one is thrown back well faster than it arrived. This method solves any straight-line collision where you know the masses, the approach velocities and the coefficient of restitution.

Inelastic collisions

An inelastic collision occurs when two objects collide and they behave as one object after the collision.

A common example of a perfectly inelastic collision is when two objects collide and then stick together afterwards. This equation describes the conservation of momentum for an inelastic collision:

m1v1 + m2v2 = (m1 + m2)vcombined

Momentum is still conserved. Kinetic energy is not — it has gone into deformation, sound and heat.

Inelastic collisions

Equal masses, B at rest

Assume that m = 1 kg and v = 1 m/s.

mAvA initial + mBvB initial = mAvA final + mBvB final

(1 kg)(1 m/s) + (1 kg)(0 m/s) = (1 kg)v1 final + (1 kg)v2 final

1 = vA final + vB final

vB final = 1 – vA final

The momentum equation is exactly the same one. Only the second equation changes.

Inelastic collisions

Equal masses, B at rest — solution

Also we know that the coefficient of restitution is 0.

e = (vBf – vAf) / (vAi – vBi)

0 = ((1 – vA final) − vAf) / ((1 m/s) – (0 m/s))

0 = 1 – 2vAf

VAf = 0.5 m/s

vB final = 1 – 0.5 m/s = 0.5 m/s

Both come out at the same velocity, which is what "behave as one object" means. e = 0 is precisely the statement that the velocity of separation is zero.

Collisions in two dimensions

The problem

For elastic collisions in two dimensions we need to break the momentum into components. Each ball has a mass of 0.1 kg and ball B was at rest. What is the speed and direction of ball B after the collision?

Po = Pf

Pox = Pfx  ,  Poy = Pfy

Momentum is a vector, so conserving it means conserving it in every direction at once. Nothing is conserved diagonally — x is conserved, and y is conserved, separately.

Collisions in two dimensions

Momentum in x

A leaves at 2 m/s, 30° above its original line. Write the x equation and the only unknown left in it is B’s x component.

Pox = Pfx

mAvAox + mBvBox = mAvAfx + mBvBfx

(0.1 kg)(5 m/s) + (0.1 kg)(0 m/s) = (0.1 kg)(2 m/s·cos30) + (0.1 kg)(VBfx)

0.5 + 0 = 0.17 + 0.1VBfx

VBfx = 3.3 m/s

The masses are equal here so they would cancel, but they are left in deliberately — the method does not depend on them being equal.

Collisions in two dimensions

Momentum in y

Now the same thing along y. Nothing was moving in y before the collision, so the whole of that side of the equation is zero.

Poy = Pfy

mAvAoy + mBvBoy = mAvAfy + mBvBfy

(0.1 kg)(0 m/s) + (0.1 kg)(0 m/s) = (0.1 kg)(2 m/s·sin30) + (0.1 kg)(VBfy)

0 = 0.1 + 0.1VBfy

VBfy = − 1 m/s

This is the step students find surprising: zero before means zero after, so whatever A takes upward B must take downward by exactly as much.

Collisions in two dimensions

Magnitude and direction

Two components make one vector. Pythagoras for the size of it, and the arctangent for which way it points.

Vbf2 = Vx2 + Vy2

Vbf2 = (3.3 m/s)2 + (−1 m/s)2

Vbf = 3.45 m/s

tanθ = Vy / Vx = −1 / 3.3

θ = tan−1(−0.303) = 16.85° below the x axis

Both numbers are needed: a velocity in two dimensions is not reported until you have said how fast and which way.

In the previous example we had an idea of the angle of the balls velocities before and after the collision. This allowed us to determine the magnitude of the velocity. However that is not always the case.

To determine the angle and magnitude of the balls velocities before and after the collision we need to set up a frame of reference and explore the conservation of momentum in axes normal (perpendicular) and tangent (parallel) to the contact surface interaction.

Normal and tangent axes

With our original x and y axis you can see:

  • the velocities of the balls before the collision
  • The axis normal (perpendicular) to the contacting surfaces
  • The axis tangent (parallel) to the contacting surfaces

Neither of those two axes lines up with x or y, which is exactly the problem.

Turning the frame of reference

Turn the whole frame — grid, axes, velocities and all — until the line joining the centres is horizontal.

Press Turn the frame and let it run once before saying anything. The point to make afterwards is that nothing about the collision changed while it turned — the balls are doing what they were always doing. What changed is the pair of axes we chose to describe them with, and that choice is ours to make.

Components in the new frame

Then determine the normal and tangent component vectors before the collision.

Each ball's velocity is now written as two numbers on the two useful axes. The tangential number is how fast it is sliding past the other ball; the normal number is how fast it is closing on it. Only the second one is a collision.

After the collision

The tangential components remain unchanged; conservation of linear momentum is maintained for the normal components.

Two smooth balls can only push each other along the line joining their centres, so along the tangent there is no force at all — and with no force, the first law says that component of the motion simply carries on. The collision lives entirely on the normal.

Transfer of momentum

Understanding Momentum is really important when dealing with the combined motion of the human body and techniques to transfer momentum from one limb to another.

The outcome of transferring momentum from one limb to another can be explained by the conservation of momentum of the system.

Transfer of momentum in sit to stand

The transfer of momentum during human movement can be discussed in reference to simple and complex tasks.

In a sit to stand task we use transfer of momentum to help us stand. By leaning forward our trunk has momentum which can be used to assist in hip and leg extension.

Transfer of momentum in the high jump

During a complex task such as high jump we use transfer of momentum to help us increase our vertical velocity.

The free limbs contribute by transferring their individual momentum to the velocity of the whole body:

  • Contralateral arm relative momentum
  • Ipsilateral arm relative momentum
  • Lead leg relative momentum
  • Combined free limb relative momentum

Free limb relative momentum

  • This shows that the RM of the arms are negative at touch-down as the arms are being driven downwards
  • the leg is flexing at the knee giving its CM a net upward velocity
  • The RM of the leg continues to increase as it is flexed and elevated
  • The RM of arms becomes positive as they reach the bottom of their swing and are elevated
  • The RM of each arm are similar and they reach their peak at approximately the same point in time but that of the lead leg reaches its peak earlier than the arms

Free limb relative momentum

The combined free limb RM (CFLRM) reaches a peak just after the lead leg but just before the arms. The relative momentum of all free limbs reduces at take-off, illustrating the transfer of each segment momentum to the rest of the body.

2nd Law: The Law of Acceleration

Newton's second law gives us the connection between force (F) and motion (a).

It provides a framework for thinking about how all of the forces (internal and external) can control motion.

We will use this law as a basis for our discussion of static and dynamic analysis including inverse dynamics.

3rd Law: The Law of Reaction

Law III

“Every applied force is accompanied by a reaction force, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.”

  • each force of the pair (the action force and the reaction force) is applied to a different body
  • for example, when a jumper generates a force against the ground, the ground generates an equal and opposite force against the jumper, causing the jumper to accelerate upward
