Biomechanics (EPHE 341)

Angular Kinetics 3

Newton’s three laws, rewritten for things that turn — and the one quantity a person can change at will.

Three lectures ago a force made a moment about a joint. Two lectures ago we found the point a free body turns about. This lecture asks the next question: once it is turning, how hard is it to stop, and what can the person do about it? The answer is one quantity, the moment of inertia, and the thing worth getting across today is that it is the only term in all of rotational mechanics that a human being can change on purpose, in the middle of a movement, in a fraction of a second. Mass you cannot change. Limb length you cannot change. Where you hold your limbs, you can — and that is the whole of diving, gymnastics, figure skating and the swing phase of running. Readings: McGinnis Chapter 7.

Angular Kinetics 3

The same three laws, said again for rotation

Every law from linear kinetics has an angular twin. The sentences are the same sentences with the nouns swapped.

LinearAngular
what resistsmass mmoment of inertia I
what actsforce Ftorque M
1st lawconstant v unless a force acts constant ω unless a torque acts
2nd lawF = ma M = Iα
momentump = mv L = Iω
impulseFt = Δp Mt = ΔL

One row is not a swap. Mass is a number; moment of inertia is a number per axis, and it changes when you move. That one difference is what the rest of this lecture is about.

Put the table up and read down the first column, then the second. The point of showing them side by side is that there is nothing new to learn here conceptually — if you understood F = ma you already understand M = Iα, provided you understand I. Then stop on the callout, because it is the honest warning. Mass is a property of an object. Moment of inertia is a property of an object AND an axis AND a configuration. The same skater has five different moments of inertia depending on which way she is turning and what she is doing with her arms. That is not a complication to be tidied away; it is the mechanism by which every one of these athletes does what they do.

Angular Kinetics 3

Newton’s first law, for turning

Linear. A body maintains a constant state of rest or constant velocity unless acted upon by an external force.

Angular. A body maintains a constant state of angular motion unless acted upon by an external torque.

Both halves matter, and the second half is the one people forget. “At rest” is a constant state of angular motion. So is spinning at eleven revolutions a second. Neither changes by itself.

In linear kinetics, inertia is quantified by mass — the resistance to a change in the state of motion.

Angular inertia is the property of an object to resist changes in its angular motion. It depends on two things:

  • the mass of the object
  • the distribution of that mass relative to the axis of rotation

The second one is new. Nothing in linear kinetics cared where the mass was. Everything in angular kinetics does.

Spend the time on "at rest and in motion". The law is usually taught as a statement about things that are still, and then the interesting half gets lost. A diver leaving the board spinning at four radians a second will still be spinning at four radians a second a full second later unless something touches her. Nothing does. That is the first law and it is also the entire reason a dive has to be set up before the feet leave the board. The two-item list at the end is the hinge of the lecture. Write it on the board if you write nothing else.

Angular Kinetics 3

Moment of inertia: I = Σ m r2

Here is the definition and here is what it does. Imagine the object as a crowd of particles. Each one resists being moved, and each one is being asked to travel on a circle whose radius is its own distance from the axis. A particle twice as far out has to be moved twice as fast for the same angular speed, and the work of getting it there goes as the square. So each particle contributes m times r squared, and the object's total resistance is the sum. Drag the masses and watch the two terms. His slide 7 is the first preset: ten kilograms at a quarter metre and ten at three quarters gives six and a quarter kilogram metres squared. His slide 8 asks what happens if you move one further out, so move it: at one metre the answer is ten point six two five. The mass did not change. Nothing changed but a distance, and the resistance to turning went up by seventy per cent. Then press the third preset, which doubles a mass instead of moving it. Doubling the far mass adds five point six. Moving it from point seven five to one metre adds four point four. You can feel the squaring in that comparison.

Angular Kinetics 3

What the squaring means

I = Σ m r2

= m1r12 + m2r22 + m3r32 + …

Mass appears once. Distance appears twice. So:

  • double a mass and its contribution doubles
  • double its distance and its contribution quadruples

Which is why his slide says, in one line, that the distribution of mass has a greater influence on angular inertia than mass.

The units follow from the definition and are worth saying out loud once: kilograms times metres squared, kg·m2. Not kilograms. Not newtons. A moment of inertia is not a force and not a mass.

A useful habit: whenever you meet a new rotational quantity, check its units against the linear one it replaces.

m → kg becomes I → kg·m2; p → kg·m/s becomes L → kg·m2/s; Ft → N·s becomes Mt → N·m·s. The extra metre is the moment arm, every time.

This slide exists so that nobody writes kilograms after a moment of inertia on the midterm. The habit in the callout is worth teaching properly. Every angular quantity is its linear twin with one extra factor of length — because every angular quantity is a linear quantity multiplied by a moment arm. If a student can reconstruct the units they can reconstruct the equation.

Angular Kinetics 3

The radius of gyration

Keeping track of every particle is not practical, so here is the shorthand. Ask a different question: if I took the whole mass of this object and squeezed it into a single lump, how far from the axis would the lump have to sit to be exactly as hard to turn? That distance is the radius of gyration, k, and by definition I equals m k squared. For his two masses about the end: six point two five divided by twenty is point three one two five, and the square root is zero point five five nine metres. That is his slide 11. Notice it is not the average of a quarter and three quarters, which would be a half. It is pulled toward the far mass, because the far mass contributes more — the squaring again. Switch the axis to the centre of gravity and the whole thing shrinks: k becomes exactly a quarter metre, and I becomes one point two five. The object did not change. We moved the axis, and the next slide is about why that always helps.

Why the centre of gravity

Of all the axes an object could turn about, one of them is special — and it is special for a reason you can compute.

A one-line divider. The previous lecture established that a free body rotates about its centre of gravity. This one explains why, and the explanation is a sentence about moments of inertia rather than a rule to remember.

Angular Kinetics 3

Anything thrown turns about its own centre of gravity

We established last lecture that an object in the air and free to rotate turns about its centre of gravity. Now we can add the second half:

  • its resistance to turning is its moment of inertia
  • which depends on its mass and on the distribution of that mass

A bat and a racquet are nearly the same length and nothing like the same object to swing, because one has most of its mass near the far end and the other has a strung hoop and a light shaft.

Thrown, both of them tumble about the marked point and nothing else — whatever shape they are, and however you throw them.

A short bridge slide. The marked dot is the centre of gravity of each implement, and it is nowhere near the middle of either. Worth asking: which is harder to swing, and about what? About the hands, the bat, comfortably — its mass is out at the end. About its own centre of gravity, much less so. Same object, two axes, two very different answers, which is the slide after next.

Angular Kinetics 3

Example problem — about the centre of gravity

Same object: a 10 kg mass 0.25 m from point A, and a 10 kg mass 0.75 m from A. This time, find I about its centre of gravity.

First, locate the centre of gravity — last lecture’s sum:

CofG = Σ(m · r) / Σm

= [ (10)(0.25) + (10)(0.75) ] / (10 + 10)

= 10.0 / 20 = 0.50 m from A

Then the moment of inertia about that point, with both masses now 0.25 m away:

I = (10)(0.25)2 + (10)(0.25)2

= 0.625 + 0.625 = 1.25 kg·m2

And the radius of gyration about the centre of gravity:

k = √(I/m) = √(1.25 / 20) = 0.25 m

About A it was 6.25. About the centre of gravity it is 1.25 — a fifth of the resistance, from the same object. Nothing moved but the axis.

Work it on the board at the pace of the slide. Two steps, and the first is last lecture's sum, which is the point: this lecture is built on top of that one, not beside it. The number to dwell on is the comparison in the callout. A fivefold difference, from choosing a different line to turn about. That is a large effect for something students often treat as bookkeeping, and it is the reason the next slide matters.

Angular Kinetics 3

So why does a free object turn about its centre of gravity?

His slide says: calculating the moment of inertia about different points shows us that the smallest moment of inertia occurs at the centre of gravity. So let us calculate it about every point and look. Slide the axis along the bar. The moment of inertia traces a parabola, and the bottom of the parabola is at the centre of gravity. That is not a feature of these two masses — switch to the measured body and sweep the axis from the floor to the top of the head, and the same parabola appears with its minimum at this person's centre of gravity. The dashed green line is the reason. Move the axis a distance d away from the centre of gravity and the moment of inertia becomes I at the centre of gravity, plus m d squared. That is the parallel-axis theorem, and it is exact — the two lines sit on top of each other, not near each other. Because m and d squared are both positive, you can never do better than d equals zero. The centre of gravity is the cheapest axis there is, for any object, always. Which is why, with nothing to hold it anywhere else, that is where a free body turns.

Angular Kinetics 3

The parallel-axis theorem

Iaxis = ICofG + m d2

where d is the perpendicular distance from the centre of gravity to the axis.

It does three jobs at once:

  • it explains the parabola on the last slide
  • it says the minimum is always at d = 0
  • it is how a table of segment data covers every axis you might need

Check it on the worked example: 1.25 + (20)(0.50)2 = 1.25 + 5.00 = 6.25, which is I about A.

Winter’s table gives k about the segment’s own centre of gravity. Everything else — about the hip, about the whole body — is that number plus m d2.

This is the most useful single equation in the lecture, and students usually meet it as a formula to memorise. Give them the picture instead: moving the axis away from the centre of gravity is like adding a point mass m at the distance you moved it. The object's own internal arrangement contributes I at the centre of gravity; the offset contributes m d squared; they add. Do the check out loud. One point two five plus five is six point two five, which is exactly what we got the long way on the first example. Two routes, one answer — and the short route is the one that works when the object is a person with sixteen segments.

Angular Kinetics 3

Moment of inertia about different axes

An object can turn about many axes, so it has a moment of inertia for each one — because the mass is spread out differently with respect to each.

For a standing person the three that matter are:

  • mediolateral — through the hips, side to side. A somersault turns about this one.
  • anteroposterior — front to back. A cartwheel.
  • longitudinal — head to toe. A twist.

They are not close to each other. For a standing adult the twist axis is roughly ten times easier to turn about than the other two — which is why a twist is the cheapest rotation in the air and a somersault the most expensive.

Name the three axes with the three movements, because the names on their own do not stick and the movements do. Somersault, cartwheel, twist. The ratio in the callout is worth stating before the next slide proves it, so that they have a prediction to check. Ten to one is a big number and students will not believe it until they see the computation.

Angular Kinetics 3

Manipulating the moment of inertia of the human body

Humans and animals change the orientation of their limbs constantly, and every change redistributes mass about every axis. The moment of inertia changes with it — not as a side effect, but as the thing being controlled.

Three examples, all on the next few slides:

  • a sprinter folds the knee so the leg is cheaper to swing forward
  • a diver tucks so the same angular momentum buys three somersaults instead of one
  • a cat, with no angular momentum at all, still turns itself over

Only one quantity in M = Iα is under voluntary control, and it is I. That is why so much technique is really about limb position.

The cat starts with zero angular momentum and lands feet-down with zero angular momentum. It never breaks the first law — it changes its moment of inertia about one axis while turning about another, twice, in opposite senses.

The cat is worth ninety seconds because it is the case students think must break the rule. It does not. Angular momentum is zero before the drop and zero at landing. What the cat does is bend in the middle, tuck the front legs in and extend the back ones, rotate the front half one way through a large angle while the back half rotates the other way through a small one — because the back half, with its legs out, has the larger moment of inertia. Then it swaps: front legs out, back legs in, and rotates the back half round. Net rotation of the whole animal, net angular momentum still zero. Divers do the same thing to add a twist to a somersault without having twisted on the board.

A sprinter reduces the moment of inertia about the hip by flexing the knee

His slide makes this claim and gives no number, so here is the number. A real running trial. At every frame the figure computes the moment of inertia of the whole swinging leg about that leg's own hip, two ways: as he actually ran, and with the knee straightened — the shank and foot swung out to lie along the thigh, with the hip and thigh left exactly where they are. That second one is the comparison the slide is really making, and it is drawn as the dashed ghost. Press run. The knee works between a hundred and one and a hundred and fifty-nine degrees across the stride, and at the tightest point of swing the real leg is twenty-six per cent easier to swing than the straight-leg version of itself. The thigh has not moved. The mass has not changed. The foot is simply closer to the hip, and r is squared. The second half of the point: this costs nothing. During swing the foot has nowhere to be. The leg only has to be long again at the instant it lands, and it is. Ask them where else this shows up. Answer: everywhere. A sprinter's heel comes almost to the buttock; a distance runner's barely leaves the ground, because they are not trying to turn the leg over nearly as fast and the extra knee flexion costs muscle work.

Angular Kinetics 3

Angular momentum

In linear kinetics, an object in motion has momentum — inertia in motion:

p = m v

p = m (inertia) · v (in motion)

Angular momentum is the tendency of an object to remain in angular motion. It is determined by the moment of inertia and the angular velocity:

L = I ω

L = I (angular inertia) · ω (in angular motion)

Units: kg·m2/s.

The first law, angular. A body maintains a constant state of angular motion unless acted on by an external torque.

So in the air, with gravity the only force acting, and gravity acting at the centre of gravity:

Linitial = Lfinal

Iiωi = Ifωf

— the conservation of angular momentum, and the reason a diver can decide in the air how many somersaults to do, but not whether to do any.

The step that matters is the one in the middle of the right column, and it is usually skipped: WHY is there no torque in the air? Because the only force is gravity, and gravity acts at the centre of gravity. A force through the axis has no moment arm about that axis. d perpendicular is zero, so M is zero, so L cannot change. That is the whole argument, it is two lines, and students who have it can derive the conservation law instead of memorising it. The last sentence of the right column is the one to leave them with. In the air you control I. You do not control L. Everything a diver does between the board and the water is spending a budget that was fixed before her feet left.

Is that actually true? A measured flight phase

The claim on the last slide rests on a premise — in the air, nothing but gravity — and that premise is measurable, so let us measure it rather than assert it again. This is the same vertical jump we used in Linear Kinetics II and in Work, Energy and Power. The body is built from markers alone: fourteen segments, Winter's table, no force plate. Differentiate the centre of mass twice and look at the shaded band, which is where the plate reads zero. Minus nine point seven one, plus or minus nought point four three metres per second squared, against gravity's minus nine point eight one. One per cent. The plate was not used to compute that curve — this is the markers independently confirming that for four hundred and fifty-one milliseconds nothing is touching him but the earth. So there is no torque about his centre of mass, so his angular momentum is frozen. Now press the second tab, and here is the part the textbook diagrams never show. His angular momentum in flight is nought point six kilogram metres squared per second, against four point four during the push. He jumped straight up. He bought almost no angular momentum, and conservation is a two-edged rule: it means he cannot lose it, and it also means he cannot get any. However hard he tucks, nought point six will not make a somersault. At the moment of inertia of a tight tuck, nought point six kilogram metres squared per second is two hundredths of a revolution a second — over the four hundred and fifty milliseconds he is in the air, that is about four degrees. Not a somersault. Four degrees. That is the honest version of conservation, and it is a better answer to "why can't I do a flip" than any amount of talk about core strength.

Example problem — the figure skater

If a figure skater has an angular velocity of 12 rad/s with her arms extended (I = 0.36 kg·m2), what does her angular velocity change to when she brings her arms in (I = 0.04 kg·m2)?

Write down the proper equation

Linitial = Lfinal

Iiωi = Ifωf

Write down given and unknown values

Ii = 0.36 kg·m2  ωi = 12 rad/s

If = 0.04 kg·m2  ωf = ?

Substitute and solve

(0.36)(12) = (0.04)ωf

ωf = 108 rad/s

Check the answer against the world. 108 rad/s is 17.2 revolutions a second. The fastest scratch spins on film are about 5.

The algebra is right. The inputs are not: 0.36 → 0.04 is a 9 : 1 change in moment of inertia, and a person cannot do that. Measured skaters manage roughly 3.5 : 1 — which, from a realistic 9 rad/s start, lands near the 5 rev/s you can see.

Converting an answer into something you can picture is not an optional last step. It is how you find out whether you believe it.

rad/s → rev/s: divide by 2π ≈ 6.28. Worth doing every single time an angular velocity comes out of a problem.

Work the example straight, then stop at the callout and do the conversion on the board. Divide by two pi. Seventeen revolutions a second. Ask them whether they have ever seen that. Nobody has. Then ask where the error is, and let them find that it is not in any line of the algebra — every line is correct — it is in the two numbers the question starts with. This is the most transferable thing in the lecture. Dimensional sanity is cheap and it catches a large fraction of real mistakes. Tell them it is worth marks on the exam to write "which is 17 rev/s, so one of the given values must be unrealistic" under a correct calculation.

Angular Kinetics 3

Example problem — the diver, with the radius of gyration

A 60 kg diver leaves the board with a radius of gyration of 0.5 m and an angular velocity of 4 rad/s. What is his angular velocity in a tuck, with a radius of gyration of 0.25 m?

Equations

Iiωi = Ifωf    I = m k2

Moments of inertia in the two positions

I1 = m k12 = (60)(0.5)2 = 15 kg·m2

I2 = m k22 = (60)(0.25)2 = 3.75 kg·m2

Then conservation

(15)(4) = (3.75)ωf

ωf = 16 rad/s

Halve k and you quarter I, because k is squared — so the angular velocity goes up by four, not two.

16 rad/s is 2.5 rev/s, which is what a tucked diver actually does. This one survives the sanity check.

Note how this problem is built: it does not hand you the moments of inertia, it hands you radii of gyration and makes you build them. That is the realistic version, because a radius of gyration is what a table gives you and a moment of inertia is not. The callout is the arithmetic point: halving k quarters I, so omega quadruples. Students very often expect doubling. And do the sanity conversion again, because the answer passes this time. Sixteen radians a second is two and a half revolutions a second, which over a one-and-a-half-second dive is nearly four somersaults — about right for a tucked three-and-a-half. Two problems, same check, one passes and one fails. That contrast is the lesson.

Angular Kinetics 3

Newton’s second law: the law of acceleration

Linear. A force applied to a body causes an acceleration of that body, of a magnitude proportional to the force, in the direction of the force, and inversely proportional to the body’s mass.

F = m a

Angular. A torque applied to a body causes an angular acceleration of that body, of a magnitude proportional to the torque, in the direction of the torque, and inversely proportional to the body’s moment of inertia.

τ = F d⊥    M = I α

The greater the inertia of an object, the more force — or torque — is needed to make it accelerate.

If a net external torque acts at a distance from the axis of rotation, the object accelerates angularly in the direction of that torque, in proportion to it, and in inverse proportion to its moment of inertia.

Two ways to get more angular acceleration out of the same muscle: push harder, or push further from the axis. The second is usually cheaper, and it is most of what equipment design is for.

Read the two sentences in parallel. They are the same sentence. If a student can state the linear one they can produce the angular one by substituting three nouns, and that is worth demonstrating out loud rather than asserting. The callout is the practical consequence and it links back to the torque lecture. A longer wrench, a longer oar, a longer club: all of them are buying d perpendicular rather than F.

Angular Kinetics 3

Angular impulse: a torque applied for a certain amount of time

Multiply both sides of M equals I alpha by time, and alpha times t is the change in omega, so M t is the change in I omega — the change in angular momentum. That is the angular impulse–momentum relationship, and it is the same sentence as force, time and momentum from two lectures ago. His example is loaded on the chip: a diver exerting twenty newton metres for one point four seconds with a moment of inertia of nine. Twenty times one point four is twenty-eight newton metre seconds of angular impulse, divided by nine is three point one one radians per second, which is his slide 35. Move the sliders and watch the rectangle. Its area is the angular impulse, and you can buy the same area with a big torque briefly or a small one for a long time — which is exactly the trade-off a diver makes on a springboard, or a gymnast makes on a vault. Then say the quiet part. Three point one one radians per second is half a revolution a second. Twenty newton metres is not a small torque and one point four seconds is a long time on a board, and that is all it buys. Everything she does in the air is spending that, and the tuck is how she spends it well.

Angular Kinetics 3

The angular impulse–momentum relationship

A change in the angular momentum of a system depends on:

  1. the magnitude of the external torques, and
  2. the time over which those torques act.

Therefore an angular impulse causes a change in angular momentum:

M t = (Iω)f − (Iω)i

M t = ΔL

SI units: N·m·s. Angular impulse is a vector — it has a sense, and torques in opposite senses subtract.

Each line is one substitution. Read it downward and it is a derivation, not a list to memorise.

Walk the chain on the right line by line, because it is genuinely a derivation and students usually treat it as four unrelated formulas. J equals M t, by definition. M is I alpha, so J is I alpha t. Alpha t is the change in omega, so J is I omega. And I omega is L. Four lines, one idea. The vector note matters for the next slide and for the final: in a real movement several torques act at once and they can cancel. A gymnast on a beam is applying torques constantly and her angular momentum is roughly zero, because they cancel.

Angular Kinetics 3

Example problem — the Olympic diver

An Olympic diver exerts a torque of 20 N·m with his body in full extension (I = 9 kg·m2) for 1.4 s before leaving the platform. What is his angular velocity at the moment he leaves?

Given

M = 20 N·m t = 1.4 s  I = 9 kg·m2

Solution

M t = (Iω)f − (Iω)i

(20)(1.4) = (9)ω − (9)(0)

28 = 9ω

ω = 3.11 rad/s, in the direction the torque was applied

The published figure, and the whole lecture in one picture: through the dive L is a flat line while I and ω cross over each other.

Work the problem, then spend the remaining time on the figure, because it is the best summary of the lecture that exists and it is worth reading properly. Three curves against frame number. The flat one is angular momentum — it was set on the platform and it does not move. The one that falls and comes back is the moment of inertia, as he tucks and opens. The one that rises and falls is the angular velocity. They are mirror images because their product has to stay constant. Point at the crossing. That is the tuck. Everything a diver is trained to do happens in that window, and all of it is managing I so that omega integrates to the right number of somersaults by the time he reaches the water. And note that the figure ends where he enters: at the moment the water touches him, an external torque exists again, and L stops being conserved.

Angular Kinetics 3

What to take away

  • I = Σ m r2 — mass counts once, distance counts twice. Distribution beats mass.
  • I = m k2 — the radius of gyration is the distance at which all the mass would give the same I, and it is how segment tables are written.
  • Iaxis = ICofG + m d2 — which is why the centre of gravity is always the cheapest axis, and why a free body turns about it.
  • One I per axis per posture. For one person, 1.0 to 15.0 kg·m2.
  • L = Iω, and in the air L is frozen — gravity acts at the centre of gravity, so it has no moment arm about it. Measured: −9.71 m/s2 and no torque.
  • M = Iα, and Mt = ΔL — angular momentum is bought on the ground and spent in the air.
  • Check your answers against the world. 108 rad/s is 17 rev/s, and nobody spins that fast.

The through-line: of everything in M = Iα and L = Iω, the only term a person can change mid-movement is I. That is why technique looks the way it does.

Close on the callout. If they remember one sentence from this lecture, it should be that one — because it converts a page of equations into a way of looking at any sporting movement.
