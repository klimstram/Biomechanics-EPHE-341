Nyquist–Shannon Sampling Theorem

In sampling an analog signal, the goal is to represent the original signal as accurately as possible with as few discrete points as possible. How high must the sampling frequency be for an analog signal to be accurately represented by discrete data points?

Due to limitations in computer technology, it is best to sample as few times as possible, while still maintaining the fidelity of the original signal. The original slides step through 4, 5, 7, 10, 15 and 20 Hz — the chips do the same thing, and the slider fills in everything between. Note: there is more than one set of sine waves that could have fit the samples shown here. That is what the dashed line is.

Nyquist–Shannon Sampling Theorem

For a digital signal to represent an analog signal perfectly, the sampling frequency must be at least twice the analog signal’s highest frequency.

A sampling frequency of twice the highest frequency in the analog signal is called the Nyquist Frequency. Walk the slider up from 4 Hz: below 20 Hz there is always another sine wave that fits the samples, and the dashed line is it. At 20 Hz and above there is not.

Nyquist–Shannon Sampling Theorem

To ensure that sharp direction changes in the signal are not missed, sampling frequencies of 5–10 times the Nyquist Frequency are often used.

Sampling higher than the Nyquist Frequency does not lead to better representation of a pure sine wave, and it can slow computer processing time. Real signals are not pure sine waves — the peaks and the turns are where extra samples earn their keep. Turn "join the samples" off and on at 20 Hz and then at 100 Hz: the corners are the difference.

Filtering, smoothing and fitting can improve S/N using different approaches.

Filtering:

involves altering or removing certain frequency components from a signal.

Smoothing:

involves averaging or aggregating data points to reduce short-term fluctuations and highlight longer-term trends or cycles.

Curve Fitting:

to find a mathematical function that best represents a dataset. This function may be a simple linear equation, a complex polynomial, or any other mathematical form that closely matches the pattern of the data.

Filtering removes unwanted components from a signal or dataset, such as noise or interference, and can be applied in real time to an ongoing data stream. Low-pass filters remove high-frequency components; high-pass filters remove low-frequency components; band-pass filters allow a certain range; band-stop filters block a specific range. Smoothing reduces short-term fluctuations to reveal the underlying trend; it is usually applied to a record you already have in full.

Smoothing

Moving average (over a window of samples). Simple averaging gets rid of noisy spikes.

Frequency domain

A time-domain signal is converted to a frequency-domain signal with a mathematical technique called a Fourier Transform.

The chips are the lecture's own three examples: a pure 1 Hz wave, a pure 5 Hz wave, and 2 Hz + 30 Hz. Since the first two are pure sine waves, the frequency-domain graph clearly shows that the whole signal is one frequency. Then turn the noise up. Noise has no single frequency, so it lifts the entire spectrum rather than making a spike — which is exactly what makes it separable.

Fourier transform

Any repeating signal, however square-edged, is a sum of sine and cosine waves at whole multiples of its own rate — 1 Hz, 2 Hz, 3 Hz and so on for a signal that repeats once a second. The Fourier transform is the arithmetic that finds how much of each, and those amplitudes are exactly what the frequency-domain graph plots. The three panels are the same numbers three ways. Start with one harmonic and walk the slider up, or press Build up. The dashed line is the real signal; the solid line is everything the terms kept so far can rebuild. The residual RMS is the gap between them. Walking GRF: six to eight harmonics carry almost all of it, which is where the usual 6 Hz low-pass cutoff for walking comes from. Switch to Running GRF and the same slider position no longer reaches — the impact is a shorter event, so its energy sits at higher frequencies, and a cutoff tuned for walking would shave the peak off. That is the whole argument for choosing a cutoff from the signal rather than from habit. Square wave is the animation this slide used to show: odd harmonics only, amplitudes falling as 1/k, and a corner that keeps ringing however many terms you add. 'sin & cos parts' splits each harmonic into the two waves that make it. Sines alone cannot build a signal that is not odd-symmetric about its own mean, which is why a real GRF needs the cosine terms and the offset as well.

Digital filtering

Is a way of reducing the frequency content of the signal so that it just obtains the signal you are interested in without the noise.

A common use of Fourier transforms is to find the frequency components of a time-domain signal that is mixed with noise.

Because noise is often of a different frequency than the intended signal, a filter can be used to remove the noise (ie: parts of the signal that are a certain frequency).

Digital filtering

Types:

low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, band stop and notch (single or small band-stop filter, useful for AC interference)

Designs:

Butterworth, critically-damped, Chebyshev etc. (sharper cutoffs), Generalized Cross-validation (GCV also called Woltring filter, 1986)

Problems:

Phase distortion (usually phase-lags occur)

The phase lag is why biomechanics filters forwards and then backwards — running the same filter twice in opposite directions cancels the delay exactly. The figure on the next slide does that.
