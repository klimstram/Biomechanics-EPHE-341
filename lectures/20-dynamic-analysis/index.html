Dynamic Analysis

Last week every equation had zero on the right-hand side. This week the zeros become ma and Iα, and the method does not change.

Open by making the promise small, because it is true and it is reassuring. Last week they learned a method: free body diagram, force table, three equations, solve. Today they do not learn a new method. They learn that the same method works when things are accelerating, and the only edit is that the right-hand sides stop being zero. Then say what it buys. Static analysis could tell you about a gymnast holding still. Inverse dynamics gives the moment at someone's knee in the fourteenth step of a run, from a camera and a plate, with nothing inside the body measured at all.

Dynamic Analysis

Dynamic Equilibrium

  • State of an object which is moving with constant* (uniform) linear and/or angular velocity. * no acceleration.
  • Dynamic equilibrium exists if there is a balance between the applied forces and inertial forces for a body in motion, with all the applied forces resulting in equal and oppositely directed inertial forces.
  • Therefore the same rules apply from static to dynamic equilibrium but you need to keep in mind that the object is in motion.
The thing students assume is that "moving" means "dynamic". It does not. A cyclist holding 30 km/h on the flat has zero net force acting on them, and so does a cheetah at top speed once it stops speeding up. The second bullet is where the phrase comes from: put ma on the diagram as though it were a force pointing backwards, and the sums balance again. That is d'Alembert's idea, and it is why the third bullet is true — the same rules carry over. We will keep ma on the right-hand side for the rest of the lecture rather than moving it across, but the slide title is worth explaining or it sounds like a contradiction.

Dynamic Analysis

When it is not in equilibrium

  • When an object is not in equilibrium the net forces do not equal zero and it requires a non-equilibrium dynamic analysis.
  • To understand dynamic analysis we need to start with Newton’s second law.
Short slide, one job: say that the interesting case is the one where the sums are not zero, and that it needs a different kind of analysis. Point at the vectors and note that they move, tilt and change length through the step. That is what a force plate gives us, and it is the only external force in the whole problem. Then hand straight over to the second law.

Dynamic Analysis

Newton’s Second Law — the law of acceleration

Linear

A net force applied to a body causes an acceleration of that body of a magnitude proportional to the force, in the direction of the force, and inversely proportional to the body’s mass.

ΣF = ma

Angular

A net torque (moment) applied to a body causes an angular acceleration of that body of a magnitude proportional to the torque, in the direction of the torque (moment), and inversely proportional to the moment of inertia.

ΣM = Iα

Two cautions that cost marks later. The a in F = ma is the acceleration of the centre of mass — not of the heel, not of the joint. And the M and the I in M = Iα must be taken about the same axis.

Read the two statements as a pair and let the symmetry do the work. Force to mass as torque is to moment of inertia; linear acceleration to angular acceleration. The two cautions are worth writing on the board. The centre-of-mass one is the reason the next few slides insist on taking everything about the centre of mass, and the same-axis one is why we do not mix a moment taken about the joint with an I taken about the centre of mass.

Dynamic Analysis

In two dimensions

  • In a 2-dimensional analysis it is important to consider the components of forces and moment(s).
  • This leaves us with the 3 equations of a system under dynamic conditions.

ΣFx = max

ΣFy = may

ΣM = Iα

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) wrote the rotational law for a rigid body, and the two laws together describe any motion of anything solid. Hence the Newton–Euler equations, which is what this method is called in every paper that uses it.

Three equations means three unknowns, and not one more. Everything about how inverse dynamics is organised follows from that one sentence.

Three equations per segment. Say the number out loud, because the whole structure of the method is a consequence of it and we come back to it in about ten minutes. Euler is worth thirty seconds. Students meet the phrase "Newton-Euler equations" in every biomechanics paper they will ever read and nobody tells them where the Euler came from.

Dynamic Analysis

Why two dimensions

  • Dynamic analysis of a multi-segment rigid body (like a human body) in 3-dimensions can be very complex.
  • We will cover 2-dimensional examples of dynamic analysis.

Everything from here is planar: motion in the sagittal plane, forces in the sagittal plane, one moment axis perpendicular to it.

This is not only a teaching simplification. A large fraction of published gait analysis reports sagittal joint moments computed exactly this way, because the sagittal plane is where most of walking happens.

At the end of the lecture we run the method on a real stride and compare it with a three-dimensional solution of the same data.

Pre-empt the objection. Yes, people are three-dimensional. In 3D there are six equations rather than three and the moment equation picks up terms with no planar equivalent — the method is identical, the bookkeeping is not. And promise the payoff: at the end we check our two-dimensional answer against a three-dimensional one computed by the people who recorded the data.

Forces in, motion out — or motion in, forces out. One of them you can do to a person.

Dynamic Analysis

Forward dynamics

  • There are 2 approaches to solving dynamic problems depending on what variables are known or can be manipulated.
  • Forward and inverse dynamics.

Forward dynamics analysis

  • When you know the forces and are trying to determine the accelerations.
  • An example of this is in robotics where you have motors that produce a prescribed force or torque.
  • That is, given the forces and torques, what are the linear and angular accelerations?

forces → a, α → motion

This is the direction every physics problem you have ever been set runs in. It is also the direction you cannot run on a person, because nobody can tell you what the muscle forces were.

Name both approaches here, then take forward dynamics on its own for the next few slides and come back for inverse. The asymmetry is worth planting early. Forward dynamics on a person would mean knowing every muscle force. The handful of studies that have measured internal forces directly used instrumented implants in a few dozen people in the whole history of the field.

Dynamic Analysis

Simulation

Another application of forward dynamics is in the simulation of human movement, when approximate muscle forces are input into models to replicate the performance of a movement.

Build a model — segments, joints, muscles with force–length and force–velocity properties — choose a pattern of activation, and let the equations run. The model walks, or jumps, or falls over.

Then change something you could never change in a person: delete a muscle, weaken a tendon, alter a moment arm, add a prosthesis. Run it again.

Forward dynamics answers what would happen if. Inverse dynamics answers what did happen. We need both, and they are not substitutes.

The what-if versus what-happened distinction is the useful one. Simulation is how you test a surgery before performing it, or a shoe before building it. And set up the ending: a forward model carries hundreds of assumptions, an inverse-dynamics calculation maybe ten. We count them later and find out what they cost.

Dynamic Analysis

Inverse dynamics analysis

  • When you know the kinematics and are trying to solve for the kinetics.
  • An example of this type of solution is when you can measure the movement of the body segment and want to solve for the muscle force and joint reaction forces required to produce that movement.

motion + external forces → internal forces

You cannot put a load cell inside somebody. You can put a camera in front of them and a force plate under them — so for human movement this is the only direction available.

Everything from here to the end of the lecture is this one calculation.

This is the hinge of the lecture. Say plainly that inverse dynamics is not a second-best — for human movement it is the only door into the building. Two quick examples next, one linear and one angular, then the real thing.

Dynamic Analysis

Example Problem #1 (inverse dynamics)

What is the resultant force applied to a 23 kg object if the acceleration is 17.6 m/s²?

Solution

ΣFx = m·ax

F = 23(17.6)

F = 404.8 N

Thirty seconds. One equation, one substitution. The only thing worth saying is that we are running it backwards from the usual school problem: the acceleration was the measurement and the force is the answer. That is the whole idea of inverse dynamics in one line.

Dynamic Analysis

Example Problem #1 (inverse dynamics)

Calculate the moment required for an angular acceleration of 112 rad/s², given the moment of inertia about the elbow is 0.18 kg·m².

Solution

ΣM = I·α

M = 0.18(112)

M = 20.2 N·m

The angular twin of the previous slide, and the first time they see a moment of inertia used in anger. Worth one sentence: 0.18 kg·m² is a real forearm, 112 rad/s² is a brisk elbow flexion, and 20 N·m is what a joint has to produce to do it. Those are the orders of magnitude for the rest of the lecture.

The method

Inverse Dynamics

Inverse dynamics in human gait analysis

  • We can record the movement of the human body using a motion capture system, video camera, accelerometers, gyros, etc…
  • and measure the external forces,
  • but we do not know what is going on inside the body to produce the motion.

Based on recorded data (motion trajectories, GRFs) and simple dynamics principles (Newton–Euler equations) we can reconstruct the net joint moments and forces.

The word net is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and we come back to it before the end.

This is the sales pitch for the whole method. Cameras and a plate on the outside, joint moments on the inside, and nothing invasive anywhere. Flag the word "net" now and promise to return to it. Students hear "the moment at the knee" and picture a muscle. It is the sum of everything crossing that joint — every muscle, every ligament, the capsule, the contact. A co-contracting pair can produce enormous forces and a net moment of zero.

Inverse Dynamics

One segment at a time, starting at the bottom

  • Inverse dynamics involves taking into account all the forces and moments that act on each segment, and the effect of these forces and moments on the following segments.
  • For example, if we want to find out the forces and moments at the hip during a movement we need to consider each segment separately.
  • We need to start with the most distal segment and then carry the solutions for each joint forward.

Step 1: Foot
Step 2: Leg
Step 3: Thigh

Each step hands its answer to the next one. Nothing can be solved out of order.

Two ideas, and the second one is the one that makes the rest feel inevitable rather than arbitrary: the order is forced. Do not justify it yet — the next figure does that with a ledger of unknowns. Just assert it here and let them wonder why.

Inverse Dynamics

Why the order is forced

Press "Start at the hip" and let them look at the ledger. The thigh has an unknown joint at both ends: six unknowns, three equations. There is no clever algebra that fixes that. Then press "Start at the foot". One end of the foot is the ground, and a force plate has measured it. Three unknowns, three equations. Solve, hand the ankle up, and now the shank has three unknowns too. The phrase to leave them with: inverse dynamics runs from the ground up because the ground is the only place where anything is known.

Inverse Dynamics

A net joint force and a net joint moment

1. We need to consider all the forces acting upon the segment and the moments that they would produce.

  • bone-on-bone forces
  • ligament forces
  • muscle forces, and the joint moment of force they produce
  • the segment’s own weight
  • the external force, where there is one

2. Really we can say that we can add all these forces to create a net joint force and a net joint moment, and then account for the internal and external moments.

Walk the list on the left slowly. The commonest mistake in the whole topic is leaving something off the diagram, and the second commonest is forgetting that the joint carries a moment as well as a force. Then make the simplification explicit: every muscle, ligament and contact force crossing the ankle is replaced by one net force and one net moment. That replacement is exact for the mechanics — the segment accelerates identically — and lossy for the anatomy, because afterwards there is no way of telling which muscle did it.

Inverse Dynamics

The forces, in general form

If we calculate all values with reference to the centre of mass we can use the general forms of the Newton–Euler equations that we learned earlier.

ΣFx = Fx-proximal + Fx-distal = max

ΣFy = Fy-proximal + Fy-distal + mg = may

While these are the general forms of the equations you need to keep close attention to the directions (signs) of the forces when calculating the values.

Two sentences to land, and they are both in the caption. The distal end of the foot is the ground, and the plate measured that force. The proximal end is the ankle, and that is what we are solving for. Every segment after this one has the same shape: one end known, one end unknown. Then the signs. The equations as written assume every component is positive in the positive direction. Put a negative number in and the arithmetic handles it — but you have to be the one who knows the number is negative.

Inverse Dynamics

The forces, with specific values

In this example we can give specific forces.

ΣFx = Fankle-x + GRFx = max

Fankle-x = max − GRFx

ΣFy = Fankle-y + GRFy + mg = may

Fankle-y = may − GRFy − mg

Same diagram, same equations, names instead of placeholders. Nothing new has happened — that is the point of showing it twice. Rearranged, each force equation gives one unknown directly. Two of the three unknowns are now gone, and only the ankle moment is left. It is very important to pay close attention to the signs.

Inverse Dynamics

The moments

ΣM = Iα

Mankle + MFankle/CG + MGRF/CG = Iα

Mankle

+ Fankle-x · dP(sinθ)

+ Fankle-y · dP(cosθ)

+ GRFx · dD(sinθ)

+ GRFy · dD(cosθ)

= Iα

Read the top line first and name the three pieces: the moment at the ankle itself, the moment the ankle force makes about the centre of mass, and the moment the ground reaction force makes about the centre of mass. Then expand. Each force contributes two terms because each force has two components, and each component turns the segment through its own arm. dP is the distance from the centre of mass to the proximal joint; dD to the distal end; theta is the segment's angle. The pairing is the thing students get backwards. Fx is paired with dP sin theta, which is the VERTICAL offset. The arm a force turns through is perpendicular to it.

Inverse Dynamics

The same moments, with the geometry drawn

ΣM = Iα

Mankle

+ Fankle-x · dP(sinθ)

+ Fankle-y · dP(cosθ)

+ GRFx · dD(sinθ)

+ GRFy · dD(cosθ)

= Iα

dPsinθ and dDsinθ are vertical offsets, and they go with the horizontal forces. The arm a force turns through is perpendicular to it.

Same equation, with the arms resolved. This is the slide to point at when somebody asks why sine goes with x. Drop a vertical from the centre of mass to the level of the joint: that height is dP sin theta, and it is the arm that a horizontal force turns through. Go across instead and you get dP cos theta, which is the arm for a vertical force. Draw it on the board if there is one. Fx applied above the centre of mass turns the segment; applied level with it, it does not.

Up the chain

Now that we have found the net ankle joint force and moment we can then use that information to input into the next limb segment.

ΣFx = max

ΣFy = may

ΣM = Iα

Same three equations, new segment. The leg’s distal end is the ankle, and we have just finished measuring it.

Nothing new in the method. The only thing to flag is that the distal arrows have flipped, and the next slide says why. If anyone is drifting, this is the moment to say that the whole lecture is now just this slide repeated: solve a segment, flip what you found, hand it up.

Inverse Dynamics

The leg, with specific forces

In this example we can give specific forces.

ΣFx = Fknee-x − Fankle-x = max(leg)

Fknee-x = max + Fankle-x

ΣFy = Fknee-y − Fankle-y + mg = may(leg)

Fknee-y = may + Fankle-y − mg

Need to consider action/reaction forces. Those minus signs are the whole content of this slide.

The step everyone drops. What the foot's free body produced was the force the shank applies to the foot. What the shank feels at its lower end is that force reversed. Put both diagrams side by side in their heads: the arrow at the ankle pointed up on the foot, so it points down on the leg. Same number, opposite sign, and that is why the equations have minus signs in front of the ankle terms. The arithmetic will not complain if you forget. You will just get the wrong knee force and never find out.

Inverse Dynamics

The leg, with specific moments

In this example we can give specific moments.

ΣM = Iα

Mknee + MFknee/CG + MFankle/CG − Mankle = Iα

Mknee = Iα − MFknee/CG − MFankle/CG + Mankle

Really just remember that what we solved for at the ankle will affect each limb up the chain.

This is the slide the next figure unpacks, so read the equation carefully and count the terms with them: four moments, and they add to I alpha. Mknee is the one we want. MFknee/CG and MFankle/CG are the moments the two joint forces make about the centre of mass. Mankle is the moment we already solved at the foot, arriving here reversed — hence the minus. Then the rearranged line: everything known on the right, the unknown alone on the left. That is the shape of every step up the chain.

Inverse Dynamics

Four moments on one segment

This is the figure for the last two slides and it is worth the time. Take the terms one at a time with the buttons. The moment at the distal end; the moment at the proximal end; the moment about the centre of mass made by the distal force; the moment about the centre of mass made by the proximal force. Inverse Dynamics

In summary…

What do you need to remember from the last few slides? In order to solve for joint forces and moments:

  • You need to know the kinematics of the movement and any external forces.
  • Break up each multi-limb segment into individual limbs.
  • Use the Newton–Euler equations to solve for unknown net forces and moments for each joint, starting from the most distal segment.
  • Transfer forces and moments up the link of segments.

This gives you an idea of the forces and moments experienced at each joint that result from the external forces and the movements.

Pause here and let them write it down. Four lines, and it is the whole method. If time is short, this is the slide to stop on. Everything after it is one worked example and then an honest look at what the answer can be trusted to say.

Inverse Dynamics

What inverse dynamics can and cannot tell us

What it can tell us

  • NET joint moments, powers and forces.

What it cannot tell us

  • Contributions/activations of individual muscles.
  • Total amount of muscle work done — it does not account for co-contraction of muscles.
  • Internal bone-to-bone forces at joints.

Other assumptions and limitations

  • Frictionless joints.
  • Generalized, uniform and/or concentrated mass distribution.
  • Estimation of joint centre of rotation.
  • Measurement errors: skin and motion artifacts, temporal or spatial misalignment between force plates and motion capture.
This is the slide that keeps the method honest, and it is worth more than it looks. Net is the word. A knee moment of 40 N·m does not mean one muscle pulling. It means everything crossing the joint summed to 40 N·m, and a co-contracting pair could be producing hundreds of newtons each while the sum stays small. Keep the limitations list in mind for the last part of the lecture — we run the method on a real stride, compare it with a three-dimensional solution of the same data, and every item on this list is in the difference.

On Real Data

The same method, run for real

Press Walk and let a full stride go by before you say anything. Foot first, then leg, then thigh — the four steps from the summary slide, on a real recording. Then the honest conversation. The shapes match: r is 0.99 at the ankle and 0.98 at the knee, and both peaks land on the same instant of the cycle as the published ones. The magnitudes do not: ours runs fifteen per cent high at the ankle and thirty-seven per cent at the knee. Say plainly that the gap was chased. It is not a timing offset between the camera and the plate — the best fit is two milliseconds. It is not the centre of pressure — shifting it helps push-off and ruins early stance. It is not one joint centre — the ankle wants to move forwards and the knee wants to move backwards, so it is not a registration error either. What is left is every item on the limitations slide: a cadaver-derived anthropometric table, joint centres estimated from markers on skin, two dimensions instead of three. That is the honest size of the disagreement between two reasonable pipelines on identical data. The thing to leave them with: a joint moment is not a measurement. It is a calculation, and two careful people can differ by fifteen per cent.

On Real Data

When do you actually need the dynamics?

Back to the first two slides of the lecture, now with a number on them. Take the full Newton-Euler solution of this stride and set every ma and I alpha term to zero — that is, treat each segment as if it were in equilibrium. At the ankle in stance, the push-off peak moves from 1.738 to 1.737 N·m/kg. Six hundredths of one per cent. The foot weighs eight hundred grams and the ground is pushing on it with 678 newtons, so the inertial terms cannot matter there, and they do not. Then press "The knee" and show the other half. Over stance the two differ by a fifth of the peak. Through swing the equilibrium answer has the wrong sign for most of the phase, because there is no external force at all and the segment's own acceleration is the only thing acting. So the rule is not "dynamics always matters". It is: the inertial terms matter when the external forces are small and the segment is moving. Which is exactly the case the worked example chose, and it is why those numbers came out in fractions of a newton metre.

Dynamic Analysis

What to take away

  • Dynamic analysis is static analysis with ma and Iα on the right. The diagram, the table and the conventions are unchanged.
  • Three equations means three unknowns, which is why you start at the segment touching the ground and work upward.
  • Four moments act on every segment, and they add to Iα: one at each end, and one about the centre of mass from each end’s force.
  • Both the force and the moment reverse at every joint on the way up.
  • Take moments about each segment’s own centre of mass, and pair each force with the arm perpendicular to it.
  • A joint moment is a calculation, not a measurement, and it is only as good as the anatomy you fed it.
The six lines they should be able to reproduce cold. If you only have time to say one thing, say the last one. It is the difference between a student who can do the arithmetic and one who can read a paper.

Dynamic Analysis

Where this goes next

Multiply a joint moment by the joint’s angular velocity and you get joint power — how fast that joint is putting energy into the body or taking it out. That one multiplication turns this lecture into the standard clinical description of walking.

And the honest limitation remains: inverse dynamics tells you what the joint did, never which muscle did it. Getting from a net moment to individual muscle forces needs either a measurement we cannot make, or a forward model and an assumption about what the nervous system is trying to optimise.
