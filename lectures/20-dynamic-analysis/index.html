Dynamic Analysis

Last week every equation had zero on the right-hand side. This week the zeros become ma and Iα, and nothing else changes.

Open by making the promise small, because it is true and it is reassuring. Last week they learned a method: free body diagram, force table, three equations, solve. Today they do not learn a new method. They learn that the same method works when things are accelerating, and the only edit is that the right-hand sides stop being zero. Then say what it buys. Static analysis could tell you about a gymnast holding still. Inverse dynamics tells you the moment at someone's knee in the fourteenth step of a run — from a camera and a plate, with nothing inside the body measured at all. Essentially everything the field knows about joint loading comes from this calculation.

Dynamic Analysis

Where this fits

Newton’s first law gave us two cases, and last week we took the first one.

  • ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0 — nothing is accelerating. Static analysis.
  • ΣF ≠ 0 or ΣM ≠ 0 — the state of motion is changing. Dynamic analysis.

Almost everything a person does is the second case. Walking is the second case. So is standing up, throwing, landing, and pedalling.

A body moving at constant velocity is still the first case. No acceleration means no net force, however fast it is going. His slide 2 calls this dynamic equilibrium, and it is genuinely a statics problem wearing a coat.

The phrase comes from a trick: put the quantity ma on the diagram as though it were a force, pointing backwards, and the sums are zero again. That is d’Alembert’s idea, and it is why the method does not have to change.

Two things to land here. First, constant velocity is a statics problem. Students assume "moving" means "dynamic". It does not. A cyclist holding 30 km/h on the flat has zero net force on them. Second, the phrase "dynamic equilibrium" on his slide 2 confuses people until they see where it comes from. D'Alembert's trick: move ma to the left side and call it an inertial force. Then every dynamics problem looks like a statics problem. We will not use that framing for the rest of the lecture — we will keep ma on the right — but it explains the slide title.

Dynamic Analysis

The two equations, in general form

ΣF = m·a

Newton’s second law. The net force on a body equals its mass times the acceleration of its centre of mass — not of any other point on it.

ΣM = I·α

The rotational twin. The net moment equals the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration, both taken about the same axis.

In two dimensions that is three scalar equations, and three is exactly what a free body diagram gives you:

ΣFx = m·ax

ΣFy = m·ay

ΣM = I·α

Three equations means three unknowns, and not one more. Everything about how inverse dynamics is organised follows from that one sentence.

Two cautions, both of which cost marks later. The a in F = ma is the acceleration of the centre of mass. Not the heel, not the joint — the centre of mass of the segment you drew the free body around. The M and the I in M = I alpha must be about the same axis. We will take every moment about the segment's own centre of mass for the rest of the lecture, which is why his slide 21 says "if we calculate all values with reference to the centre of mass we can use the general forms". And flag the three-equations-three-unknowns line now. It is the reason we start at the foot, and we will come back to it in about ten minutes.

Dynamic Analysis

Leonhard Euler, 1707–1783

Newton gave us F = ma for a particle. It was Euler who wrote the rotational law for a rigid body and showed that the two together are enough to describe any motion of anything solid.

Hence Newton–Euler equations, which is what the method in this lecture is properly called, and what you will see it called in every paper that uses it.

Euler also lost the sight in one eye at 28 and in the other at 59, and produced roughly half of his work after going completely blind, dictating it to his sons. He is still the most prolific mathematician who has ever lived.

Thirty seconds of history, no more, but it earns its place: students meet the phrase "Newton-Euler equations" in every biomechanics paper they will ever read and nobody ever tells them where the Euler came from. The blindness detail is worth one sentence. It tends to stick.

Dynamic Analysis

Why two dimensions

Everything in this lecture is planar: motion in the sagittal plane, forces in the sagittal plane, one moment axis perpendicular to it.

In three dimensions there are six equations rather than three, and the moment equation acquires terms that have no two-dimensional equivalent. The method is identical. The bookkeeping is not.

This is not only a teaching simplification. A large fraction of published gait analysis reports sagittal joint moments computed exactly this way, because the sagittal plane is where most of walking happens.

At the end of the lecture we will run the real thing on a real stride and find out how much the simplification costs.

Pre-empt the objection. Yes, people are three-dimensional. The sagittal plane carries most of the mechanical work of walking, and two-dimensional inverse dynamics was the standard tool for decades and is still what most clinical reports show for flexion-extension moments. And promise the payoff: at the end we check our two-dimensional answer against a three-dimensional one computed by the people who recorded the data, and the difference is measurable and interesting.

Two directions

Forces in, motion out — or motion in, forces out. One of them you can do to a person.

Dynamic Analysis

Forward and inverse

Forward dynamics. You know the forces and the moments. You solve for the accelerations, and from them the motion.

This is what a simulation does, and what a robot’s controller does. It is also what every physics problem you have ever been set does.

forces → a, α → motion

Inverse dynamics. You know the motion, because you filmed it, and you know the external forces, because you stood the person on a plate. You solve for the forces and moments inside.

This is the one you can do to a human being, and it is the rest of this lecture.

motion + external forces → internal forces

The asymmetry is the whole point. You cannot put a load cell inside somebody. You can put a camera in front of them and a force plate under them — so the only direction available is the inverse one.

Make the asymmetry concrete. Forward dynamics on a person would mean knowing every muscle force. Nobody knows those. The handful of studies that have measured internal forces directly used instrumented implants in a few dozen people in the whole history of the field. So inverse dynamics is not a second-best. For human movement it is the only door into the building.

Dynamic Analysis

Where forward dynamics does earn its keep

Build a model of a body — segments, joints, muscles with force–length and force–velocity properties — choose a pattern of muscle activation, and let the equations run. The model walks, or jumps, or falls over.

Then change something you could never change in a person: delete a muscle, weaken a tendon, alter a moment arm, add a prosthesis. Run it again.

Forward dynamics answers questions of the form what would happen if. Inverse dynamics answers what did happen. You need both, and they are not substitutes.

The cost is that a forward model is only as good as the assumptions inside it, and there are hundreds of them. An inverse-dynamics answer rests on far fewer — which is the subject of the last third of this lecture.

His slide 12 is a single line about simulation; this expands it into something students can hold. The what-if versus what-happened distinction is the useful one. Simulation is how you test a surgery before performing it, or a shoe before building it. And set up the ending: a forward model has hundreds of assumptions, an inverse-dynamics calculation has maybe ten. We are going to count them later and find out what they cost.

Inverse Dynamics

What you can actually measure

We can record the movement: a motion capture system, video, accelerometers, gyroscopes.

We can record the external forces: a force plate gives the ground reaction force and where it acts.

We cannot record anything inside. Not one tendon, not one joint contact force, not one ligament.

So: recorded trajectories, recorded ground forces, Newton–Euler, and out comes the net joint moment and the net joint force at every joint, at every instant.

The word net is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and we will come back to it before the end.

This is his slide 16, and it is the sales pitch. Flag the word "net" now and promise to return to it. Students hear "the moment at the knee" and picture a muscle. It is the sum of everything crossing that joint — every muscle, every ligament, the capsule, the contact. A co-contracting pair of muscles can produce enormous forces and a net moment of zero.

Inverse Dynamics

Why you have to start at the foot

This is his slides 17 and 18 turned into arithmetic, and it is the part that makes the rest feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. Press "Start at the hip" and let them look at the ledger. The thigh has an unknown joint at both ends: six unknowns, three equations. There is no clever algebra that fixes that. Then press "Start at the foot". One end of the foot is the ground, and a force plate has measured it. Three unknowns, three equations. Solve, hand the ankle up, and now the shank has three unknowns too. The phrase to leave them with: inverse dynamics runs from the ground up because the ground is the only place where anything is known.

Inverse Dynamics

The foot, cut free

Draw the foot on its own and put on it everything that touches it:

  • the ground reaction force, acting at the centre of pressure — measured;
  • its own weight, acting at its centre of mass — from the anthropometric table;
  • the force the shank applies at the ankle, two components — unknown;
  • and the moment the shank applies at the ankle — unknown.

Three unknowns, three equations. The two force sums come first, because the ankle force appears in the moment equation and you need its value before you can use it. That is the only ordering rule in the whole method.

His slide 19 makes the simplification explicit: every muscle, ligament and contact force crossing the ankle is replaced by one net force and one net moment. That replacement is exact for the mechanics and lossy for the anatomy.

Walk the list slowly. The commonest mistake in the whole topic is leaving something off the free body diagram, and the second commonest is forgetting that the joint carries a moment as well as a force. The "exact for the mechanics, lossy for the anatomy" line is worth saying twice. Replacing twenty muscle forces by one force and one moment loses no mechanics at all — the segment accelerates identically. What it loses is any way of telling which muscle did it.

Inverse Dynamics

The moment arms

Take every moment about the segment’s own centre of mass. Then Iα is the plain Iα from the table, with no parallel-axis correction.

His slides write each force’s moment as two terms:

Fx·d(sinθ) + Fy·d(cosθ)

which is the same thing as the cross product r × F, written out. The horizontal force acts through a vertical arm, and the vertical force through a horizontal one.

Pairing Fx with the horizontal distance is the single commonest error in this topic. The arm a force turns through is perpendicular to it.

His slides give the arms as positive lengths and assign the signs by looking at the picture. That works, and it is also where marks go missing. If you write the positions as signed vectors first and take r × F, the signs look after themselves — including the reversal at the ankle that Newton’s third law demands.

Draw this on the board if there is one. Fx pointing forward, applied above the centre of mass: it turns the segment. Applied level with the centre of mass: it does not. The arm is the vertical offset. His notation dP sin theta and dP cos theta is correct — the sine of the segment angle times the segment-to-joint distance is the vertical component. Students read it as "x goes with x" and get it backwards. The signed-vector advice is the thing to actually teach. It is one extra line of setup and it removes an entire category of error.

Inverse Dynamics

Up the chain

The step everyone drops. What the foot's free body produced was the force AND the moment that the shank applies to the foot. What the shank feels at its lower end is both of those, reversed. His slide 26 writes the force reversal explicitly — minus F ankle — and his slide 27 carries the moment reversal as the minus M ankle term. Press "Forget the moment" and watch the knee answer move. The arithmetic does not complain, the units are still right, and the answer is wrong by exactly the ankle moment. That is what makes it such a reliable mistake.

Inverse Dynamics

The method, in six lines

  • Record the kinematics and any external forces.
  • Get each segment’s mass, centre of mass and moment of inertia from an anthropometric table.
  • Differentiate the kinematics twice for a and α.
  • Start at the most distal segment and write its three equations.
  • Reverse the force and the moment you found, and apply them to the next segment up.
  • Repeat to the top of the chain.

That is his slide 28, and it is the examinable part. Everything after this is one worked example and then a long, honest look at how much the answer can be trusted.

Pause here and let them write it down. Six lines, and it is the whole method. If you are short of time, this is the slide to stop on. Everything after it is either practice or epistemology.

The one number that cannot be right

This is the part to teach, not just to show. Slides 33 and 34 put the ankle one centimetre vertically and two centimetres horizontally from the foot's centre of mass. That is 22 millimetres away in total. A foot's centre of mass is about ten centimetres from the ankle. Three of his own numbers say so. The foot inertia he gives, 0.00995 with a mass of 1.16, implies a radius of gyration of 93 millimetres; Winter's ratio then puts the centre of mass 97 millimetres from the ankle. His own Example 2 uses 0.07 and 0.07, which is 99 millimetres. And the figure he drew puts the centre of mass just under halfway along the foot, which is correct — only the two labels are wrong. Slide it across and the ankle moment goes from 0.39 to about 1.0 newton metres and the knee from 1.71 to about 2.3. Then say the useful thing: everything else on those slides is right to the digit. On Real Data

The recording

One stride of overground walking. A woman, 55 kg, 1.61 m, walking at 1.14 m/s with a cadence of 106 steps a minute and a stride length of 1.31 m.

Markers at 60 Hz; the ground reaction force and the centre of pressure at 960 Hz. The plate is under the right foot for the first 60 % of the cycle. Peak vertical force 678 N, which is 1.26 body weights.

Lencioni et al. (2019), Scientific Data 6:309, CC BY 4.0 — the same recording lectures 16 and 19 use.

The reason for using this one: the authors published their own joint moments for the same stride, computed with their own three-dimensional model. So we can do it from scratch and then mark our own work against somebody who was not trying to agree with us.

Segment masses and inertias from Winter’s table, joint centres taken at the skin markers, every derivative taken on a periodically extended record so the seam of the stride does not produce an artefact.

Emphasise the independence. This is not a demonstration that was tuned until it agreed. The published curves were computed by the people who collected the data, in a different lab, with a different model, years before this deck existed. Mention the shank length check if there is time: measured 0.385 metres on a 1.61 metre person is 0.239 of stature, against Winter's 0.246. The markers and the table agree about the body before we compute anything.

On Real Data

The same method, run for real

Press Walk and let a full stride go by before you say anything. Then the honest conversation. The shapes match: r is 0.99 at the ankle and 0.98 at the knee, and both peaks land on the same instant of the cycle as theirs. The magnitudes do not: ours runs fifteen per cent high at the ankle and thirty-seven per cent at the knee. Say plainly that the gap was chased. It is not a timing offset between the camera and the plate — the best fit is two milliseconds. It is not the centre of pressure — shifting it helps push-off and ruins early stance. It is not one joint centre — the ankle wants to move forwards and the knee wants to move backwards, so it is not a registration error either. What is left is the model. Dempster's table against theirs; skin markers taken as joint centres against their regressions; two dimensions against three. That is the honest size of the disagreement between two reasonable pipelines on identical data, and it is the first three lines of his own slide 29. The thing to leave them with: a joint moment is not a measurement. It is a calculation, and two careful people can differ by fifteen per cent.

On Real Data

So how much of this is dynamics?

This is the figure last week's lecture asked for, and the answer is not what anybody expects. Last week we solved a walking ankle as a statics problem and got 1.74 newton metres per kilogram against the published 1.51, and left the missing fifteen per cent hanging with the obvious suspect being the dynamics we had thrown away. It was not them. Take the full Newton-Euler solution and set every ma and I alpha term to zero: the ankle's push-off peak moves from 1.738 to 1.737. Six hundredths of one per cent. The foot weighs eight hundred grams, and the ground is pushing on it with 678 newtons. Then press "The knee" and show the other half. Over stance the two differ by a fifth of the peak. Through swing the static answer has the wrong sign for most of it, because there is no external force at all and the segment's own acceleration is the only thing acting. So the rule worth remembering is not "dynamics matters" or "dynamics does not matter". It is: dynamics matters when the external forces are small and the segment is moving. Which is exactly the case his worked example chose — and now they know why he chose it.

On Real Data

What inverse dynamics cannot tell you

It gives you: the net joint moment, the net joint force and the joint power, at every joint, at every instant.

It cannot give you:

  • which muscles produced the moment, or how hard any one of them pulled;
  • the total muscular work, because co-contraction is invisible to it — two muscles pulling hard against each other sum to nothing;
  • the bone-on-bone contact force, which is far larger than the net joint force and is not the same quantity.

And it assumes: frictionless joints; rigid segments with masses and inertias taken from a table built on cadavers; joint centres estimated from markers on skin that slides over bone; and perfect alignment, in space and in time, between the camera system and the force plate.

Those assumptions are not hypothetical. We just watched them cost fifteen per cent at the ankle and thirty-seven at the knee on a clean recording of ordinary walking.

His slide 29, with the previous two figures standing behind it. The co-contraction point deserves a demonstration if you have thirty seconds: have them hold their arm still and contract biceps and triceps together as hard as they can. Enormous forces, zero net moment, and inverse dynamics would report nothing happening. The net joint force versus bone-on-bone force distinction is the one students most often get wrong on exams. The net joint force is what is left after the muscles have been summed into the moment. The actual contact force between the bones is much larger, because the muscles squeeze the joint together.

Dynamic Analysis

What to take away

  • Dynamic analysis is static analysis with ma and Iα on the right. The diagram, the table and the conventions are unchanged.
  • Three equations means three unknowns, which is why you start at the segment touching the ground and work upward.
  • Both the force and the moment reverse at every joint on the way up.
  • Take moments about each segment’s own centre of mass, and pair each force with the arm perpendicular to it.
  • In stance the ground force dominates everything; in swing the segment’s own inertia is all there is.
  • A joint moment is a calculation, not a measurement, and it is only as good as the anatomy you fed it.
The six lines they should be able to reproduce cold. If you only have time to say one thing, say the last one. It is the difference between a student who can do the arithmetic and one who can read a paper.

Dynamic Analysis

Where this goes next

Multiply a joint moment by the joint’s angular velocity and you get joint power — how fast that joint is putting energy into the body or taking it out. That one multiplication turns this lecture into the standard clinical description of walking.

And the honest limitation remains: inverse dynamics tells you what the joint did, never which muscle did it. Getting from a net moment to individual muscle forces needs either a measurement we cannot make, or a forward model and an assumption about what the nervous system is trying to optimise.

All done.

His slide 42 says "All done!!" and that is fair enough, but it is worth one forward look. Joint power is moment times angular velocity, and it is what every clinical gait report plots underneath the moments. The ankle generates a large burst of power at push-off; the knee mostly absorbs. One multiplication away from everything in this lecture. And close on the limitation, because it is the honest end of the arc: this method is how we know almost everything we know about joint loading, and it still cannot name a single muscle.
