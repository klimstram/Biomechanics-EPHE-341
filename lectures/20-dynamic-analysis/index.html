Dynamic Analysis

Last week every equation had zero on the right-hand side. This week the zeros become ma and Iα, and the method does not change.

Open by making the promise small, because it is true and it is reassuring. Last week they learned a method: free body diagram, force table, three equations, solve. Today they do not learn a new method. They learn that the same method works when things are accelerating, and the only edit is that the right-hand sides stop being zero. Then say what it buys. Static analysis could tell you about a gymnast holding still. Inverse dynamics gives the moment at someone's knee in the fourteenth step of a run, from a camera and a plate, with nothing inside the body measured at all.

Dynamic Analysis

Dynamic Equilibrium

  • State of an object which is moving with constant* (uniform) linear and/or angular velocity. * no acceleration.
  • Dynamic equilibrium exists if there is a balance between the applied forces and inertial forces for a body in motion, with all the applied forces resulting in equal and oppositely directed inertial forces.
  • Therefore the same rules apply from static to dynamic equilibrium but you need to keep in mind that the object is in motion.

Equal times, equal distances. That is the whole test, and you can run it on any recording you have.

Do not define dynamic equilibrium in the abstract. Put the strobe up and ask them how they would tell, from this picture alone, whether the person is speeding up. The answer is the spacing. Equal times, equal distances, no acceleration. They can measure it off the figure, and the four numbers underneath are what they would get. Then the honest part, because it sets up the next slide: the four distances are not quite equal. Walking is not uniform motion, it only looks like it from a distance, and the next slide is about what to do when the balance does not hold.

Dynamic Analysis

When it is not in equilibrium

When an object is not in equilibrium the net forces do not equal zero and it requires a non-equilibrium dynamic analysis. To understand dynamic analysis we need to start with Newton’s second law.

This is the previous slide differentiated, and it is the whole argument for the lecture. Press Walk and let it run. Then stop it anywhere and read the four numbers down the right. The forward speed is never the same twice; it swings from 1.06 to 1.39 metres per second about a mean of 1.15. The centre of mass rises and falls 34 millimetres, twice every stride. So the acceleration is not zero. So the net force is not zero. So last week's method — everything sums to zero — does not apply, and the rest of the lecture is what to do instead. Worth saying plainly: there is no force plate in this figure. Markers only. You can show a body is not in equilibrium from the motion alone, and that is exactly the asymmetry inverse dynamics exploits.

Dynamic Analysis

Newton’s Second Law — the law of acceleration

Linear

A net force applied to a body causes an acceleration of that body of a magnitude proportional to the force, in the direction of the force, and inversely proportional to the body’s mass.

ΣF = ma

Angular

A net torque (moment) applied to a body causes an angular acceleration of that body of a magnitude proportional to the torque, in the direction of the torque (moment), and inversely proportional to the moment of inertia.

ΣM = Iα

Two cautions that cost marks later. The a in F = ma is the acceleration of the centre of mass — not of the heel, not of the joint. And the M and the I in M = Iα must be taken about the same axis.

Read the two statements as a pair and let the symmetry do the work. Force to mass as torque is to moment of inertia; linear acceleration to angular acceleration. The two cautions are worth writing on the board. The centre-of-mass one is the reason the next few slides insist on taking everything about the centre of mass, and the same-axis one is why we do not mix a moment taken about the joint with an I taken about the centre of mass.

Dynamic Analysis

In two dimensions

  • In a 2-dimensional analysis it is important to consider the components of forces and moment(s).
  • This leaves us with the 3 equations of a system under dynamic conditions.

ΣFx = max

ΣFy = may

ΣM = Iα

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) wrote the rotational law for a rigid body, and the two laws together describe any motion of anything solid. Hence the Newton–Euler equations, which is what this method is called in every paper that uses it.

Three equations means three unknowns, and not one more. Everything about how inverse dynamics is organised follows from that one sentence.

Three equations per segment. Say the number out loud, because the whole structure of the method is a consequence of it and we come back to it in about ten minutes. Euler is worth thirty seconds. Students meet the phrase "Newton-Euler equations" in every biomechanics paper they will ever read and nobody tells them where the Euler came from. He is blind in the right eye in that portrait — he lost it in his twenties and the other one about fifteen years before he died, and he produced roughly half his output after going completely blind, dictating to his sons. Worth one sentence if the room is awake.

Dynamic Analysis

Why two dimensions

  • Dynamic analysis of a multi-segment rigid body (like a human body) in 3-dimensions can be very complex.
  • We will cover 2-dimensional examples of dynamic analysis.

In the plane a segment can do exactly three things: move across, move up, and turn. Three degrees of freedom, three equations — the same three from the last slide. In three dimensions it is six of each.

This is not only a teaching simplification. A large fraction of published gait analysis reports sagittal joint moments computed exactly this way, because the sagittal plane is where most of walking happens.

Answer the objection with a count rather than an opinion. In the plane a rigid segment can translate in x, translate in y, and rotate. Three degrees of freedom. That is why three equations close it, and the number on the previous slide was not arbitrary. In three dimensions it is six degrees of freedom and six equations, and the moment equation picks up terms with no planar equivalent. The method is identical; the bookkeeping is not. Then walk the figure. Everything blue belongs to the centre of gravity and everything purple belongs to the segment. Those five quantities are the complete kinematic description of one segment at one instant, and they are all measured — this is frame 62 of the stride we solve at the end of the lecture. Note that alpha is large and negative while omega is positive: the shank is swinging forwards and being braked hard, just before the knee extends for heel strike. That is the moment the hamstrings are working hardest, and we compute it later. And promise the payoff: at the end we check our two-dimensional answer against a three-dimensional one computed by the people who recorded the data.

Two directions

Forces in, motion out — or motion in, forces out. One of them you can do to a person.

Dynamic Analysis

Forward dynamics

  • There are 2 approaches to solving dynamic problems depending on what variables are known or can be manipulated.
  • Forward and inverse dynamics.

Forward dynamics analysis

  • When you know the forces and are trying to determine the accelerations.
  • An example of this is in robotics where you have motors that produce a prescribed force or torque.
  • That is, given the forces and torques, what are the linear and angular accelerations?

forces → a, α → motion

This is the direction every physics problem you have ever been set runs in. It is also the direction you cannot run on a person, because nobody can tell you what the muscle forces were.

Name both approaches here, then take forward dynamics on its own for the next few slides and come back for inverse. The asymmetry is worth planting early. Forward dynamics on a person would mean knowing every muscle force. The handful of studies that have measured internal forces directly used instrumented implants in a few dozen people in the whole history of the field.

Example Problem #1 (forward dynamics)

What is the acceleration if a force of 100 N is applied to an object with a mass of 10 kg?

One equation, one substitution. Then move the slider: double the force and the acceleration doubles, because a is F over m and m has not changed. The wavy line on his original figure is the acceleration. He draws it wavy so it is never mistaken for a force vector, which is a habit worth copying.

Dynamic Analysis

Example Problem #2 (forward dynamics)

What is the acceleration if a force of 100 N and a force of 150 N are applied to a 10 kg object such that no rotation can occur — that is, both forces pass through the centre of gravity?

The only new thing is the sum. Both forces act through the centre of gravity, so nothing turns and the two just add. The slider moves the first force only, so they can watch the sum and the answer move together while the second force sits still.

Dynamic Analysis

Example Problem #3 (forward dynamics)

Calculate the angular acceleration caused by the 100 N force on the segment if the moment of inertia of the segment is 0.15 kg·m².

Same shape of problem, the angular version. The force is perpendicular to the segment, so the moment arm is just the 0.1 m. Stop on the sign. His caption says this clockwise torque causes a negative moment and a negative angular acceleration, and it does — so the answer is minus 66.67, and the printed 66.67 has lost its sign. Dynamic Analysis

Example Problem #4 (forward dynamics)

Calculate the angular acceleration caused by both the 100 N and the 200 N forces on the segment if the moment of inertia of the segment is 0.15 kg·m².

Two moments now, each force through its own arm, and they add. Ask before you reveal: the 200 N force is twice as big but acts on a shorter arm. Which contributes more? 100 times 0.1 is 10; 200 times 0.06 is 12. Nearly the same, which is the point — a moment is a force AND an arm, and neither one on its own tells you anything.

Simulation

Another application of forward dynamics is in the simulation of human movement, when approximate muscle forces are input into models to replicate the performance of a movement.

Dynamic Analysis

Inverse dynamics analysis

  • When you know the kinematics and are trying to solve for the kinetics.
  • An example of this type of solution is when you can measure the movement of the body segment and want to solve for the muscle force and joint reaction forces required to produce that movement.

motion + external forces → internal forces

You cannot put a load cell inside somebody. You can put a camera in front of them and a plate under them — so this is the only direction available.

Everything from here on is this one calculation, and the figure is what it must account for: ten per segment, thirty for the limb.

This is the hinge of the lecture. Say plainly that inverse dynamics is not a second-best — for human movement it is the only door into the building. Then walk the figure once, slowly, top to bottom. Every cut you make through a limb exposes three things: a force in x, a force in y, and a moment. Every segment you cut out carries four of its own: its weight, m a-x, m a-y and I alpha. Ten per segment. Thirty for the limb. That number is the reason for everything that follows. Three equations per segment against ten quantities only works because most of them arrive known — from the table, from the markers, from the plate, or from the segment below. Two quick examples next, one linear and one angular, then the real thing.

Dynamic Analysis

Example Problem #1 (inverse dynamics)

What is the resultant force applied to a 23 kg object if the acceleration is 17.6 m/s²?

Solution

ΣFx = m·ax

F = 23(17.6)

F = 404.8 N

Click through it a line at a time with them: the equation, then the substitution, then the answer. Thirty seconds. One equation, one substitution. The only thing worth saying is that we are running it backwards from the usual school problem: the acceleration was the measurement and the force is the answer. That is the whole idea of inverse dynamics in one line.

Dynamic Analysis

Example Problem #1 (inverse dynamics)

Calculate the moment required for an angular acceleration of 112 rad/s², given the moment of inertia about the elbow is 0.18 kg·m².

Solution

ΣM = I·α

M = 0.18(112)

M = 20.2 N·m

Same click-through. Ask them which equation before you reveal it. The angular twin of the previous slide, and the first time they see a moment of inertia used in anger. Worth one sentence: 0.18 kg·m² is a real forearm, 112 rad/s² is a brisk elbow flexion, and 20 N·m is what a joint has to produce to do it. Those are the orders of magnitude for the rest of the lecture.

The method

Break the limb into segments, start at the one touching the ground, and carry the answer upward.

Inverse Dynamics

Inverse dynamics in human gait analysis

  • We can record the movement of the human body using a motion capture system, video camera, accelerometers, gyros, etc…
  • and measure the external forces,
  • but we do not know what is going on inside the body to produce the motion.

Based on recorded data (motion trajectories, GRFs) and simple dynamics principles (Newton–Euler equations) we can reconstruct the net joint moments and forces.

The word net is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and we come back to it before the end.

This is the sales pitch for the whole method. Cameras and a plate on the outside, joint moments on the inside, and nothing invasive anywhere. Flag the word "net" now and promise to return to it. Students hear "the moment at the knee" and picture a muscle. It is the sum of everything crossing that joint.

Inverse Dynamics

One segment at a time, starting at the bottom

  • Inverse dynamics involves taking into account all the forces and moments that act on each segment, and the effect of these forces and moments on the following segments.
  • For example, if we want to find out the forces and moments at the hip during a movement we need to consider each segment separately.
  • We need to start with the most distal segment and then carry the solutions for each joint forward.

Step 1: Foot  ·  Step 2: Leg  ·  Step 3: Thigh

Two ideas, and the second is the one that makes the rest feel inevitable rather than arbitrary: the order is forced. Point at the cut ends. Each one carries a force AND a moment to the segment above, and that is the only channel between them. Solve the foot, hand the ankle up; solve the leg, hand the knee up. Do not justify the order yet — the next figure does that with a ledger of unknowns. Just assert it here and let them wonder why.

Inverse Dynamics

Why the order is forced

Walk the three steps in order. Each one shows the segment's free body, what is known at its two ends, and the ledger of unknowns against equations. Step 1, the foot: the distal end is the ground and a plate has measured it, so three unknowns against three equations. Solve it. Step 2, the leg: its distal end is the ankle, which step 1 just produced. Three again. Step 3, the thigh: distal end is the knee, from step 2. The phrase to leave them with: inverse dynamics runs from the ground up because the ground is the only place where anything is known.

Inverse Dynamics

A net joint force and a net joint moment

1. We need to consider all the forces acting upon the segment and the moments that they would produce — bone-on-bone forces, ligament forces, every muscle crossing the joint and the joint moment of force it produces.

2. Really we can say that we can add all these forces to create a net joint force and a net joint moment, and then account for the internal and external moments.

One force and one moment, at each joint, for each segment. That replacement is exact for the mechanics — the segment accelerates identically — and lossy for the anatomy, because afterwards there is no way of telling which muscle did it.

Walk the left-hand figure slowly. Every one of those arrows is real and none of them is measurable. Then the right-hand one: all of them added into a single force at the joint centre and a single couple. That is what inverse dynamics solves for, and it is the whole reason the method is tractable. The exactness point is worth saying twice. Nothing is approximated by the replacement. What is lost is attribution, not mechanics.

The forces, in general form

Read the top line first and name the three pieces: the moment at the ankle itself, the moment the ankle force makes about the centre of mass, and the moment the ground reaction force makes about the centre of mass. Then expand. Each force contributes two terms because each force has two components, and each component turns the segment through its own arm. dP is the distance from the centre of mass to the proximal joint; dD to the distal end; theta is the segment's angle. Hover each term as you read it. The pairing is the thing students get backwards: Fx is paired with dP sin theta, which is the VERTICAL offset. The arm a force turns through is perpendicular to it.

Same equation, with the arms resolved. This is the slide to point at when somebody asks why sine goes with x — and the hover does the pointing for you. Drop a vertical from the centre of mass to the level of the joint: that height is dP sin theta, and it is the arm that a horizontal force turns through. Go across instead and you get dP cos theta, which is the arm for a vertical force. Fx applied above the centre of mass turns the segment; applied level with it, it does not.

Inverse Dynamics

Every segment carries four moments

Straight off the last two slides, and it is worth the time. Take the terms one at a time with the buttons. The moment applied at the far end; the moment applied at the joint we are solving; the moment the far-end force makes about the centre of gravity; the moment the joint force makes about the centre of gravity. Four, and they add to I alpha. On the foot in swing two of them are zero — nothing is touching the far end at all. In stance the ground pushes but still does not twist, so the moment at the far end stays zero and the other three are large and nearly cancel. That missing term is exactly why the chain can start at the foot and nowhere else. Then switch to the leg, where all four are present, and point at the second one: that is the one we solve, every time, all the way up.

The leg, with specific forces

In this example we can give specific forces.

ΣFx = Fknee-x − Fankle-x = max(leg)

Need to consider action/reaction forces. Those minus signs are the whole content of this slide.

The step everyone drops. What the foot's free body produced was the force the shank applies to the foot. What the shank feels at its lower end is that force reversed. The leg, with specific moments

Inverse Dynamics

In summary…

What do you need to remember from the last few slides? In order to solve for joint forces and moments:

  • You need to know the kinematics of the movement and any external forces.
  • Break up each multi-limb segment into individual limbs.
  • Use the Newton–Euler equations to solve for unknown net forces and moments for each joint, starting from the most distal segment.
  • Transfer forces and moments up the link of segments.

This gives you an idea of the forces and moments experienced at each joint that result from the external forces and the movements.

Pause here and let them write it down. Four lines, and it is the whole method. If time is short, this is the slide to stop on. Everything after it is one worked example and then an honest look at what the answer can be trusted to say.

Inverse Dynamics

What inverse dynamics can and cannot tell us

What it can tell us

  • NET joint moments, powers and forces.

What it cannot tell us

  • Contributions/activations of individual muscles.
  • Total amount of muscle work done — it does not account for co-contraction of muscles.
  • Internal bone-to-bone forces at joints.

Other assumptions and limitations

  • Frictionless joints.
  • Generalized, uniform and/or concentrated mass distribution.
  • Estimation of joint centre of rotation.
  • Measurement errors: skin and motion artifacts, temporal or spatial misalignment between force plates and motion capture.
This is the slide that keeps the method honest, and it is worth more than it looks. Net is the word. A knee moment of 40 N·m does not mean one muscle pulling. It means everything crossing the joint summed to 40 N·m, and a co-contracting pair could be producing hundreds of newtons each while the sum stays small. Keep the limitations list in mind for the last part of the lecture — we run the method on a real stride, compare it with a three-dimensional solution of the same data, and every item on this list is in the difference.

Those two distances are the ones to flag as suspect — 0.01 and 0.02 metres make a moment arm of 22 millimetres on a foot nineteen centimetres long, and the slide after the widget takes that apart.

Inverse Dynamics Analysis Example

The foot, the ankle moment

Mankle + Fankle-xdPx + Fankle-ydPy = Iα

Based on the direction of the forces you can determine whether they will cause a positive or a negative moment. Therefore:

Mankle − Fankle-xdPx − Fankle-ydPy = Iα

Mankle = Fankle-xdPx + Fankle-ydPy + Iα

Mankle = (10.52 N)(0.01 m) + (3.70 N)(0.02 m) + (0.00995 kg·m²)(21.63 rad/s²)

Mankle = 0.105 N·m + 0.074 N·m + 0.2152 N·m

Mankle = 0.39 N·m

This positive torque denotes an ankle dorsiflexor moment.

The sign line is the whole slide. Read the first equation, then say that both of those forces turn the foot the other way from the moment we are solving for, so both change sign, and then rearrange. Then the arithmetic, one click at a time, and stop on the sizes: 0.105 and 0.074 from the two forces, 0.215 from I alpha. More than half the ankle moment in swing is the foot's own angular acceleration. In stance that term is invisible — we measured it, and it is six hundredths of one per cent. Point out what 0.39 newton metres is: almost nothing. A foot weighs eight tenths of a kilogram and nothing is touching it.

First solve for the moment caused by the forces at the knee about the CofG. x will cause a negative moment, y will cause a positive one.

MFknee/CG = − Fknee-xdPx + Fknee-ydPy

Same pairing as the foot, one segment up. Horizontal force through the vertical offset, vertical force through the horizontal offset. The signs are read off the figure, not remembered: the knee force in x pushes the top of the segment forward, which turns it one way; the knee force in y pushes it up, which turns it the other. Have them point before you tell them. Two terms, nearly cancelling, and the survivor is 2.14 newton metres.

The leg, the knee moment

Really just remember that what we solved for at the ankle will affect each limb up the chain.

This is the four-moment slide arriving with numbers in it, so say that: term one is the ankle moment coming up reversed, term two is the knee moment we are solving, terms three and four are the two joint forces about the centre of mass. Four, and they add to I alpha. The ankle contributed 0.39 of the 1.72. Small, but a fifth of the answer, and dropping it is the mistake the previous widget was about. Then stop. The same method, run for real

The chain from slide 18, the four moments from slide 25, all sixty-nine frames, m and I off Dempster’s table.

It is not a timing offset between the camera and the plate — the best fit is two milliseconds. It is not the centre of pressure — shifting it helps push-off and ruins early stance. It is not one joint centre — the ankle wants to move forwards and the knee wants to move backwards, so it is not a registration error either. What is left is every item on the limitations slide: a cadaver-derived anthropometric table, joint centres estimated from markers on skin, two dimensions instead of three. That is the honest size of the disagreement between two reasonable pipelines on identical data. The thing to leave them with: a joint moment is not a measurement. It is a calculation, and two careful people can differ by fifteen per cent.

On Real Data

When do you actually need the dynamics?

Slide 1 promised the only edit was zeros becoming ma and Iα. Here is that promise measured.

Back to the first two slides of the lecture, now with a number on them. Take the full Newton-Euler solution of this stride and set every ma and I alpha term to zero — that is, treat each segment as if it were in equilibrium. At the ankle in stance, the push-off peak moves from 1.738 to 1.737 N·m/kg. Six hundredths of one per cent. The foot weighs eight hundred grams and the ground is pushing on it with 678 newtons, so the inertial terms cannot matter there, and they do not. Then press "The knee" and show the other half. Over stance the two differ by a fifth of the peak. Through swing the equilibrium answer has the wrong sign for most of the phase, because there is no external force at all and the segment's own acceleration is the only thing acting. So the rule is not "dynamics always matters". It is: the inertial terms matter when the external forces are small and the segment is moving. Which is exactly the case the worked example chose, and it is why those numbers came out in fractions of a newton metre.

Dynamic Analysis

What to take away

  • Dynamic analysis is static analysis with ma and Iα on the right. The diagram, the table and the conventions are unchanged.
  • Three equations means three unknowns, which is why you start at the segment touching the ground and work upward.
  • Four moments act on every segment, and they add to Iα: one at each end, and one about the centre of mass from each end’s force.
  • Both the force and the moment reverse at every joint on the way up.
  • Take moments about each segment’s own centre of mass, and pair each force with the arm perpendicular to it.
  • A joint moment is a calculation, not a measurement, and it is only as good as the anatomy you fed it.
The six lines they should be able to reproduce cold. If you only have time to say one thing, say the last one. It is the difference between a student who can do the arithmetic and one who can read a paper.

Dynamic Analysis

Where this goes next

Multiply a joint moment by the joint’s angular velocity and you get joint power — how fast that joint is putting energy into the body or taking it out. That one multiplication turns this lecture into the standard clinical description of walking.

And the honest limitation remains: inverse dynamics tells you what the joint did, never which muscle did it. Getting from a net moment to individual muscle forces needs either a measurement we cannot make, or a forward model and an assumption about what the nervous system is trying to optimise.

All done.

One forward look before closing. Joint power is moment times angular velocity, and it is what every clinical gait report plots underneath the moments. The ankle generates a large burst of power at push-off; the knee mostly absorbs. One multiplication away from everything in this lecture. And close on the limitation, because it is the honest end of the arc: this method is how we know almost everything we know about joint loading, and it still cannot name a single muscle.
