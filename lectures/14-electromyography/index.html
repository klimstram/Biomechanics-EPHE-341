Electromyography

The electrical signal a contracting muscle leaves behind, and what it will and will not tell you

Everything so far in this course has been mechanics: forces, moments, velocities. This lecture is the one place we look at the signal the nervous system actually sends, and at how much of it survives the trip to an electrode on the skin. The honest summary of the whole lecture is this: EMG tells you about timing very well, about neural strategy quite well, and about force only with a great deal of care.

Electromyography

By the end of this lecture you should be able to…

  • Describe the physiological basis of the EMG signal, from motor unit to electrode
  • Explain why EMG is recorded as the difference between two electrodes
  • Choose where to put electrodes, and say what goes wrong at the innervation zone and the tendon
  • Process a raw signal: band-pass, rectify, RMS or linear envelope, and normalise
  • Relate EMG to force, allowing for electromechanical delay and for the fact that the relationship is not proportional
  • Interpret amplitude and frequency changes during fatigue, and know how reliable each one is
Six objectives, and the last two are the ones students most often get wrong in a lab report. EMG is not a force meter, and the median frequency does not always fall.

Electromyography

What the word means

Electro

Myo

Graphy
representation, writing, description

A visual or numeric representation of the electrical activity associated with muscle contraction.

We record, analyse and interpret these signals to understand how the nervous system controls muscle — in health and in disease.

Worth saying out loud at the start: EMG is a representation. It is not the muscle’s command, and it is not the muscle’s force. It is a voltage, measured at a distance, through tissue, by two electrodes that cannot tell which muscle a given potential came from.

Electromyography

What we need to know

  1. Whole muscle structural, physiological and neural organisation
  2. The electrochemical mechanism of contraction
  3. The resulting contraction and the concurrent electrical signal collected through EMG electrodes

Three steps, and the signal at the end of them is a long way from the command at the start. Most of the care in using EMG is about remembering what happened in between.

This is the roadmap for the first third of the lecture. We go down to the sarcomere and back up to the electrode, and then everything after that is about what the electrode can and cannot see.

Look at soleus — 86 per cent type I at the surface, 89 per cent deep. Now look at biceps brachii: 42 per cent surface, 50 per cent deep. A surface electrode samples the superficial fibres preferentially, so for some muscles the signal is not representative of the whole muscle at all.

Electromyography

Neural organisation of muscle

  • Each muscle is made up of many units of contraction called motor units
  • A motor unit is a motor nerve and all the muscle fibres it innervates
  • A descending command that excites the motor nerve contracts all the fibres in that unit, together — an all-or-none response

The motor unit, not the fibre, is the smallest thing the nervous system can switch on. It is therefore the smallest thing EMG can see.

All-or-none is the key idea. There is no such thing as half a motor unit firing. Force is graded by how many units are on and how fast each one repeats — not by any one of them contracting harder.

Electromyography

Motor units come in sizes

  • Motor units can be small (few fibres) or large (many fibres)
  • Within any one motor unit, all fibres are of the same type (I, IIa, IIb)
  • Most small units are type I; most large units are type II
Size and type travel together. That is what makes the recruitment order useful rather than arbitrary — ordering by size also orders by fatigue resistance.

Electromyography

Recruitment, and the Henneman size principle

  • The nervous system grades contraction by altering how many units are recruited and how often they fire
  • At low firing rates, small units are recruited first; at higher rates the larger units join in
  • Force is increased by adding more active motor units

Orderly recruitment from small to large is the Henneman size principle. It gives smooth, non-jerky force production and leans on the fatigue-resistant units first.

Note the step heights in the lower panel. Unit one adds a little, unit three adds a lot. That asymmetry is the whole reason the EMG–force relationship is not a straight line, and the next slide lets you see it happen.

Electromyography

One electrode, many motor units

Drive the pool up from nothing. At low drive you can pick out individual motor unit action potentials — that is what an indwelling electrode sees. By about 30 per cent they have merged into an interference pattern and you can no longer count anything. Now the right-hand panel. The trains are statistically independent, so they add in power, not amplitude: equal units would give amplitude growing as the square root of their number. The two sliders are the assumptions that decide which way the curve bends — how much louder a big unit is than a small one, and how far up the range recruitment continues. Push them to the corners and you can produce a curve above the diagonal or below it. The lesson is not that EMG–force is concave or convex. It is that the shape is a consequence, not a law, which is why published relationships disagree.

Electromyography

The electrochemical mechanism of contraction

  • Neural signals travel along the motor axon and disperse through the fibres it innervates
  • The signal propagates by the opening of voltage-gated sodium channels in the neurolemma and the sarcolemma

That propagating change in membrane potential is the thing EMG measures. Everything downstream of it — calcium, crossbridges, force — is invisible to the electrode.

This is the point in the lecture to be very clear about what EMG is a measure of. It is the electrical event. It is not the calcium, it is not the crossbridge cycling, and it is not the force.

Electromyography

The action potential

Two features matter for us. First, the whole event lasts a few milliseconds — that sets the frequency content of EMG, which is why we band-pass between about 20 and 450 hertz. Second, the refractory period puts a ceiling on firing rate, which is why rate coding runs out and recruitment has to take over.

Electromyography

Excitation–contraction coupling

Movement of the structural components of muscle is produced through the propagation of the action potential within the muscle, by a process called excitation–contraction coupling.

This is where the delay comes from. The electrical event finishes in a few milliseconds; the force takes tens of milliseconds to appear.

Hold on to the fact that the A band stays constant. The filaments do not shorten; they slide. We will come back to this delay when we get to electromechanical delay — it is the physical reason force lags the signal.

Electromyography

Contraction, and a signal we can measure

We know the contraction of muscle is tied to an electrical event, so we can try to find a way to measure that electrical event.

Electromyography means placing electrodes on or in the muscle and measuring the action potentials travelling along the muscle fibres.

muscle → electrodes → amplifier → A/D converter → computer

Note the chain. Every stage in it can add something that is not muscle, and the amplifier stage in particular is where most of the damage is either done or prevented.

Electromyography

Two ways of recording EMG

1. In-dwelling electrodes
fine wires or needles placed in the muscle

2. Surface electrodes
pairs of contacts placed on the skin over the muscle

The choice is not about quality, it is about what question you are asking. If you want to know what one motor unit is doing, you need a wire in the muscle. If you want to know when a whole muscle turns on during a movement, you want surface electrodes.

The second disadvantage is the one people forget. A single motor unit is a wonderfully clean signal and it tells you almost nothing about what the muscle as a whole is doing.

Electromyography

Surface electrodes

AdvantagesDisadvantages
easy to record only for superficial muscles
minimal discomfort to participants signal contaminated by cross-talk
indicates global muscle activity may affect the movement patterns of the subject

Surface EMG is the algebraic sum of all motor unit firings near the electrode at that instant. It is a crowd, not a voice.

That phrase, algebraic sum, is doing a lot of work. Algebraic means the potentials can cancel as well as add — two units firing with opposite polarity at the same instant give you less signal, not more. This is called amplitude cancellation and it is one reason EMG amplitude under-reports what the muscle is doing at high force.

Electromyography

The action potential travels past the electrodes

  • As action potentials travel along the muscle fibres, EMG electrodes — indwelling or surface — measure the change in voltage that accompanies their travel
  • The potential is generated at the motor end plate and propagates towards both tendons

Because it travels, where you put the electrodes decides what you record. That is the next figure.

Notice in the figure that the action potentials leave the junction in both directions. That detail is what makes the innervation zone a bad place to record, and it is the single most common electrode-placement mistake.

Electromyography

Where you put the pair, and what it costs you

Slide the pair along the fibre and watch the right-hand panel. Over the belly there is a broad plateau — move the sensor a centimetre and nothing much changes, which is what you want from a measurement. Now slide it onto the end plate. The two electrodes see mirror images of each other and the difference collapses to nothing. Either side of that null the amplitude spikes, and that spike is a trap: a few millimetres of sensor movement there changes your amplitude by a factor of two, so any comparison between days or between people becomes meaningless. At the tendon the travelling wave has run out of fibre and the signal fades. One honesty note: this is modelled. Our cycling dataset has no electrode array, so no conduction velocity anywhere in this lecture is measured.

Electromyography

One electrode is very sensitive to things that are not muscle

A problem with unipolar recording is that it will pick up anything that changes the potential at that point, not just muscle:

  • movement of the electrode or the cable
  • electronics — computers, lighting, anything on the mains
  • other muscles nearby

These are referred to collectively as noise.

Mains hum is the classic one — 60 hertz here, 50 in most of the world. It is everywhere, it is large, and a single electrode has no way to tell it apart from muscle.

Electromyography

Two electrodes, and a differential amplifier

  • Use a bipolar (two-pole) configuration and a differential amplifier
  • The signal and the noise are sampled at two points; subtract one from the other and what is common to both cancels

(m1 + n) − (m2 + n) = m1 − m2

The result, m1 − m2, is the motor unit action potential (MUAP) as recorded: the electrical activity of all the fibres in one motor unit, seen as a difference.

after Enoka 2002, Fig. 1.30

The algebra on this slide is the entire justification for bipolar recording, and it is worth noticing how strong the assumption is: n has to be identical at both electrodes. The next two slides test that assumption.

Electromyography

What the difference actually looks like

Electrode two sees the same potential as electrode one, delayed by the time the wave takes to cover the gap: tau equals d over v. Five milliseconds at the standard twenty-millimetre spacing and a typical four metres per second. Subtracting a delayed copy of a signal from itself is a comb filter — turn on the spectrum panel. It has zeros wherever a whole number of wavelengths fits into that delay, so at twenty millimetres and four metres per second the first zero lands at two hundred hertz, right in the middle of the EMG band. Narrow the spacing and the notch moves up and out of the way, which is one of the real reasons the SENIAM recommendation is twenty millimetres or less. Now turn up the mains hum. It is identical at both electrodes so it vanishes exactly, which is the whole point. Then turn up the crosstalk source.

Electromyography

What differencing removes — and what it does not

Removed. Anything genuinely common to both electrodes: mains hum, and any distant source far enough away that it arrives at both contacts identically. The cancellation is exact.

Not removed. Cross-talk. A neighbouring muscle is nearer one electrode than the other, so it does not arrive identically and does not cancel. In the figure opposite, a source sitting just a few millimetres off centre survives the subtraction at about a quarter of the amplitude of the signal you wanted.

So a clean-looking trace is not evidence that you are recording the muscle you think you are. Differential amplification solves the noise problem. It does not solve the anatomy problem — that is what electrode placement is for.

This slide exists because the algebra two slides ago is so persuasive that students conclude subtraction fixes everything. It fixes exactly one class of problem. Crosstalk is a different class and no amount of amplifier quality will touch it. If you want to know whether you have crosstalk, the test is anatomical and behavioural: get the participant to contract the neighbouring muscle in isolation and see whether your channel moves.

Electromyography

Every motor unit has its own signature, and they all add up

  • Each motor unit has its own characteristic MUAP, set by the number, type and position of its fibres relative to the electrode
  • Very rarely is one motor unit firing alone — the electrode records the sum of many units firing at different times
The shape of a given unit’s MUAP is essentially an accident of geometry — how many fibres, how deep, how far along the fibre. Two units of identical size can look completely different at the electrode.

Electromyography

The interference pattern

The surface EMG signal is therefore a summation of all the MUAPs near the electrode, which gives a gross approximation of the activity of the whole muscle — useful for timing, rate of force development and fatigue.

Once the units overlap you cannot count them any more. That is why surface EMG answers “when” and “roughly how much” rather than “which units”.

Look at the far left of that trace, circled. You can see individual potentials. By the right-hand end you cannot see anything but a band. Nothing has gone wrong — that is simply what summation looks like, and it is the normal state of a surface recording.

Electromyography

Henneman, seen on the electrode

During a contraction the small motor units are normally recruited first and the larger ones after — orderly recruitment from small to large is the Henneman size principle. It gives smooth force production and preferentially uses the non-fatigable units, so the muscle resists fatigue.

In the left-hand trace you can see it happening: a small unit fires alone for a while, then a visibly bigger one joins it. That is recruitment, caught on a surface electrode, which only happens at very low force.

Electromyography

Some other important considerations

  • Electrode placement
    • the neuromuscular junction — the motor end plate
  • EMG signal processing
  • Electromechanical delay

The rest of the lecture is these three, in that order.

Three things, and each of them can wreck a result on its own. Placement decides what you record, processing decides what you report, and electromechanical delay decides whether your timing conclusions are right.

Electromyography

Orienting the electrodes on the muscle

  • Orient the electrodes so the MUAPs travel parallel to the line joining them — along the fibres, not across them
  • Place them on the muscle belly, and not on the innervation zone (the motor end plates)

Across the fibres there is no delay between the two contacts, so the difference has nothing to work with and the signal collapses — the same arithmetic as sitting on the innervation zone.

Both failures are the same failure. The bipolar amplifier works by exploiting a time difference between the two contacts, so anything that removes the time difference removes the signal — whether that is sitting on the end plate or lining the pair up across the fibres instead of along them.

Electrode placement summary

  • On the muscle belly
  • Avoid the tendon and the lateral edge of the muscle
  • Align the electrodes parallel with the muscle fibres
  • Mind the motor point or innervation zone
    • place on the midline of the belly, midway between the tendon and the nearest innervation zone
    • avoid placing directly over the motor point

Electromyography

Standard placements — SENIAM

You do not have to work the placement out from first principles every time. SENIAM (Surface EMG for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles) publishes an agreed position and orientation for each muscle.

www.seniam.org

Using a standard placement is what makes your numbers comparable with anyone else’s — including your own, next week.

This is also the answer to “where exactly do I put it” in the lab. Look it up, follow it, and write down which placement you used.

Electromyography

Setting up — preparing the skin

  • Make sure the site is properly cleaned and prepared
  • Tissue — skin and subcutaneous fat — acts as a filter
  • Clean the skin with alcohol
  • Shave excess hair
  • Lightly abrade the skin
  • Use electrode gel, paste or cream

Skin impedance is the one part of the signal chain you can improve for free, before you record anything.

Tissue acting as a low-pass filter is why a deeper muscle looks smoother than a superficial one, and why body composition changes the amplitude you record from the same muscle in two different people. It is also another reason amplitude must be normalised before it is compared.

Electromyography

Why process the signal at all

  • EMG is an indirect measure of muscle force
  • It also carries neural information — which motor units fire and when
  • How we process it decides what we can read from it:
    • timing of muscle activity
    • force
    • fatigue
Notice in that figure how closely the processed EMG tracks the force, and then notice that it is not the same shape — it rises earlier and falls earlier. Both of those are coming up.

Electromyography

Timing: onset and offset

  • The raw EMG can be used without any processing to tell us about timing — when the muscle turns on and off
  • Processing usually improves the result, but it is not required for timing

Normalisation. Amplitude must be made relative before it can be compared between people or conditions:
normalised EMG = EMG ÷ (max EMG), usually from an MVC or MVIC.

Onset detection is deceptively hard. The usual rule is some number of standard deviations above the resting baseline, held for some minimum duration, and both of those thresholds change the answer. Whatever you choose, say what it was. And normalisation: raw millivolts from my biceps and raw millivolts from yours are not comparable quantities. Skin, fat, electrode position and amplifier gain all differ.

Electromyography

Full-wave rectification

The most common way to process EMG is to produce an average value associated with its magnitude. Two steps:

  1. Take the absolute value of the signal — full-wave rectification (FWR)
  2. Take an average over some time period

Step one is not optional. Raw EMG is zero-mean by construction, so averaging it without rectifying gives you nothing but zero.

Zero-mean is worth dwelling on. It is not an accident of the recording, it is forced by the amplifier: a differential, AC-coupled amplifier cannot pass a DC level. So the mean of the raw signal is zero no matter how hard the muscle is working.

Electromyography

The processing chain, on a real recording

This is two seconds of vastus lateralis recorded at 2222 hertz during steady cycling — three pedal strokes. Everything you see is applied to the measured trace live. Walk the chips left to right. The band-pass takes out the low-frequency movement artefact you can see wandering in the raw trace. Rectification flips the negative half up. Then either a moving RMS or a low-pass filter gives you the envelope. Two things to play with. Drag the RMS window and watch smoothness trade directly against timing — there is no setting that gives you both. Then switch the zero-phase chip off: filtering once, forwards only, pushes the envelope eighty milliseconds late. That is larger than the electromechanical delay we are about to discuss, so if you filter in one direction and then measure a delay, you are mostly measuring your own filter.

Electromyography

Root mean square

Taking the root mean square is a way of taking an average magnitude of the EMG signal. It can be used over different time windows.

RMS = √( 1/T · ∫tT EMG(t)² dt )

Squaring removes the sign, so RMS does not need a separate rectification step — and unlike the rectified mean it has a physical meaning: it is the signal’s power.

RMS is the better of the two averages precisely because independent motor unit trains add in power. That makes RMS the quantity that behaves predictably as more units join in — which is exactly what we saw in the recruitment widget.

Electromyography

The linear envelope

Another way to average is to low-pass filter the rectified signal. The resulting tracing is called a linear envelope.

The filter can be set to be more or less sensitive. A lower cut-off is smoother and slower; a higher cut-off follows the bursts more closely and is noisier.

Filter forwards and backwards (zero-phase) or the envelope comes out late — and you will read that lag as physiology.

Second-order Butterworth at two or three hertz is the usual choice for whole-body movement. Higher-order filters ring, and on a rectified signal — which cannot be negative — the ringing shows up as an envelope that dips below zero, which is a giveaway that the filter is wrong rather than the muscle.

Electromyography

EMG and force — the relationship is not a straight line

  • There is a non-linear relationship between EMG and muscle force, even during isometric contractions
  • During fatigue the relationship changes
  • During dynamic contractions it becomes more complex still — length and velocity are changing, and so is the tissue between muscle and electrode

We derived the reason earlier: independent trains add in power, and the units are not all the same size. The shape of the curve follows from recruitment, not from a law of nature.

The lower figure is the one to look at twice. Each loop is a different minute of a fatiguing protocol, and the relationship moves. So even if you calibrate EMG against force at the start of a session, that calibration is already wrong by the end of it.

Electromyography

Electromechanical delay

  • The delay between neural activation of a muscle and the development of muscle tension
  • Approximately 30–50 ms, depending on the muscle and the joint
  • It comes from excitation–contraction coupling and from taking up slack in the series elastic elements

Relating EMG to force or to movement without allowing for this will misplace both.

Notice the figure marks a delay at the start and at the end, and that they are not the same size. Switching a muscle off takes longer than switching it on, which the next slide lets you see.

Electromyography

Why force arrives after the signal

The green trace is the measured envelope from the same cycling recording. The orange is modelled force, driven by that envelope through first-order activation dynamics. Two separate things add up. The electromechanical delay slider is the propagation and slack-taking part. On top of it, activation itself takes time: at thirty milliseconds of delay the measured onset-to-onset gap comes out at about thirty-nine, and that extra nine milliseconds is the activation climbing past the detection threshold. Now look at the trailing edges rather than the leading ones. Deactivation is the slower of the two time constants, so force outlasts the signal that caused it by considerably more than it lagged at the start. If you measure electromechanical delay at onset and assume the same number applies at offset, you will be wrong.

Electromyography

Fatigue — what the textbook says

  • A single MUAP decreases in amplitude with fatigue, and produces less force
  • Surface EMG of a submaximal contraction increases in amplitude as fatigue develops, because more motor units are recruited to hold the same force
  • The frequency content shifts down: conduction velocity falls, so each MUAP is stretched out in time and the spectrum is compressed

Two predictions: amplitude up, median frequency down. The next slide tests both against measured data.

The mechanism for the frequency shift is worth stating because it is not arbitrary. Metabolite accumulation slows conduction velocity; a slower wave takes longer to pass the electrode; a longer waveform has lower frequency content. Median frequency is just a summary of where the power sits.

Electromyography

…and what six real sprints show

This is vastus lateralis through a thirty-second all-out effort, six participants, one point per pedal revolution. Per revolution rather than per second, because during cycling the muscle is only active for part of each turn and a one-second window is mostly silence. Click through the participants. Amplitude rises in every one of the six, strongly in several. Median frequency falls in only four, and in P04 and P05 it goes the other way. So the amplitude prediction holds up and the frequency prediction is a tendency. That is not a failure of the physiology — the mechanism is real — it is a statement about dynamic contractions, where the muscle is intermittently active, the detection volume moves under the skin as the limb changes shape, and thirty seconds is simply not very long. The classic result comes from sustained isometric holds, and that is the context where you should expect to see it. If you report a median-frequency slope in a lab, report the correlation too.

Electromyography

What the signal can be analysed for

  • Coordination
    • sequencing and co-contraction
  • Force
    • linear and non-linear relations — with care
  • Fatigue
    • decrease in the single MUAP
    • increase in surface EMG during a fatiguing contraction
    • change in the frequency content
Ordered by how much you can trust them. Coordination and timing are what surface EMG does well. Force needs the whole chain of caveats we have just been through. Fatigue sits in between: the amplitude change is dependable, the frequency change is conditional.

Electromyography

What to take away

  • EMG is the electrical event, not the force. Everything between the action potential and the crossbridge is invisible to the electrode.
  • It is a sum, and an algebraic one. Potentials cancel as well as add, so amplitude under-reports at high force.
  • Bipolar recording removes what is common. Mains hum goes exactly; cross-talk is not common to both electrodes and stays.
  • Spacing is a filter. Differencing two contacts d apart has zeros at multiples of v/d — 20 mm at 4 m/s notches 200 Hz, inside the band.
  • Placement is arithmetic, not folklore. On the end plate the difference cancels; at the tendon the wave runs out; the belly is the stable plateau.
  • Process deliberately. Rectify because the raw mean is zero; filter forwards and backwards or you will read your own lag as electromechanical delay.
  • Normalise before comparing. Raw millivolts are not comparable between people, days, or electrode placements.
  • Amplitude rises with fatigue; frequency usually falls. “Usually” is the honest word — 4 of our 6 sprinters, against 6 of 6 for amplitude.
If a student leaves with only one of these, make it the first. Almost every misuse of EMG in the literature is a version of treating it as a force meter.
