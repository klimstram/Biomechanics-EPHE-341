McGinnis Chpt 4

Work, Energy and Power

By the end of this lecture you should be able to…

  • State how work, energy and power are defined and how the three are related
  • Distinguish kinetic, gravitational and elastic energy, and add them
  • Apply the work–energy theorem and conservation of mechanical energy
  • Separate positive from negative work, and generation from absorption
  • Calculate joint power as the product of moment and angular velocity
  • Interpret the stretch–shortening cycle and gait in energetic terms
The order of this lecture is deliberate. The first part builds the whole framework — all three quantities and all three forms of energy — before we touch a single problem. The second part is nothing but problems. The third is where it shows up in the body. Tell them that up front. The temptation in this material is to start calculating immediately, and students then end up with four formulas and no idea which one answers the question in front of them.

Two ways to describe the same motion

Nothing here is new physics — it is a change of bookkeeping

FORCE AND MOMENTUM — what you have already seen

  • Newton's second law: ΣF = m·a
  • Impulse–momentum: F·Δt = Δ(m·v)
  • Force accumulated over time
  • Answers: how fast is it going?

WORK AND ENERGY — what we add today

  • Work: force accumulated over distance
  • Energy: the capacity to do work
  • Power: the rate at which work is done
  • Answers: how high, how far, how much did it cost?

Both routes start from the same force record. Integrate it over time and you get velocity. Integrate it over distance and you get energy. Choose whichever one matches what the question gives you.

Be explicit that this is a choice of tool and not a new law. Everything in this lecture could be done with forces and impulse; it would just be much harder. The rule of thumb at the end of the callout is the practical one: if the problem hands you a time, reach for impulse. If it hands you a distance or a height, reach for work.

How the three fit together

The whole lecture on one slide — click any box

Spend real time here. This is the map the rest of the lecture fills in, and it is worth coming back to it whenever someone loses the thread. The three sentences to say out loud: WORK is the transaction — a force acting through a distance. ENERGY is the account balance — the capacity to do work, and it comes in three mechanical forms that simply add. POWER is the rate — how fast the transaction happens. Work is how energy moves from one place to another. It is not a kind of energy; it is the transfer. That distinction is the one students lose, and it is why they write things like "the body has 200 J of work". Click each box to bring up its definition and units. Everything on this map gets its own slide over the next twenty minutes.

Work

A force acting through a displacement

W = F · d · cos θ

SI unit

joule (J)
1 J = 1 N·m

A scalar

No direction of its own. The sign comes entirely from cos θ.

θ

The angle between the force and the displacement — not the ground.

No displacement means no work. Holding a barbell overhead is exhausting and mechanically zero.

The cos θ is the part students drop. Emphasise that θ is between the force and the DISPLACEMENT, not between the force and the ground or the force and some convenient axis. The diagram is worth pointing at: the force is applied at an angle, the block moves horizontally, and only the horizontal component of that force is doing anything. The vertical component is pressing the block into the floor and contributing nothing. Ask about the barbell before revealing the callout. Most of the class will say work is being done because it is hard.

The sign of work

All three of F, d and θ are drawn to scale — move any of them

Every slider changes the picture, not just the number. The force arrow's LENGTH is F, the gap between the ghost block and the solid one is d, and the filled part of the bar is the work on a fixed ±2400 J scale — so growing either F or d grows the bar, and the two tick marks show ±F·d, the most this force over this distance could possibly do. Drag the angle from 0° through 90° to 180°. At 0° the force and the displacement agree and the work is positive; at 90° there is no component along the motion at all and the fill vanishes between the two ticks; past 90° it is negative. The 90° case is worth dwelling on. The normal force on a sledge, or the tension in a string whirling a ball, do no work at all — they are perpendicular to the motion the whole way.

Positive and negative work

The muscle is doing both, every step you take

POSITIVE WORK — θ < 90°

  • Force and motion in the same direction
  • The muscle shortens under load — concentric
  • Energy flows into the body segment
  • Pushing out of the bottom of a squat

NEGATIVE WORK — θ > 90°

  • Force opposes the motion
  • The muscle lengthens under load — eccentric
  • Energy flows out of the segment
  • Lowering into a squat, or landing

Negative work is not wasted work. It is how you stop, how you land, and — in a countermovement — how you load a tendon before you use it.

This is the slide that makes "negative work" stop sounding like a mistake. The same muscle group, the same load, the same joint — the only thing that changes is whether it is shortening or lengthening. Worth asking which one feels harder and which one actually costs more metabolically. Eccentric work feels easier and costs roughly a third as much oxygen for the same mechanical magnitude, which is why downhill walking is easy on the lungs and brutal on the muscle the following day. The sign convention: work done BY the muscle ON the segment. Keep that straight and the signs follow.

Internal and external work

Not all of the work a body does moves the body

EXTERNAL WORK

  • Work done on objects or against the environment
  • Raising the centre of mass, lifting a load, overcoming drag
  • What a force plate or an ergometer measures

INTERNAL WORK

  • Work done moving the segments relative to the centre of mass
  • Swinging the legs and arms in running
  • Invisible to the force plate, but it still costs energy

A runner on a treadmill at constant speed raises their centre of mass by nothing over a full stride, so the external work is near zero — and yet they are clearly working hard. All of it is internal.

The treadmill example in the callout is the one that lands. Net external work over a stride is close to zero at steady speed on the level, and the runner is still burning 15 times their resting metabolic rate. This matters for interpreting the lab. When we compute work from a force plate we are getting external work on the centre of mass only. It is a real number and it is not the whole story.

Energy

The capacity to do work — and it comes in three mechanical forms

Emech = KE + Ug + Ue

KINETIC

KE = ½ · m · v²

Because it is moving

GRAVITATIONAL

Ug = m · g · h

Because of where it is

ELASTIC

Ue = ½ · k · x²

Because something is deformed

  • All three are scalars, measured in joules — the same unit as work
  • They simply add; there is no vector sum to do
  • Work is how energy moves between forms and between bodies
  • Energy is never created here, only shifted or dissipated
Same unit as work, and that is not a coincidence — energy is defined as the capacity to do work, so it has to be measured in the same currency. The three forms are an advance organiser. Each gets its own slide next, but seeing them side by side first means that when we reach conservation they already know what the three terms in the sum are. The last bullet is worth stating plainly: in this course energy is not created. Chemical energy from food becomes mechanical energy at the muscle, and we pick up the accounting from there.

Kinetic energy

The energy an object has because it is moving

KE = ½ · m · v²

  • A scalar, and always ≥ 0 — there is no such thing as negative kinetic energy
  • Mass enters once; velocity enters twice
  • Doubling the speed quadruples the energy. Halving the mass only halves it
  • This asymmetry is why injury severity scales so brutally with speed
The asymmetry between the two terms is the whole slide. Mass once, velocity squared. A useful question before moving on: a 70 kg sprinter at 10 m/s, or a 140 kg lineman at 5 m/s — which carries more kinetic energy? Same momentum, and the sprinter has twice the kinetic energy. That is the setup for the momentum-against-energy slide two on.

Kinetic energy

Doubling the velocity quadruples the energy

Drag the velocity slider and watch the two dropped lines: the grey one is at half the speed of the red one, and its energy is always a quarter, wherever you put them. That ratio is fixed by the square. Then drag the mass slider. Everything scales, but nothing about the SHAPE changes — mass is a multiplier, velocity is the curve. The shot put sits well below the curve because it carries its own mass of 7.26 kg rather than the body mass on the slider.

Momentum and energy are not the same thing

Double the speed: momentum doubles, kinetic energy quadruples

v = 5 m/sv = 10 m/schange
Momentum p = m·v350 kg·m/s700 kg·m/s×2
Kinetic energy ½mv²875 J3500 J×4
  • Momentum tells you what it takes to stop it in a given time
  • Kinetic energy tells you what it takes to stop it in a given distance
  • A 70 kg athlete at 10 m/s and a 140 kg athlete at 5 m/s have the same momentum — and the lighter, faster one carries twice the energy
This slide exists because students merge the two concepts, and the merge is encouraged by the fact that both are "how much motion there is". Make the distinction concrete with the braking question. Stopping in a given TIME is an impulse problem and scales with momentum. Stopping in a given DISTANCE is a work problem and scales with kinetic energy. That is why doubling your speed roughly doubles your reaction-time distance but quadruples your braking distance. That same quadrupling is the reason the helmet problem at the end of Part 02 has the numbers it does.

The work–energy theorem

Net work equals the change in kinetic energy

Wnet = ΔKE = ½mvf² − ½mvi²

WHAT IT SAYS

  • Wnet > 0 → the object speeds up
  • Wnet < 0 → it slows down
  • Wnet = 0 → the speed is unchanged

WHERE IT IS USED

  • Sprint acceleration: net forward work becomes kinetic energy
  • Braking at heel-strike: negative work removes it again
  • Throwing: work on the implement becomes its kinetic energy

It is a theorem, not a definition — it follows from ΣF = ma. And it is net work: every force that acts counts, friction and gravity included.

Derive it on the board if there is time: start from F = ma, use v² = v₀² + 2ad, multiply through by m/2 and the result falls out in three lines. Students trust it more once they have seen it come from somewhere. The word NET is the one that gets missed in problems. If a sprinter is accelerating against drag, the net work is the propulsive work minus the drag work, and it is only the net that appears as a change in speed.

The work–energy theorem

The area and the energy are the same number

Press play. The shaded area under the force–displacement line is the work done; the column on the right is the kinetic energy. They are equal at every instant of the run, not just at the end — that is what the theorem asserts. Then change the net force, or the mass, and run it again. A heavier runner gets the same work done on them but ends up slower, because the same energy is spread over more mass.

Gravitational potential energy

Energy stored in an object's vertical position

Ug = m · g · h

  • h is measured from a reference height that you choose — only changes in Ug are physically meaningful
  • Raising 1 kg by 1 m stores about 9.81 J
  • A change in Ug is minus the work done by gravity
  • Linear in height — unlike the other two, nothing is squared
The reference height is the bit worth labouring, and the next slide makes it a control you can move. Students often think there is a "correct" zero. There is not — floor level, the centre of mass at standing, and sea level are all legitimate, and every one of them gives a different U for the same body. What no choice can change is a DIFFERENCE. That is why every problem in this course is about ΔU. Worth noting this is the only one of the three that is linear. Kinetic and elastic are both quadratic, and that difference shows up in every problem where you ask what happens if you double something.

Gravitational potential energy

Move the reference and the number changes; the climb does not

Drag the reference slider. U swings from positive to negative without the climber moving at all. Then drag the height slider and note that Δh — the length of the green arrow — is what actually did anything. Set the reference above the climber to get a negative potential energy, which is a perfectly good answer and usually the moment someone objects.

Elastic potential energy

Energy stored in a deformed spring — or a tendon

Ue = ½ · k · x²

  • k is the stiffness in N/m, x the deformation from resting length
  • Double the stretch, four times the energy — same quadratic structure as KE
  • Tendons, ligaments and aponeuroses are all biological springs
  • Real tissue returns about 90–93% of what it stores; the rest is lost as heat
Same quadratic structure as kinetic energy, and the same lesson: the term that is squared dominates. A tendon that stretches twice as far stores four times as much, which is why a deep countermovement can store so much more than a shallow one. The horse is the clearest illustration in biology. There is essentially no muscle below the knee and hock — the limb is a set of springs, and the motors are all up at the trunk. That is a design for cheap, fast locomotion, and the human Achilles is the same idea in a less extreme form. The 90–93% return figure comes back in Part 03 as hysteresis.

Elastic potential energy

Stiffness and stretch do not contribute equally

Start on the elastic band and drag the stretch slider — the grey dot is always at half the stretch of the red one and always at a quarter of the energy. Then press the Achilles tendon chip. The stiffness jumps from 12 to 150 N/mm and the stretch drops from 40 mm to 18, and the stored energy goes UP, because stiffness is linear and stretch is squared but the tendon is more than twelve times stiffer. The vaulting pole chip is the extreme case — very compliant, enormous deflection, and it stores more than either.

Conservation of mechanical energy

When only gravity and springs act, the total does not change

KE + Ug + Ue = constant

  • The three terms trade back and forth, but their sum is flat
  • This holds only without friction, drag or muscle work
  • In the flight phase of a jump it is exactly true — nothing but gravity acts
  • During push-off it fails completely, because the muscles are pouring energy in
The condition is what matters. Once the feet leave the ground, nothing but gravity is acting and conservation is exact — that is the one place in biomechanics where this is genuinely clean. The vault is the best single illustration because all three terms are involved in sequence: the athlete sprints in with kinetic energy, plants the pole and stores much of it elastically, and the pole returns it as height. Ask where the energy is at three moments — mid run-up, maximum pole bend, at the bar. Honest caveat: a good vaulter also does substantial muscle work on the pole during the swing, so it is not purely a conversion. The clean conservation example is the flight phase of a jump.

Conservation of mechanical energy

Kinetic into potential and back again, through one flight

Press play. The blue region is kinetic energy, the red above it is potential, and the green line on top — their sum — never moves. That flat line is the whole idea. At the peak the kinetic energy is zero and every joule is potential. On landing it has all come back. Pause at the top and ask what the velocity is; "zero" is the answer they should be able to read straight off the figure.

Power

The rate at which work is done, or energy transferred

P = W / t

Time-average power

P = F · v

Instantaneous linear power

P = M · ω

Joint power — moment × angular velocity

  • SI unit: the watt — 1 W = 1 joule per second
  • These are one quantity written three ways, not three quantities
  • P = F·v follows from P = W/t: W = F·d, divide by t, and d/t is v
  • 1 horsepower ≈ 746 W; a fit cyclist holds about 300 W for an hour
Make the "one quantity, three expressions" point explicitly, and do the one-line derivation of P = F·v. Students treat these as three separate formulas to memorise and then pick the wrong one. M·ω is the form you will use for the rest of the course, from Angular Kinetics onward, and it is the one we come back to in Part 03 when we look at what individual joints are doing. Two people carrying the same load up the same stairs do identical work. The one who runs produces more power. That is the whole concept, and it is worth saying in those words.

Power

One lift, three formulas, the same watts

The curve is instantaneous power through the lift and the dashed line is the time-average up to the cursor. They are different numbers at every instant, and the average is always the smaller — worth pointing out, because "peak power" and "average power" get used interchangeably in the strength literature and they should not be. Press the M·ω button. Nothing about the lift changes; the same watts are simply being counted at a joint with a 0.42 m moment arm instead of at the bar.

The framework, in one table

Everything from here on is an application of these four lines

DefinitionUnitWhat it tells you
WorkW = F·d·cos θJ How much energy was transferred by a force acting through a distance
EnergyKE + Ug + UeJ How much capacity to do work a body holds, in three forms that add
Work–energyWnet = ΔKEJ Net work is the change in kinetic energy
PowerP = W/t = F·v = M·ωW How fast that transfer happens

Energy is the currency, work is the transaction, power is the rate. Every problem in the next section is asking where a fixed number of joules went.

Pause here. This is the end of the framework and the beginning of the arithmetic, and it is worth checking that everyone has the three-way distinction before moving on. A quick verbal check that works: "a 70 kg person runs up a flight of stairs. Which of these four lines do I use if you ask how much energy it took? Which if you ask how hard it was?" The first is work or ΔU_g, the second is power.
Part 02

Working problems

Eight of them, in the order the framework was built

Problem 1 — work at an angle

W = F · d · cos θ

A strongman competitor pulls a truck 100 m along a flat road. The harness rope runs up to the truck at 20° above horizontal, and he maintains a steady 2000 N of tension.

How much work does he do on the truck?

Given:

F = 2000 N

d = 100 m (horizontal)

θ = 20°

Ask for a guess before turning over, and ask specifically whether the answer is more or less than 200,000 J. Most will say the angle makes it bigger because the rope is longer. It makes it smaller, because only the horizontal component is going anywhere. Point at the dashed line on the diagram: that is the direction of motion. θ is measured from there to the rope, not from the ground to the rope — they happen to be the same here, which is worth saying out loud so it does not become a rule.

Problem 1 — solution

Only the component along the motion counts

W = F · d · cos θ

W = (2000 N)(100 m)(cos 20°)

W = (2000)(100)(0.940)

W ≈ 188,000 J = 188 kJ

  • At 0° — rope horizontal — it would have been the full 200 kJ
  • The missing 12 kJ was never lost; that component was lifting, not pulling, and the truck did not move upward
  • The vertical component, 2000·sin 20° = 684 N, does zero work over the whole 100 m
The second bullet pre-empts the usual objection — "where did the energy go?" Nowhere. Work is not something the force has and spends; it is a product of force and the displacement it actually produced. A vertical force over a horizontal displacement produces nothing. Sanity check worth doing out loud: 188 kJ is roughly the energy in a chocolate bar, which is a nice reminder of how inefficient we are. Pulling a truck 100 m costs the athlete several times that in metabolic terms.

Problem 2 — work against gravity

A lift at constant velocity

An athlete lifts a 100 kg barbell 2.1 m from the floor to overhead at a roughly constant velocity.

How much work does the athlete do on the bar?

Given:

m = 100 kg

d = 2.1 m (vertical)

a ≈ 0 (constant velocity)

θ = 0° (force and displacement both up)

Ask what force the athlete has to apply before showing the solution. "Constant velocity" is the key phrase — it means the acceleration is zero, so the lifting force is exactly the weight and no more. Students want to add something for "the effort of lifting". There is nothing to add. If it were accelerating we would need more; it is not.

Problem 2 — solution

And the first appearance of a conversion

  • At constant velocity ΣF = 0, so the lifting force equals the weight of the bar

F = m · g = (100 kg)(9.81 m/s²) = 981 N

  • The force and the displacement are both upward, so θ = 0° and cos θ = 1

W = F · d · cos(0°) = (981 N)(2.1 m)(1)

W ≈ 2060 J

Those 2060 J did not vanish — they are sitting in the bar's new position as gravitational potential energy, and you can get them back by dropping it. Wagainst gravity = ΔUg.

The callout is the whole reason this problem is second rather than first. It is the first worked example where the work done turns into a named form of stored energy, and it is the bridge to the next two problems. If anyone asks about the work done lowering it back down: that is negative work of the same magnitude, the muscles are lengthening, and the energy comes back out of the system as heat rather than being recovered.

Problem 3 — kinetic energy

And what happens when you change the speed

A 70 kg sprinter is running at 10 m/s.

(a) How much kinetic energy do they have?
(b) How much work was required to get them there from a standing start?
(c) What happens to (a) if they reach 12 m/s?

Given:

m = 70 kg

vi = 0, vf = 10 m/s

then vf = 12 m/s

Part (b) is the one to dwell on. The work–energy theorem says the net work is the change in kinetic energy — so once they have part (a) they already have part (b) and do not need to know anything about the force, the distance or the time. That is the payoff of the energy route, and it is worth naming: we just answered a question about a 40 m acceleration without knowing the force or the distance.

Problem 3 — solution

A 20% increase in speed costs 44% more energy

(a) at 10 m/s

KE = ½ · m · v² = ½(70)(10)²

KE = 3500 J

(b) starting from rest, vi = 0 so ΔKE is all of it

Wnet = ΔKE = 3500 − 0 = 3500 J

(c) at 12 m/s

KE = ½(70)(12)² = 5040 J

an extra 1540 J — 44% more

v (m/s)KE (J)relative
58750.25 ×
1035001.00 ×
1250401.44 ×
20140004.00 ×

The last 2 m/s cost almost half as much again as the first 10 m/s put together. That is the squared term, and it is why the final metres of a sprint are so expensive to buy.

Make them check the 44% rather than asserting it: 12² / 10² = 144/100. The ratio does not depend on the mass at all. This also explains a coaching observation they may have met — that improving a 10.5 s sprinter to 10.4 s is far harder than improving an 11.5 s sprinter by the same tenth. The energy required scales with the square of the speed, and the power required scales worse still.

Problem 4 — gravitational potential energy

Where does a vaulter's height come from?

A 70 kg pole vaulter raises their centre of mass to 6.0 m above its standing height.

(a) How much gravitational potential energy have they gained?
(b) How fast would they have to be running for their kinetic energy alone to supply it?

Given:

m = 70 kg

Δh = 6.0 m

g = 9.81 m/s²

Part (b) is the interesting half and it is a straight equate-the-two-energies problem: set ½mv² = mgΔh, cancel the mass, and solve. Before turning over, ask whether a sprinter can run that fast. The answer comes out just under 11 m/s, which is at or beyond world-class sprint speed — and that is the honest explanation of why vaulters do not clear 6 m on run-up speed alone. The take-off, the swing and the pull all add work.

Problem 4 — solution

And why vaulting is not purely a conversion

(a)

ΔUg = m · g · Δh = (70)(9.81)(6.0)

ΔUg ≈ 4120 J

(b) set the kinetic energy equal to it — the mass cancels

½ · m · v² = m · g · Δh

v = √(2 · g · Δh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 6.0)

v ≈ 10.8 m/s

10.8 m/s is faster than almost any vaulter runs — elite approach speeds are about 9.5 m/s, worth roughly 3160 J.

The missing 960 J is muscular work done during the plant, the swing and the pull. The pole is a very good spring, but it is not a free lunch.

  • Note the mass cancelled in (b) — this is the same cancellation that appears in every jump-height calculation
  • Nothing here depended on the shape of the vault, only on its two endpoints
The honesty in the callout matters. The textbook version of this problem implies the vault is a clean KE-to-PE conversion, and it is not — about a quarter of the height is bought with muscular work after take-off. The last bullet is the general lesson about energy methods: you only ever need the endpoints. We did not need to know anything about the pole, the trajectory, or how long it took.

Problem 5 — work and power together

The same climb, asked two ways

A 70 kg student climbs a staircase 3.0 m high.

(a) How much gravitational potential energy do they gain?
(b) If the climb takes 2.0 s, what average power did they produce?
(c) And if they walk it in 8.0 s instead?

Given:

m = 70 kg

Δh = 3.0 m

Δt = 2.0 s, then 8.0 s

The diagram is making one point: the horizontal travel along the treads is irrelevant. Gravity acts vertically, so only the vertical displacement produces work against it. Students reliably try to use the length of the staircase. Part (c) is there to separate work from power in the one place where the distinction is unavoidable — identical work, four times the time, a quarter of the power.

Problem 5 — solution

Same work, very different power

ΔUg = m · g · Δh = (70)(9.81)(3.0)

ΔUg ≈ 2060 J

P = W / t = 2060 J / 2.0 s

P ≈ 1030 W (about 1.4 hp)

P = 2060 J / 8.0 s

P ≈ 258 W

2060 J is the same number as the barbell in Problem 2 — a 100 kg bar raised 2.1 m and a 70 kg student raised 3.0 m are the same job.

Once it is energy, it does not remember how it was earned.

  • 1030 W for two seconds is genuinely large — roughly three times what a trained cyclist can hold for an hour
  • 258 W, walked, is about what that same cyclist can hold for an hour
  • The difference between those two numbers is the subject of the power–duration curve in Part 03
The callout is the point of putting the two problems in one lecture. Energy is a currency — it does not remember how it was earned. The two power numbers are the hook for Part 03. Ask why a person who can produce 1030 W for two seconds cannot produce it for an hour. Nobody will say "willpower" twice if you have set this up properly: it is energy systems, and the curve is in Part 03.

Problem 6 — the Margaria–Kalamen stair sprint

A field test of peak anaerobic power — you will run it in the lab

A 75 kg athlete sprints up a staircase. Timing gates are set on the 3rd and 9th steps, each step is 0.175 m high, and the athlete covers the six steps between the gates in 0.52 s.

What is their peak anaerobic power, in watts and in watts per kilogram?

Given:

m = 75 kg

step = 0.175 m, 6 steps between gates

Δt = 0.52 s

Note that the gates are on steps 3 and 9 — the first two steps are the run-up, so the athlete is already at speed when the clock starts. That is deliberate: the test is measuring power, not acceleration from rest. Ask what would happen to the number if you timed from the bottom instead. It would fall, because you would be including the acceleration phase where vertical velocity is still building, and the test would no longer be measuring what it claims to.

Problem 6 — solution

Only the vertical rise counts

  • The work being measured is against gravity, so the horizontal travel is irrelevant

Δh = 6 × 0.175 m = 1.05 m

P = m · g · Δh / Δt

P = (75)(9.81)(1.05) / 0.52

P ≈ 1486 W

P / m = 1486 / 75 ≈ 19.8 W/kg — competitive for a power athlete

Dividing by body mass is what makes the test comparable between athletes, and it is the number that actually gets reported. A 100 kg athlete producing 1486 W is unremarkable; a 60 kg athlete producing it is exceptional. Hold on to 19.8 W/kg — it comes back at the very end of the lecture next to 43.8 W/kg from the measured jump, and the comparison between them says something about what the two tests are actually measuring.

Problem 6 — the same test, with your own numbers

Change the mass and watch what does not move

Leave it on the lecture's numbers first — 1486 W, 19.8 W/kg, right at the edge of the power-athlete band. Then change the mass without changing anything else. The watts go up and the W/kg does not move, because both sides scale together. That is the cleanest demonstration of why the test is normalised. Then slow the time from 0.52 s to 0.95 s and watch it fall through two bands. Half a second of difference over six steps is the entire range from trained to untrained, which is why the timing gates have to be good.

Problem 7 — elastic potential energy

A gymnast on a beat board

A gymnast lands on a beat board of stiffness k = 8600 N/m and compresses it 0.07 m.

(a) How much elastic energy is stored in the board?
(b) If all of it were returned to a 55 kg gymnast, how much extra height would it buy?

Given:

k = 8600 N/m

x = 0.07 m

m = 55 kg

Ask for a guess on (a) first. 8600 N/m sounds enormous and 7 cm sounds small, and students tend to guess high. It comes out at 21 J, which is a small number — about the energy of lifting the gymnast 4 cm. That is the honest lesson of this problem and the reason it is worth setting: a beat board is not primarily an energy store. It works mostly by changing the TIMING of the ground reaction force, not by banking a big chunk of energy.

Problem 7 — solution

A small number, and that is the point

(a)

Ue = ½ · k · x² = ½(8600)(0.07)²

Ue = ½(8600)(0.0049)

Ue ≈ 21 J

(b) set the returned elastic energy equal to a gain in height

Ue = m · g · Δh

Δh = 21 / (55 × 9.81)

Δh ≈ 0.039 m (about 4 cm)

Compress the board twice as far and you store four times as much — 84 J, worth about 16 cm. The squared term is why a heavier, faster approach pays off so disproportionately.

  • 21 J is genuinely small next to the ≈1500 J of a gymnast's run-up kinetic energy
  • No real board returns 100% — hysteresis takes a cut, so 4 cm is an upper bound
  • The board's main job is to reshape the force–time curve, letting the gymnast apply a large force for longer than a rigid floor would allow
The quadratic point in the callout is the one to make them calculate: 0.14² is 0.0196, four times 0.0049. Doubling the compression quadruples the store, exactly as with kinetic energy. The third bullet is the piece of real biomechanics in this problem. If a student leaves thinking springboards work by storing energy, they have the wrong model. Go back to the impulse–momentum lecture: the board lets the athlete keep force on for longer, and the impulse is what changes the velocity.

Problem 8 — conservation, and the mass cancelling

The single most useful result in jump testing

A 75 kg athlete leaves the ground with a vertical velocity of 2.9 m/s. How high does their centre of mass rise?

Once they are airborne, only gravity acts — so mechanical energy is conserved.

Given:

m = 75 kg

vtake-off = 2.9 m/s

at the peak, v = 0

Ask what is true at the peak before turning over. "Vertical velocity is zero" is the insight the whole problem turns on — and note it is the VERTICAL velocity, which matters for a long jumper who is still moving fast horizontally at the top. Also worth asking why we can use conservation here at all, given that we spent Part 01 saying it usually fails. Because the feet are off the ground: no muscle work, no friction, only gravity.

Problem 8 — solution

The mass cancels — jump height depends only on take-off velocity

  • At the peak all of the take-off kinetic energy has become potential energy

½ · m · vto² = m · g · Δh

Δh = vto² / (2 · g)

Δh = (2.9 m/s)² / (2 × 9.81 m/s²)

Δh ≈ 0.43 m (43 cm)

The mass divided out of both sides. Measure take-off velocity — or flight time — and you have the height without weighing anybody. This is why jump mats work.

Write the cancellation out on the board — m appears on both sides and divides away. It is also why "heavier athletes jump lower" is false as stated. Heavier athletes often produce less take-off velocity, which is a different claim, and it is about force production rather than about the physics of flight. The same cancellation turned up in Problem 4(b) with the vaulter. Point back to it.

Problem 8 — try to make the mass matter

Drag it from 45 kg to 110 kg and watch the height

Set the take-off speed to 2.9 m/s and check that Δh reads 0.429 m, which is the answer we just worked. Now drag the mass slider from 45 kg to 110 kg. Every energy on the figure changes — the flat line moves up and down — and the peak height does not move at all. That is the cancellation, made in front of them rather than asserted. Then drag take-off speed instead. Small changes there move the height a lot, because it is squared.

Problem 9 — energy dissipation and the helmet

The energy is fixed; all you can change is the distance

A cyclist riding at 6 m/s falls. Their head — about 8.1% of a 70 kg body mass, so 5.7 kg — starts 1.5 m above the ground and is brought to rest by the impact.

(a) How much energy must be dissipated?
(b) What average force does that take if the head stops in 0.5 cm (no helmet) against 3 cm (a typical helmet)?

Given:

mhead = 5.7 kg

v = 6 m/s, h = 1.5 m

d = 0.005 m, then 0.03 m

This is Greg's original problem from the 2012 version of this lecture, with one change: it uses the energy of the HEAD rather than of the whole rider. Putting all 70 kg through the skull gives about 76 kN, which is eleven times what a skull actually fails at, because the rest of the body does not stop in three centimetres. Head and neck are 8.1% of body mass in Winter's tables. Ask before turning over whether a helmet absorbs energy. It does not — not meaningfully. It spends the same energy over a longer distance, and W = F·d does the rest. That reframing is the whole point of the problem.

Problem 9 — solution

Six times the distance, one sixth of the force

(a) kinetic plus the potential it still has to lose

E = ½mv² + mgh

E = ½(5.7)(6)² + (5.7)(9.81)(1.5)

E = 102 + 84 ≈ 186 J

(b) that energy is dissipated by a force over a distance

F = E / d

no helmet: 186 / 0.005 ≈ 37,200 N

helmet: 186 / 0.03 ≈ 6,200 N

Skull fracture becomes likely somewhere around 4–7 kN. Without a helmet the impact is roughly six times over that threshold; with one it is at its lower edge.

The helmet removed none of the 186 J. It only spread it over six times the distance.

  • This is the same reasoning as a car crumple zone, an airbag, a crash mat and flexing your knees on landing
  • Every one of them is buying distance, because that is the only term in W = F·d you can control once the energy is fixed
The last bullet is the transferable idea and it is worth writing on the board: once the energy is set, the only thing you can negotiate is the distance over which you give it up. Connect it back to the momentum-against-energy slide. Stopping in a given distance is a work problem; stopping in a given time is an impulse problem. The helmet lengthens both, but the fracture threshold is a force, so the work version is the one that answers the question. The thresholds are approximate and vary with the site and direction of loading. Do not let anyone leave thinking 4 kN is a precise number.

Problem 9 — the whole curve

Change the speed, the height or the crumple distance

Start on "typical" and check it reads 6.2 kN, which is what we just worked. Press "no helmet" — the point disappears off the top of the chart, which is the honest way to draw 37 kN on an axis where the interesting band is 4 to 7. Then drag the riding speed. At 3 m/s even a bare head is survivable; at 10 m/s a typical helmet is no longer enough. The energy goes with v², so the force does too, and that is why helmet standards are written against a specified drop height rather than a vague notion of protection. Worth saying that real helmets do not deliver a constant force — the foam has a characteristic crush curve — so this is the average, and the peak is higher.
Part 03

Energy in the body

Generation and absorption, joint power, and the springs

Muscles generate and absorb energy

The same tissue is a motor, a brake and a spring

GENERATION — a source

  • Concentric: the muscle shortens while pulling
  • Chemical energy becomes mechanical energy
  • Positive work, positive power
  • Pushing off, climbing, accelerating

ABSORPTION — a sink

  • Eccentric: the muscle lengthens while pulling
  • Mechanical energy becomes heat — or tendon strain
  • Negative work, negative power
  • Landing, braking, walking downhill

Absorbed energy has two possible fates. It can be dissipated as heat and lost, or stored elastically in tendon and returned. Which one happens depends almost entirely on timing.

The callout is the hinge of the whole second half of the lecture. Energy absorbed eccentrically is not automatically wasted — it goes to heat if you are slow about it and into the tendon if you are quick, and that timing dependence is exactly the stretch–shortening cycle three slides on. A good question here: when you walk downstairs, where does the energy go? Heat, almost all of it — that is why downhill walking produces so much muscle damage and so little oxygen cost. When you hop, where does it go? Largely into the Achilles and back out again.

Joint power

The rotational form, applied one joint at a time

P = M · ω

  • M is the net joint moment, ω the joint angular velocity
  • Both are signed, and so is the product
  • Same sign → generation; opposite signs → absorption
  • Integrate joint power over time and you get the work done at that joint

This is how inverse dynamics answers "which joint did the work?" — and the answer in a vertical jump is mostly hip and knee, with the ankle contributing late and briefly.

The sign rule is the whole slide and it is worth saying three times. A joint moment that acts in the same sense as the joint is turning is doing positive work on the segment. One that opposes the turn is absorbing. The subtlety students miss: a muscle can be producing a large moment and generating no power at all, if the joint is not moving. Holding a squat at depth is a large knee extensor moment with ω = 0, so P = 0, and the person is nonetheless working very hard metabolically. That is the isometric case from the force–velocity curve, and it is the clearest demonstration that mechanical power and metabolic cost are different things.

Joint power

Four quadrants — set the moment and the angular velocity

Work around the four quadrants in order and name each one. Top right: extensor moment, joint extending — concentric extension, generating. That is the push-off. Bottom left: flexor moment, joint flexing — concentric flexion, also generating, because both are negative and the product is positive. Top left and bottom right are the absorption quadrants: the moment opposes the motion, the muscle is lengthening under load, and the joint is taking energy out of the segment. The diagonal is the thing to get them to see. It is not "extension good, flexion bad" — it is whether the two signs agree. Then set ω to zero and note that the power collapses to nothing regardless of how large the moment is.

Muscle force, velocity and power

Peak power is at neither end of the range

  • Isometric (v = 0): maximum force, and no power at all
  • vmax: no force, and again no power
  • Peak power is at about 0.3 · vmax
  • The force there is only about 30% of maximum
  • Lengthening under load: force keeps climbing to about 1.8 × Fmax
  • There the power is negative — the muscle absorbs rather than produces

Power is a product, so it is zero whenever either term is zero. The maximum has to lie somewhere in between — and it is much closer to the fast end than most people guess. Olympic lifts and plyometrics are aimed at exactly that region.

Ask where they think peak power sits before showing the figure. Most guess halfway. This is the reason heavy slow lifting and explosive lifting are different training stimuli rather than more and less of the same one. A maximal squat is close to the isometric end of the curve — enormous force, very little power. The lengthening half is worth as much time as the shortening half. A muscle being stretched under load can resist roughly 1.8 times what it can lift, which is why you can lower a weight you cannot raise, and why eccentric loading is such an effective — and such a sore — training stimulus. The sign is the other half of it. On that side the force and the velocity point in opposite directions, so the product is negative: the muscle is absorbing energy rather than producing it. That is the same negative work from Part 01 and the same absorption quadrant from the joint power figure, now drawn on the muscle's own curve. This is the hand-off to Muscle Mechanics and then to the Virtual Muscle Lab, where this curve is one of the three properties you will switch on and off.

Muscle force, velocity and power

Drag across both halves — lengthening on the left, shortening on the right

Press the isometric chip: force is at maximum for shortening, and power is exactly zero. Press v_max: force is zero, power is zero again. Then peak power, and note that the force there is only about a third of maximum. Drag slowly between them. The power curve is quite flat near its peak — anywhere from about 0.2 to 0.45 of v_max gives you more than 90% of peak power, which is why the training prescription is a range rather than a number. Then press the lengthening chip and cross into the tinted half. Two things change. Force keeps rising, flattening out near 1.8 times maximum — so the muscle resists far more than it could ever lift. And the power goes negative and gets large: at 0.3 v_max of lengthening it is about four times the peak concentric power in magnitude. That is why it is reported in the readout rather than plotted — drawn to scale it would flatten the shortening half to nothing. Note the curvature constant here is 0.30, which is the Virtual Muscle Lab's default force–velocity curvature, so this figure and the app agree when you get to lecture 13.

Peak power across activities

Explosive movements produce ten to fifty times steady-state power

Start on the linear scale, where walking and FTP cycling are invisible next to an Olympic clean. Then press log, and the spread becomes readable — the range is about seventy to one. Two honesty notes belong here. First, the cyclist's numbers are measured at the pedal and the jumper's are whole-body centre-of-mass power; they are not the same measurement and should not really share an axis without saying so. Second, the orange bar is the measured jump from this lecture — 2660 W — which is under the 4500 W of an explosive countermovement jump, because SUSU-46 is a 60.7 kg participant jumping 25 cm, not a trained jumper.

The power–duration curve

What you can hold falls sharply with how long you have to hold it

This is the answer to the question posed in Problem 5 — why 1030 W for two seconds and 258 W for eight are both true of the same person. Walk the chips left to right: 5 seconds is ATP-PCr and peak neuromuscular output; a minute is anaerobic glycolysis; five minutes is close to VO₂max; an hour is the sustainable threshold, FTP, and the curve has flattened onto critical power. The shape is the point. Between 5 s and 60 s the sustainable power falls by a factor of six; between 5 min and 60 min it barely moves. Energy systems, not willpower. Note the axis is logarithmic — on a linear duration axis this curve is a cliff followed by a flat line and tells you nothing.

The stretch–shortening cycle

Tendons store and return energy on every step

  • A muscle stretched immediately before it shortens produces more force and more work than the same muscle shortening from rest
  • The Achilles returns roughly 35–50% of the mechanical energy a running step needs — energy the muscles never have to produce
  • Less muscle work means a lower metabolic cost: running is about 50% more economical than a muscle-only model predicts
  • The same mechanism powers countermovement jumps and hopping

Two percentages that get conflated constantly: the tendon returns about 90–93% of what it stores (the rest is hysteresis, lost as heat), and what it stores happens to be about 35–50% of what a step needs. Different fractions, different meanings.

This is the punchline of the whole lecture. A runner is partly a bouncing ball, and the bouncing is nearly free — the tendon is not metabolically active while it is being stretched and recoiled. Be careful with the two percentages; the callout exists because they are routinely merged into a single wrong number. The architectural point is worth making from the figure: the gastrocnemius and soleus are in series with a single long compliant tendon, which is the arrangement you would design if you wanted a spring. Compare it with the horse limb from Part 01.

The stretch–shortening cycle

One running stance, absorbed and returned

Press play and watch the tendon lengthen through the first half of stance and recoil through the second. The pink half is absorption, the green half is return. Then drag the hysteresis slider up from 7%. At 25% loss the tendon is a poor spring and the returned energy drops to about a third of a step's requirement. Real tendon is at the good end of that range — around 7% loss — and that efficiency is what makes the mechanism worth having. Worth saying that a rubber band is worse, around 15%, and a modern running shoe midsole is worse still.

Countermovement against squat jump

Why the dip is worth 8 cm

Both jumps start from rest and finish with the same push-off range. The only difference is that the countermovement jump dips first — you can see the force drop below body weight in the blue trace while the grey one stays flat. That dip is worth about 8 cm, or 25%, and there are three reasons, not one: elastic energy stored in the tendons during the pre-stretch, active state — the muscles are already producing force before shortening begins, so no time is lost building it — and a stretch reflex that potentiates activation. Students almost always name only the first. The second is arguably the biggest contributor.

Walking is a pendulum

The other way the body saves energy — and it needs no tendon at all

  • In walking the centre of mass vaults over the stance leg, rising and falling about 4 cm each step
  • It is slowest at the top and fastest at the bottom — so kinetic and potential energy are out of phase
  • Each is largely supplied by the other, so the muscles have to replace only the difference
  • This inverted pendulum exchange recovers up to about 70% of the energy a step would otherwise cost

Running works the opposite way: there KE and PE are in phase — both lowest at mid-stance — so pendulum exchange is impossible and the saving has to come from the tendons instead.

The contrast in the callout is the thing worth remembering: two completely different energy-saving mechanisms for two gaits, and they are mutually exclusive. Walking: inverted pendulum, KE and PE out of phase, exchange between them. Running: bouncing spring, KE and PE in phase, exchange with elastic tissue. That is also why the transition speed exists — at around 2 m/s the pendulum stops working and it becomes cheaper to bounce. Ask which one a toddler uses. Walking, badly — the recovery is much lower in immature gait, which is part of why it is so tiring for them.

Walking is a pendulum

Measured gait — kinetic and potential, out of phase

This is measured, not drawn: one stride of walking from the UVic youth motion dataset, 70.3 kg, markers at 90 Hz, right heel strike to right heel strike. Press play. The blue kinetic trace and the orange potential trace mirror each other, and the green total is much flatter than either. The correlation between them is −0.92, which is about as out of phase as two real signals get. The pendulum sketch on the right is the same stride seen as a centre-of-mass trajectory: two arcs, one per step, rising 3.9 cm as the body vaults over each stance leg. One measured jump

The whole lecture read off a single trial

Phases of a countermovement jump

Each phase has its own work and power signature — drag the figure to turn it

Countermovement: the body drops, the muscles lengthen under load — eccentric, absorption, and where the tendons are stretched. Landing: eccentric again, dissipating the kinetic energy — or storing it, if another jump is coming. Every term from Parts 01 and 03 appears somewhere in those five phases, and this is a good slide to ask them to name them on. Nothing here is drawn by hand. Work in a vertical jump

The same trial, integrated against displacement instead of time

Work, energy and power are four readings off one force record

Putting it together

The same framework, different dominant terms

SprintingWnet = ΔKE Concentric work accelerates the runner; the stretch–shortening cycle recycles elastic energy each step
Vertical jumpWpush-off = Δ(KE + Ug) Push-off work becomes take-off velocity, then height — with about 25% added by the countermovement
WalkingKE ↔ Ug Inverted-pendulum exchange recovers up to 70%; the muscles supply only the shortfall
LandingWneg = −ΔKE Eccentric absorption over the longest distance the joints allow — the helmet problem, done with knees
Cycling time trialP = F · v Sustainable power at the pedal sets the pace, and the power–duration curve sets the ceiling
ThrowingW = ΔKEimplement Work is transferred down the kinetic chain and ends as kinetic energy in the hand and the implement
Nothing new here — it is a map of where each piece of the lecture shows up. Pick whichever row matches the sport the class cares about and work it out loud. The landing row is worth a sentence, because it ties Part 02 to Part 03: bending your knees on landing is the helmet problem solved biologically. Same fixed energy, same W = F·d, and the only variable you control is the distance.

Summary

WORK

W = F·d·cos θ

The transfer of energy by a force acting through a distance. Positive when the muscle shortens, negative when it lengthens, zero when nothing moves.

ENERGY

KE + Ug + Ue

The capacity to do work, in three forms that simply add. Conserved when only gravity and springs act; Wnet = ΔKE otherwise.

POWER

P = W/t = F·v = M·ω

The rate of transfer. Positive at a joint means generation, negative means absorption, and muscle power peaks near 0.3·vmax.

If you remember one thing: energy is the currency, work is the transaction, power is the rate. Every problem in this lecture asked where a fixed number of joules went.

Close on the callout. If they can place a new problem into one of those three boxes they can nearly always choose the right equation, and that is a more durable skill than remembering four formulas. Flag the two things that carry forward: M·ω is the form used from Angular Kinetics onward, and the stretch–shortening cycle is what the jump lab is built around.
