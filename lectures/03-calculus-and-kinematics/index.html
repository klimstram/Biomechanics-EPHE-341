Relationship between velocity and acceleration

Graphing can help us determine some important relationships between kinematic variables.

Here is a graph of the velocity of an object with respect to time. We can use this graph to determine what the acceleration of the object is over different intervals.

We know:

acceleration=Δ velocityΔ time=riserun

Therefore acceleration is the slope of the velocity graph.

v(t) = −3t² + 12t, the derivative of the displacement curve on the previous slide.

Displacement, velocity and acceleration

All three are related by the slopes of their graphs: the slope of the displacement graph is the average velocity, and the slope of the velocity graph is the average acceleration.

Therefore, the slope between two points on a displacement vs. time graph is equal to the average velocity between those two points — and the slope between two points on a velocity vs. time graph is equal to the average acceleration between those two points. Sweep the cursor and watch all three move together. Where the displacement graph is steepest, velocity peaks. Where displacement turns over at t = 4 s, velocity crosses zero. Acceleration is a straight line the whole way — it is the slope of a parabola, and a constant rate of change of velocity. Point out that we have not used a single formula yet; this is all read off the slopes.

Average and instantaneous

The kinematics equations take only two values, final and initial, so they give an average — and over the whole trip that average is v = Δd/Δt = 0 / 6 = 0 m/s, which is correct and useless.

Break the trip into 2 sections and average each. Or 3… 4… 5… 6… How many sections do we need before each average is the instantaneous velocity?

Calculus and kinematics

But what if we wanted to find the slope of a line over an infinitely small time interval? This is where calculus comes in handy.

  • Time derivative — the instantaneous change in some quantity with respect to time; the change in a kinematic variable over the theoretically smallest change in time.
  • Differentiation is the process of calculating derivatives. It can be accomplished using three methods:
    • Graphical method
    • Analytical method
    • Finite difference

Tangents

Before we go any further we need to review tangents. A tangent is a line that: is the best "straight" line approximation of the curve at that point  ·  is the instantaneous slope of the line at any point.

Slide the point along and watch the tangent tip over as the curve changes direction. Then shrink the zoom window: inside a small enough window the curve and the tangent are indistinguishable. That is the whole idea behind a derivative — zoom in far enough and every smooth curve is straight.

Calculus and kinematics

Back to the equation for a straight line, using velocity.

y=mx+b → v(t)=2t+b

  • v(t) is the velocity as a function of time
  • t is time  ·  b is zero

Because b is zero there is really only one term, 2t — so c = 2 and n = 1.

Said another way: for every increase in time the velocity increases by time multiplied by 2.

Calculus and kinematics

What about a more complex equation for velocity?

y = mx2 + b   →   v(t) = t2 + b

  • v(t) is the velocity as a function of time
  • t is time  ·  b is zero

There is only one term, t2 — so c = 1 and n = 2.

Said another way: for every increase in time the velocity increases by time-squared.

Integration

Unless calculus can help? Of course it can.

Integration — "to make into a whole" — a way to determine the area under the curve. There are 3 ways to calculate it:

  1. Riemann sum method — add up the area from rectangles.
  2. Integral sum — cumulatively sum all the points in the curve.
    • Remember that the velocity at any point in time was the cumulative sum of the acceleration.
  3. Analytical method — using equations again.

Integration

Multiply each velocity by the time between samples and you get the area of one strip. Add the strips up as you go — 2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + … — and the running total is the displacement.

Drag through the samples and watch the strips fill in above and the total climb below. But do we have too much area? Every strip is a rectangle, and the curve is not.

Riemann sum

A Riemann sum approximates the area under a curve by filling it with rectangles. The useful trick is to do it twice:

  1. make every rectangle short enough to fit under the curve — that total is too small, so it is a floor;
  2. make every rectangle tall enough to poke over it — that total is too big, so it is a ceiling;
  3. the real area has to be somewhere between the two.

The solid block below is the floor. The pale band on top is the difference the ceiling adds.

Named after Bernhard Riemann. Do not let the class settle for "it's roughly the area" — the point is that two sums, one deliberately too small and one deliberately too big, trap the answer between them. Drag the slider from 1 and watch the pale band thin out.

How few rectangles do we need?

Neither sum is ever the answer — but together they bracket it. Add rectangles and the floor rises, the ceiling falls, and the gap between them is exactly how much we still do not know.

The right-hand panel is the whole argument on one picture: the blue line is the lower sum, the red the upper, the shaded region everything still possible, and the green dashed line the true area they are closing in on. Press "within 5%" and it jumps to the fewest rectangles that get the bracket that tight — then 1% and watch how many more it costs. That is the real engineering question: not "is it exact" but "is it close enough yet".

Riemann sum and integral sum

  • Keep narrowing the rectangles and the floor and the ceiling are squeezed together until there is only one number left between them.
  • That number is the integral — the exact area, defined as the limit the two Riemann sums close in on.
  • It is written ∫ f(t) dt, where dt is the width of a rectangle taken to be infinitely small.
  • In practice we stop long before infinity: we stop when the bracket is narrower than we care about.
The integral is the limit of the Riemann sums as the subdivisions become infinitely small. Worth saying out loud: nobody ever takes that limit with real data — the sample rate sets the thinnest rectangle you can build, which is the next slide.

Integral sum

  • The integral sum is similar to the Riemann sum, but we add rectangles (or trapezoids) with the smallest width possible to increase the accuracy of the estimate.
  • The smallest width possible is the time between each data point — i.e. 1 / sampling frequency.
  • Therefore we multiply the height (y-value) by the width between data points, then cumulatively sum the results.
The smallest base we can use is set by the sample rate of the data.
