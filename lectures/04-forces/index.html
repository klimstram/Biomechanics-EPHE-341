Forces

What a push or a pull actually is, how to add several of them together, and how we measure the ones we cannot see.

Force

Biomechanics is the study of the effects of internal and external forces on the human body in movement and rest.

Simply defined, a force is a push or a pull.

  • A force is a vector quantity.
  • It can change an object's state of motion — start, stop, speed up, slow down, or change direction.
  • It can accelerate and/or deform an object.
  • It comes in pairs: action and reaction.
Push or pull is the whole definition. Everything else in this lecture is bookkeeping: how to describe one, how to add several, how to measure them. Ask for examples of a force that deforms rather than accelerates — a hand pressing a table, a foot flattening a shoe sole.

Force vectors

A force needs four things said about it before it is fully described. Drag the arrow and watch which of the four each control changes.

  • Magnitude — the length of the arrow
  • Direction — relative to an axis
  • Line of action — the shaft of the arrow, extended both ways
  • Point of application — where it acts on the object
Magnitude and direction make it a vector. Line of action and point of application are what make it a force acting on a body rather than an abstract vector. Move the point of application off the centre: same magnitude, same direction, same line of action — different effect. That is the seed of the moment of force, later in the course.

Force — units

The newton (N) is the SI unit of force. One newton is the force required to accelerate a 1 kg mass at 1 m/s².

1 N = (1.0 kg)(1.0 m/s²). Set both sliders to one and the figure says so. F = ma is the whole of this lecture's arithmetic. Everything measured later is either a force we push through this equation, or an acceleration we got out of it.

Internal forces

Internal forces act within an object or system — compressive (pushing) and tensile (pulling).

When you land from a jump there is a force from your lower leg on your upper leg through your knee, and an equal and opposite force from your upper leg on your lower leg through the same joint.

Internal forces always come in pairs across a joint — this is Newton's third law inside the body, and it is why a free body diagram has to say where you cut.

Measuring internal forces

It is difficult to measure internal forces in human biomechanics directly.

One invasive option is strain gauges in cadaver limbs, used to study how injuries happen and how they might be prevented.

The strain gauge is the same device as in lecture 2 — a resistance that changes when the material it is bonded to deforms.

Measuring internal forces

A strain gauge or optical fibre on or in a human or animal tendon can measure the magnitude of the muscle force.

But what about the direction?

A gauge tells you how hard the tendon is being pulled along its own length. It says nothing about where that pull points once the joint moves.

This is the hinge into the next four slides: we have magnitudes from the gauges and angles from the anatomy, and we want one resultant force.

Internal force vector resolution

Fibre optic force measurement gives the individual quadriceps forces during a squat. Find the resultant quadriceps force.

VL = 350 N at 60°, VI = 450 N at 80°, VM = 375 N at 80°. Step 1 is deciding what is being asked; step 2 is drawing what you know. Both are worth slowing down on — most wrong answers in this course start with a diagram that was never drawn. Ask before moving on: will the answer be bigger or smaller than 1175 N?

Internal force vector resolution

Step 3. Isolate each force and find its components with the trig functions. Pick a muscle and watch its two components appear.

Fx = F cos θ, Fy = F sin θ, with θ measured from the same axis every time. The sign comes out of the cosine — you do not have to decide it separately, and that is the point of using the angle from the positive x axis.

Internal force vector resolution

Step 4. Add the components — horizontal with horizontal, vertical with vertical. The same table you used for linear kinematics.

Rx = Σ horizontal = −175 − 78.1 + 65.1 = −188.8 N. Ry = Σ vertical = 303 + 443 + 369 = 1115 N. The horizontal pulls very nearly cancel, which is the anatomy doing its job: VM balances VL and VI so the patella tracks straight.

Internal force vector resolution

Step 5. Pythagoras for the magnitude, the tangent for the direction — and then check which quadrant the answer is actually in.

R² = Rx² + Ry², so R = 1130.9 N — less than the 1175 N you get by adding the magnitudes, because the sideways pulls partly cancel. tan θ = Ry/Rx = 1115 / −188.8. A calculator returns −80.4°, which points down and to the right. Rx is negative and Ry is positive, so the resultant points up and to the left: 99.6° from the positive x axis. Make them check the quadrant every time.

External forces

Forces that act on an object as a result of its surroundings. Two kinds:

  • Non-contact — gravity, magnetic, electrical
  • Contact — between objects touching each other, solid or fluid

Air resistance and water resistance are fluid contact forces. Contact forces occur in pairs: action and reaction.

Gravity and contact forces

The force of gravity acting on us is what causes us to contact the ground — and the ground pushes back.

In the water or in the air, the contact force is spread over the whole surface of the body instead of the soles of the feet.

Gravity

We experience gravity as weight.

All objects with mass attract other objects with mass. The magnitude depends on both masses and the distance between them. The Earth attracts you, and you attract the Earth with an equal and opposite force.

The result is a constant force on us, pointed at the centre of the Earth and acting through our centre of mass.

Gravity and weight

Weight is the force of gravity acting on an object: W = mg, with g = 9.81 m/s². So — what is your weight in newtons?

Mass is how much of you there is; weight is how hard the planet pulls. A 70 kg student weighs about 687 N — the number to keep in your head is "kilograms times ten". The Mars panel is the quickest way to make the distinction stick: the mass on the slide is 50 kg in both pictures.

Gravity and the normal force

Gravity acts on us towards the Earth, through our centre of mass. That causes us to contact the ground — and the ground must push back to hold us up.

The component of the ground's force acting perpendicular to the contact surface is called the normal force.

On a surface parallel to the horizontal, the normal force is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force of gravity.

Start here with the slope at zero, so the equal-and-opposite case is the one they see first. Everything from here is what happens when that convenient arrangement is taken away.

On a slanted surface

On a slope the force of gravity still points straight at the centre of the Earth — it does not tilt with the ground.

But the normal force stays perpendicular to the contact surface, so it is no longer equal and opposite to gravity.

So how do we calculate the normal force?

The figure on the right is the worked case from the original slide: a 1000 N weight on a 20° slope gives 940 N perpendicular and 342 N parallel. The interactive version on the next slides reproduces it — try 102 kg at 20°.

Tilt the axes, not gravity

With contact forces it is customary to build an axis system that is parallel and perpendicular to the contact surface. Then every force in the problem is resolved along those two axes.

  • Perpendicular to the surface — the compression axis
  • Parallel to the surface — the shear axis
  • The normal force is equal and opposite to the component of gravity that is perpendicular to the surface
The axes are drawn before any force, deliberately. Choosing the axis system is the decision that makes the rest of the problem arithmetic rather than geometry.

Worked example — 4 kg on a 20° slope

We need the angle of the slant (20°) and the mass of the object (4 kg). Draw the diagram and the axes, find the weight, then resolve it along the axes.

W = mg = 4 × 9.81 = 39.24 N. Perpendicular (adjacent) = W cos 20° = 36.87 N — and the normal force equals it. Parallel (opposite) = W sin 20° = 13.42 N — and something has to hold that, or the block slides. Drag the angle from 0 to 45 and watch the two numbers trade places. At 45° they are equal.

The general rule

For any incline of angle θ, the two components of the weight are always:

parallel=mg sin θ

perpendicular=mg cos θ

Where mg is the weight of the object. At θ = 0 the whole weight is perpendicular; at θ = 90° all of it is shear.

Friction

The parallel component of gravity tends to pull the block down the surface.

Friction force must be equal and opposite to the parallel force, or the block would slide.

Friction is proportional to the normal force and acts parallel to the surface. More on friction in a following lecture.

Steepen the slope: the shear grows and the normal force — which is what friction has to work with — shrinks at the same time. That is why there is an angle beyond which nothing stays put.

Forces in 3D

When we move we exert force in all directions.

Both internal and external forces are resolved into components with reference to anatomical planes and axis systems.

One step, three components

In the sagittal plane, the force we exert on the ground is not just downward — part of it is parallel to the surface. In the frontal plane there is a third, sideways part.

The vertical component is the one everybody quotes and the one that looks impressive. The fore–aft component is a twentieth of it and tells you far more: it is negative through the first half of stance (the ground braking you) and positive through the second (pushing you along). The crossing point is mid-stance. Press "take a step" and watch the sign flip.

Measuring external force

  • Strain gauges
  • Force transducers
  • Force plates
  • Load cells
  • Springs
  • Piezoelectric crystals
  • Dynamometers

An applied force deforms something, and that deformation is measured and amplified by the electronics. Every device on this list is a way of making a deformation into a voltage.

Single-axis devices

In many clinical settings force transducers are simple single-axis devices.

A hand-held dynamometer is a good example, used to determine muscle strength capabilities and weaknesses.

One axis means one number — and it is only the right number if the device is held along the line of the force you meant to measure.

Force plates and instrumented treadmills

The gold standard measurement of force in biomechanics.

Accurate measure of ground reaction forceDifficult to use in the field

These are ideal and accurate but impractical for field-based measurement. That single limitation is what the rest of the lecture is about.

Pressure — the cheap alternative

Often it is not feasible to measure external force directly. Pressure is the force per unit area directed perpendicular to a surface: P = F / A.

Same body, same weight, wildly different pressure — because the area changed, not the force. The spike is the extreme case: a whole body weight through under a square centimetre. The lesson for the rest of the lecture: a pressure sensor gives you force only if you also know the area it is acting over, and under a real foot that area is changing every millisecond.

How pressure sensors work

Most common pressure sensors use the piezoresistive effect.

  • Piezo — to squeeze or press
  • The piezoresistive effect is a change in the electrical resistivity of a semiconductor or metal when mechanical strain is applied

A piezoresistive sensor can be very thin, light and flexible, and many can be tiled together to make a matrix.

Pressure sensing under the foot

Portable, thin, flexible, lightweightUnidimensional — vertical loading only

Many sensors have been made and there are important capabilities and limitations to their use. Timing is the thing they are genuinely good at; absolute force is the thing they are not.

Insole systems

There are many insole plantar pressure measurement systems (PPMS). They offer the possibility of measuring external pressure — and calculating an approximate external force — outside the lab.

Research grade, commercial and one-off engineering builds. Even Nike made one. All of them trade accuracy for the ability to walk out of the door.

Centre of pressure

The centre of pressure is the instantaneous point of application of the ground reaction force. Watch where it goes during one step.

Not where the pressure is highest — the average of the whole field, weighted by pressure. It starts on the heel, runs forward along the lateral border, crosses to the met heads and leaves under the big toe. Measuring how that path moves tells us about the GRF vector and how the foot is being used. A wobbling COP in quiet standing is the standard measure of balance.

Insoles against force plates

A pressure-sensing insole with a limited sensor array can still determine centre of pressure comparably to a force plate.

Correlations of 0.87 in x and 0.99 in y, with RMS errors of about 11 mm and 17 mm.

Good enough to track the path; not good enough to replace the plate where the absolute force matters.

Dyer and Bamberg, "Instrumented insole vs. force plate: a comparison of center of plantar pressure". The y direction — heel to toe — is the easy one; side to side is where a sparse array struggles.

Instrumented devices

Force and pressure sensors can be added to mobility devices so that the object itself becomes instrumented, and we can measure the forces an individual produces during real activity.

Pedals, handrims, crutches, walker handles — anywhere a person pushes on equipment is somewhere a transducer can go.

Force plate application — running

Two things get compared:

  • Running style — heel strike or forefoot strike
  • Footwear — barefoot, shoes, minimal shoes

Lieberman et al., Foot strike patterns and collision forces in habitually barefoot versus shod runners, Nature 463, 2010.

What the force trace shows

Every style lands on about the same peak force. What separates them is the spike in the first 50 milliseconds — and how fast the load arrives.

Turn the traces on one at a time. A shod heel strike has a visible impact transient; a bare heel strike has a bigger, sharper one; a forefoot strike has none at all, because the ankle and the calf absorb the collision instead of the heel pad. Point at the rate column, not the peak column. Two runners can have identical peak forces and a threefold difference in loading rate, and it is the rate that is associated with injury in the literature. Pressure follows the same story

Important changes in the force profile and in the kinematics can both be linked to what the plantar pressure is doing.

A forefoot strike puts the first load under the metatarsal heads; a heel strike puts it under a heel pad a few centimetres behind the ankle, with the leg almost straight.

Back to the inside

Force plates and pressure sensors measure external forces. It remains difficult to measure the forces at the bones, joints and tendons directly.

But we can approximate the internal forces:

  • Inverse dynamics — work backwards from the measured external force and the measured motion
  • Electromyography — measure the electrical activity of the muscles that produced them
Both are later lectures. What matters here is that neither is a measurement of the internal force — they are inferences, and every assumption in the model ends up inside the answer.

What to take away

  • A force is a push or a pull — a vector with a magnitude, a direction, a line of action and a point of application.
  • F = ma, and weight is just gravity's version: W = mg.
  • Several forces are combined by resolving each into components, adding the components, and rebuilding one resultant. Check the quadrant.
  • On a slope, gravity does not tilt — the axes do. The perpendicular component is mg cos θ and the shear is mg sin θ.
  • External force is measured well by force plates and approximated cheaply by pressure sensors, which measure distribution and timing rather than force.
  • Internal forces are mostly inferred, not measured.
