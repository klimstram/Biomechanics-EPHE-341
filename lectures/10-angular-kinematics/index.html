McGinnis Chpt 6, pg 147–158

Angular Kinematics

By the end of this lecture you should be able to…

  • Distinguish absolute from relative angles, and distance from displacement
  • Convert between degrees, radians and revolutions
  • Define angular displacement, velocity and acceleration, with their units
  • Relate angular to linear quantities through the radius: l = rθ, vt = rω, at = αr
  • Calculate radial and total linear acceleration
  • Explain how a gyroscope measures angular velocity, and convert its output to a speed
The spine of this lecture is a single idea repeated three times: every angular quantity has a linear partner, and the radius is what connects them. Displacement, velocity and acceleration each get the same treatment. The last objective is the modern half — the lab uses wheel-mounted inertial sensors, and the arithmetic that turns a gyroscope reading into a wheelchair's speed is exactly v = rω.

What angular kinematics is

Motion about an axis, without asking what caused it

  • The study of motion of an object about a circular path, exclusive of the influences of mass and force
  • It includes angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration
  • Angular motion occurs when all points on an object move in a circular path around the same axis

"Kinematics" means we describe the motion and say nothing about what produced it. Forces and moments arrive in Angular Kinetics; this lecture is geometry and clocks.

The phrase worth underlining is "all points around the same axis". That is what makes a motion angular rather than merely curved — a thrown ball follows a curved path but is not rotating about an axis in the sense meant here. Point at the figure: the hand travels a long way, the elbow barely moves, and yet both have turned through the same angle. That observation is most of the lecture.
Part 01

Angular displacement

Angles, radians, and the link to distance travelled

Angular displacement

The angle moved through, from a starting position

θ = angle2 − angle1

  • Defined relative to an axis of rotation
  • Theta (θ) represents angular displacement
  • A vector: it has magnitude and direction
  • SI unit: the radian
Keep the symbol discipline from the start, because this lecture has more Greek letters than any other in the course: θ displacement, φ distance, ω velocity, σ speed, α acceleration. Write them on the board and leave them up.

Absolute and relative angular position

Measured against what?

ABSOLUTE ANGLES

  • Measured from an external frame of reference — one that does not move
  • Usually from the vertical or the horizontal
  • Describes the orientation of a segment in space

RELATIVE ANGLES

  • The angle formed between two limb segments
  • Measured against a line that is itself capable of moving
  • By convention, in the anatomical position all relative joint angles are 0°

A joint angle is relative. A segment angle is absolute. Reporting one when you mean the other is the most common error in a gait report.

The anatomical-position convention matters and it is not universal — some labs report a straight knee as 0° and others as 180°. Say which you are using before you report a number. The next figure makes the distinction concrete: rotate the whole leg and the absolute angles change while the joint angle does not.

Absolute and relative angles

Move the thigh and watch which numbers change

Start with both shown. Drag the thigh slider on its own: both absolute angles change, and so does the knee angle, because only one segment moved. Now move both sliders together by the same amount — rotate the whole limb. The two absolute angles change and the knee angle does not move at all. That is the definition, demonstrated rather than asserted. Use "absolute only" and "relative only" if the three arcs at once are too busy. Worth asking which of the two a goniometer measures, and which a motion capture system naturally produces.

Elevation angles and the nervous system

Absolute angles are not just a measurement convention

  • An elevation angle is an absolute angle measured from the vertical — one for the thigh, one for the shank, one for the foot
  • It has been suggested that the central nervous system coordinates the orientation of the segments, not the individual joints
  • That is a different control problem: three segment angles in space rather than three joint angles against each other
  • And it turns out the three do not move independently

If the nervous system controlled each joint separately, the three elevation angles could in principle take any combination. They do not.

θthigh   hip → knee

θshank   knee → ankle

θfoot   heel → toe

Come back to the absolute-versus-relative slide for a moment. Everything so far has treated the choice as a reporting convention — a goniometer gives you one, a motion capture system gives you the other. This slide says the choice may be more than that. The elevation angles are the absolute angles of the three lower-limb segments against vertical. The claim on the next slide is that the nervous system appears to organise locomotion in terms of these, and that the three of them are locked together far more tightly than three independent joints would be. Worth flagging the foot convention now: heel to toe, so a flat foot sits near 90°, not near 0°. Students who expect 0° will think the data is wrong.

The planar covariation law

Three angles, two degrees of freedom

  • Plot the three elevation angles against each other in 3D over a gait cycle and you get a loop
  • That loop does not fill the space — it lies, very nearly, on a plane
  • So the three angles are not independent: fix two and the third follows
  • The changes in segment orientation over the cycle are tightly coordinated, not separately controlled

“Temporal changes in lower-limb segment angles during walking covary in such a way that the trajectory lies close to a plane.”

  • Why it matters — three joints' worth of coordination reduced to two numbers is a large saving for whatever is doing the controlling
  • The orientation of the plane changes with speed and condition; the planarity itself does not
  • It is measurable in any gait lab, on any subject, from marker data you already have
This is the one slide in the lecture where angular kinematics stops being bookkeeping and becomes a claim about motor control. The honest framing: planarity is an empirical regularity that is extremely robust. What it means is still argued about — whether it reflects a genuine control strategy, or whether it falls out of the mechanics and the anatomy for free. Both camps have published. Say so; students should not leave thinking it is settled. The next slide computes it live on measured data, so nobody has to take the claim on trust.

The planar covariation law

A measured stride — turn the loop and see the plane

This is SUSU-30 from the youth motion dataset, the same subject and the same recording the Forces deck uses. The angles are computed from the markers, and the plane is fitted here by principal components when the figure loads — nothing is drawn in by hand. Start in the default view and walk the three panels together: the pose, the three traces, the point moving round the loop. Press play. Then the two buttons, in this order. "Face the plane" puts the camera along the fitted normal, which opens the loop out. "Edge on" puts the camera in the plane, and the loop collapses to a sliver — that is the demonstration. The number in the readout says 99% of the variation lies in two dimensions, with an rms deviation of under 4° against a range of about 100°. Then switch to running. The planarity survives — a little looser, 96% rather than 99% — and the plane has tilted. That is the robustness claim and the caveat in one click.

Robust across conditions

Same law, different plane

WHAT HOLDS

  • Planarity is seen across walking and running
  • Across a wide range of speeds
  • On inclines, in children and older adults, and with prosthetic limbs

WHAT CHANGES

  • The orientation of the plane
  • The shape and size of the loop on it
  • Both shift systematically with speed

WHY YOU CARE

  • A measure of coordination, not just range of motion
  • Plane orientation has been used as a marker of gait quality and of change after injury
  • It needs nothing but segment angles

The nervous system appears to coordinate the segments as a synergy rather than controlling each one separately — which is why the loop stays flat while everything about its shape changes.

The practical payoff is in the third column. Range of motion tells you how far a joint went. Planarity tells you whether the segments are still working together, and the orientation of the plane tells you how. Two patients can have identical joint ranges and quite different planes. Ask them what would have to go wrong for the loop to leave the plane — the answer is that one segment would have to stop being coordinated with the other two, which is exactly what you would expect after a nerve injury or with a passive prosthesis.

The callout is the example to use, and it is worth asking before revealing it: what is the angular displacement after a complete revolution? Zero, every time. This is exactly the distance-and-displacement distinction from linear kinematics, which is why it gets one table rather than a whole slide of its own.

Units of angular measurement

Three in common use, and only one of them works in equations

RevolutionAngular motion that proceeds in one direction and returns to the start
DegreeOne revolution contains 360 degrees
RadianThe ratio of the length of an arc to the length of the radii that enclose it

Every equation in this lecture needs radians. Degrees are for reporting; revolutions are for counting; radians are for calculating.

The callout is the single most practical thing on this slide and it is the source of most wrong answers on the problem set. If l = rθ gives a number that looks 57 times too big, the angle went in as degrees. Revolutions per minute will come back in Part 04 and in the tutor problems, so flag it now.

What is a radian?

The angle subtended by an arc one radius long

θ = l / r

  • 1 rad: the angle at the centre of a circle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius
  • 1 rad ≈ 57.3°
  • A whole revolution is the circumference over the radius, 2πr / r = 2π
  • Because it is a ratio of two lengths, a radian has no units
The last bullet is the one that explains everything else. A radian is dimensionless, which is exactly why rθ comes out in metres and rω comes out in metres per second — the radian contributes nothing to the units, it just scales. That is also why you cannot do the same with degrees. A degree is an arbitrary 1/360th; a radian is built from the circle's own geometry.

What is a radian?

Lay the radius along the rim and count how many fit

Start on 1 rad. The red arc and the blue radius below it are the same length — that is the definition, and the readout says 57.3°. Then walk the chips: π/2 is a quarter turn, π is a half turn, 2π is the whole way round. At 2π the numbered marks show six whole radians with a bit left over, which is the intuition behind 6.28. The bars at the right are the point of the figure. The arc straightens out and you can see it against one radius. Ask how many radii fit around a circle before pressing 2π.

Direction conventions

Angular quantities are vectors, so they need a sign

  • Angular displacement, velocity and acceleration are all vector quantities — magnitude and direction
  • The direction is either positive or negative, set by the right hand rule
  • Counterclockwise is positive
  • Clockwise is negative

Curl the fingers of your right hand the way the object is turning; your thumb points along the vector. For motion in the plane of the page that thumb points out of the page for positive, into it for negative.

Have them actually do it with their hands — it takes ten seconds and it sticks far better than the diagram. The sign convention matters most in Angular Kinetics, where a moment and an angular velocity with the same sign means the joint is generating power and opposite signs means it is absorbing. Flag that this convention is the one that makes that work.

Angular kinematics of maximal sprinting

Seventy-nine sprinters, 60 m, flat out

  • 79 male sprinters ran a maximal-effort 60 m
  • Leg angular kinematics were the dependent variables
  • Predictors: running speed, leg length, step frequency, and the swing / support ratio
  • Note which angles they chose: thigh, shank, foot and leg angles — absolute — alongside hip, knee and ankle — relative

The same absolute-versus-relative distinction from the start of the lecture, doing real work in a real study.

Panel A is worth a minute on its own. It lays out one full cycle — left foot strike, left toe-off, right foot strike, right toe-off — and names the support and flight phases for each leg. Sprinting has a flight phase where walking has double support, and that is the structural difference the rest of the study rests on. Panel B is the payoff for the first part of this lecture. On the left, hip, knee and ankle angles: relative. In the middle and on the right, thigh, shank, foot and leg angles: absolute, each measured against the vertical with a sign convention marked. The authors report both because they answer different questions.

What changes when you run faster

And what changes when your legs are longer

AS RUNNING SPEED INCREASES

  • Thigh angle at contralateral foot strike increases — the swing leg is further forward when the other foot lands
  • Knee extension range during support decreases
  • Backward swing angular velocities become more negative

AS LEG LENGTH INCREASES (at the same speed)

  • Greater thigh angle at contralateral foot strike
  • Changes in support-phase knee extension displacement

Faster sprinting is not a bigger version of slower sprinting. The support leg extends less, not more — it gets stiffer, and the speed comes from turning the limb over faster.

The counter-intuitive finding is the knee one, and it is worth dwelling on because it contradicts what students expect and what a lot of coaching language implies. Nobody is "driving through" and extending the support knee more at top speed. Support time is around a tenth of a second; there is no time for a big extension and any that happened would cost more than it bought. The thigh-angle result is the one that transfers to coaching: at top speed the swing thigh is further forward at the instant the other foot lands. That is a position, it is visible on video, and it is measurable with a protractor on a still frame.

Predicted sprint kinematics

Move the two predictors and watch both angles

Two of the four predictors from the paper, held against each other. Start at the centre — 9.90 m/s and a 0.812 m leg, which is where the published prediction plots were drawn — and then move speed on its own. The thigh angle climbs from about −4° to +12° across the speed range. The knee extension falls from about 20° to 14°. Two trends in opposite directions, from the same change. Then switch the plot to leg length and hold speed. A longer leg at the same speed also puts the thigh further forward, which is the part students find surprising: it is not only about how fast you are going. Say plainly that this is a two-predictor slice of a four-predictor model. Step frequency and the swing/support ratio are in the published equation and are not on these sliders.

Angular and linear displacement

The angle is shared; the distance is not

  • The angular displacement of all points along a rotating object is the same θ, however large the radius
  • But the linear path each point takes depends on how far it is from the axis
  • A point twice as far out travels twice as far

l = rθ

l is the curvilinear distance (m), r the radius of rotation (m), θ the angular displacement in radians

The bat is the example to carry through the rest of the lecture, because it comes back for velocity and again for acceleration. The hands and the tip rotate through the same angle in the same time. The tip travels several times further, so it must be moving several times faster. That is the entire argument for a long implement, and also the entire argument against one if you cannot swing it fast enough.

Angular and linear displacement

One angle, two points, two distances

Both points sweep the same angle — the purple arc at the centre — and the two coloured arcs are visibly different lengths. Drag the angle. Both arcs grow together and their ratio never changes, because that ratio is just the ratio of the radii. Then drag B's radius and watch its arc close on A's. The bars on the right are the same two lengths straightened out, which is what l = rθ actually computes. Note the readout converts the degrees to radians first — if you put degrees into rθ you get an answer 57 times too big, and that is the most common mistake on this material.

Angular displacement

Example Problem

What is the linear displacement of a point on a bicycle wheel (radius = 34 cm) if the wheel rotates 30°?

Given:

r = 0.34 m

θ = 30°

Ask what has to happen before anything else. The degrees have to become radians — that is always step one, and saying so now saves repeating it for the next three problems. This same wheel runs through the whole lecture, and the Biomechanics Tutor's Angular Kinematics section uses the same 0.34 m radius, so the numbers will look familiar when they practise.

Angular displacement

Solution

  • First, convert the angle to radians

θ = 30 × π / 180

θ = 0.52 rad

  • Then convert angular displacement to linear displacement

l = rθ

l = (0.34 m)(0.52 rad)

l = 0.1768 m

Note the units: metres times radians gives metres, because the radian is dimensionless. That is worth saying out loud the first time it happens. If anyone's calculator gives 0.1780 rather than 0.1768, they kept more decimal places in the radian conversion — 0.5236 rather than 0.52. Both are right; the difference is rounding, and it is a good moment to mention carrying precision through a chain.

Again, a scalar and a vector

Same structure as the displacement table, deliberately. Once they see that angular kinematics is linear kinematics with different letters, the rest of the lecture gets much easier. Angular velocity is also reported in revolutions per minute in a great deal of equipment — cycle ergometers, centrifuges, engines. Converting rpm to rad/s is multiply by 2π and divide by 60, and it turns up in the tutor problems.

Tangential velocity

The linear velocity of a point travelling an angular path

vt = rω

vt in m/s, r in m, ω in rad/s

  • For a given ω, linear velocities differ with distance from the axis
  • The angular velocities of the hands and the bat tip are the same; their linear velocities are not
  • Although it results from angular motion, tangential velocity is a form of linear velocity
  • Its direction is a straight line tangent to the circular path
The perpendicularity is the thing to insist on. Tangential velocity is at right angles to the radius, always, and that is why a hammer released at the right instant flies off at a tangent rather than radially outward. Ask what happens to a point's velocity direction a quarter turn later. It has rotated by the same quarter turn — the magnitude is unchanged but the direction is completely different, and that change of direction is what Part 03 is about.

Tangential velocity

Same ω everywhere — press play and watch the two arrows

Press play and let it turn. The two points stay on the same radius line, so they share ω exactly, and the arrows stay perpendicular to that line as it sweeps round. The arrow lengths are the point: A's is about twice B's, because A is about twice as far out. Drag A's radius and watch the outer arrow grow while the inner one does not move. Then drag ω. Both arrows grow together, in proportion. Two different ways to make the bat tip faster — swing faster, or use a longer bat — and the figure shows them as two different sliders.

Angular velocity

Example Problem

What is the linear velocity of a point on a bicycle wheel (radius = 34 cm) if the wheel rotates 30° in 0.2 s?

Given:

r = 0.34 m

θ = 30°

Δt = 0.2 s

Same wheel, one extra piece of information. Ask them to lay out the route before calculating anything: degrees to radians, radians to angular velocity, angular velocity to tangential velocity. Three steps, and each one is a single multiplication or division. Getting students to name the route before doing arithmetic is the habit that makes the harder problems tractable.

Angular velocity

Solution

  • Convert to radians

θ = 30 × π / 180

θ = 0.52 rad

  • Solve for angular velocity

ω = Δθ / Δt

ω = (0.52 − 0) / 0.2

ω = 2.6 rad/s

  • Convert angular to tangential velocity

vt = ωr

vt = (2.6 rad/s)(0.34 m)

vt = 0.884 m/s

0.884 m/s is about 3 km/h, which is a very slow wheel — 30° in a fifth of a second is barely moving. The arithmetic is worth sanity-checking against something physical.

The callout is a habit worth teaching: after any answer, ask whether the number is plausible for the thing described. A bicycle wheel at 0.884 m/s is walking pace, which matches a wheel that has turned only a twelfth of a revolution in a fifth of a second. Students who have slipped a factor of 57 somewhere — degrees instead of radians — would get 50 m/s here, and a sanity check catches it instantly.

The hammer throw

vt = rω, with only two things to change

  • The ball travels a circle, so its speed at release is the tangential speed, vt = rω
  • Once it leaves, it is a projectile — the range depends on that speed squared
  • So the thrower has exactly two levers:
    • increase ω across successive turns
    • keep the hammer at a large effective radius
  • The second is why throwers fight to keep the arms long and the shoulders back as the turns speed up

vt = rω

This is the slide the centripetal figure was setting up. When the thrower lets go, the hammer does not fly outward along the wire's direction — it carries on along the tangent it already had. Every thrower knows which way to face at release; this is why. The squared term is the thing to insist on. Range goes as v², so a 10% gain in release speed is a 21% gain in distance. That is why the whole event is organised around the last turn. The radius point is subtle and worth drawing out: the "effective radius" is from the axis of rotation to the ball, and it is not fixed — it shrinks if the thrower lets the arms bend or the shoulders come forward under the load. Losing 10 cm of radius costs as much as losing the corresponding fraction of ω.

The hammer throw

Set ω and r, then release

Walk the three chips: turn 1 at ω = 5, turn 3 at ω = 11, release at ω = 17. The radius stays the same and the tangential speed roughly triples, and the predicted throw goes from a few metres to near the world record. Then hold ω and drag the radius instead. From 1.0 m to 2.2 m at fixed ω does the same job — which makes the point that these are two routes to the same place, and that losing radius under load is as expensive as losing turn speed. Press release while it is turning. It leaves along the tangent, not outward, exactly as the centripetal figure showed. Pause it just before release and ask the room to point at where it will go, before pressing. The range uses the projectile equation from lecture 8, at 44° from a 1.6 m release height — worth saying out loud, because it is the first time in this lecture that two lectures have been chained together.
Part 03

Angular acceleration

Speeding up, slowing down — and simply turning

Angular acceleration

The rate of change of angular velocity

α = Δω / Δt

  • Represented by the Greek letter alpha, α
  • The change in angular velocity occurring over a given period of time
  • A vector quantity
  • SI unit: rad/s²

at = αr

The component of linear acceleration tangent to the circular path is the tangential acceleration

Notice that r is the common factor in all three: l = rθ, vt = rω, at = rα. Changing the radius changes every linear quantity in the same proportion.

The callout is the organising observation of the whole lecture and it is worth pausing on. Three angular quantities, three linear partners, one conversion factor. That is also why lengthening an implement is such a blunt instrument: it scales the distance, the speed and the acceleration at the tip all by the same amount, and it scales the moment of inertia by rather more — which is the catch, and it belongs to Angular Kinetics.

Angular acceleration

Example Problem, and solution

What is the tangential acceleration of a point on a bicycle wheel (radius = 34 cm) if the wheel's angular velocity changes from 0 to 2.6 rad/s in 0.2 s?

  • First solve for the angular acceleration

α = Δω / Δt

α = (2.6 − 0) / 0.2

α = 13 rad/s²

  • Then convert to tangential acceleration

at = αr

at = (13 rad/s²)(0.34 m)

at = 4.42 m/s²

Exactly the same shape as the velocity problem: divide by time to get the angular quantity, multiply by r to get the linear one.

Third time through the same wheel, and by now the route should be automatic. That repetition is deliberate — it is Marc's own structure and it works. The next figure puts all six steps in one column so they can see the chain whole rather than spread over four slides.

The bicycle wheel, all the way through

Every step is either × r or ÷ Δt

Reveal the chips one at a time and the whole lecture appears as a single column: degrees to radians, arc length, angular velocity, tangential velocity, angular acceleration, tangential acceleration. The numbers are the ones just worked — 0.524 rad, 0.178 m, 2.62 rad/s, 0.890 m/s, 13.1 rad/s², 4.45 m/s². They differ in the third decimal from the slides because the slides rounded 0.52 early and this carries full precision. Worth one sentence on when that matters and when it does not. Then change the radius and watch which rows move. The first row does not — the angle is the angle regardless. Everything below it scales. That is the lecture's whole point, made in one gesture.

Centripetal acceleration

Turning is accelerating, even at constant speed

  • If something is travelling round a circle then an external force or acceleration is required to change its direction — Newton's first law
  • That acceleration is centripetal, meaning centre-seeking
  • The force producing it points toward the centre
  • It is also called radial acceleration

ar = ω²r

To keep an object at angular velocity ω on a circle of radius r, an inward acceleration of ar is required

The misconception to kill here is centrifugal force. There is no outward force on the hammer; there is an inward one, supplied by the wire, and without it the hammer travels in a straight line. What the thrower feels pulling outward on their hands is the hammer's reaction on them, which is a different force on a different body. Note the ω is squared. That is why this term dominates everything once the rotation is fast, and it is the reason the hammer answer two slides on is so lopsided.

Centripetal acceleration

Press play — then cut the wire

Press play. The red arrow always points at the centre and the blue one is always tangent, and both rotate with the ball. Now press "cut the wire". The inward acceleration vanishes and the dashed line shows where the ball goes — straight along the tangent, not radially outward. Ask the class to predict before pressing it; a good number will expect it to fly outward, and this is the cleanest way to settle that. Then drag ω. Doubling it quadruples the radial acceleration, because of the square. Doubling the radius only doubles it. Those two sliders behaving differently is the thing to leave them with.

Total linear acceleration

Two perpendicular components, one resultant

  • When an object rotates there are two linear accelerations acting:
  • at, tangential — because it is speeding up or slowing down
  • ar, radial — because it is turning
  • Both are linear accelerations, even though both arise from angular motion
  • They are perpendicular, so they combine by Pythagoras

atotal² = ar² + at²

Emphasise "both are linear". Students coming off the last two slides expect a_r and a_t to be angular because they came from ω and α, and they are not — they are in m/s², they act on a point, and they add as vectors. Because the two are perpendicular the resultant is never simply their sum, and it is always dominated by whichever is larger. The next problem makes that extreme.

Total linear acceleration

Change ω and α separately, and watch which one matters

It opens at ω = 6 rad/s so that both component arrows are actually visible — at the hammer's 12 rad/s the radial arrow is eighteen times the tangential one and they draw on top of each other. So do this deliberately. Note the two arrows at ω = 6, where radial is already 98% of the total. Then push ω to 12 and watch the green arrow shrink to nothing while the readout climbs to 173 m/s². That is the hammer throw answer, and the figure has just explained why the tangential part is irrelevant to it. Then set ω to zero. Now there is only tangential acceleration — a body that is speeding up but not yet turning. Those are the two extremes, and real movements sit between them.

Total acceleration

Example Problem

During the hammer throw an athlete changes the angular velocity of the hammer (1.2 m radius) from 0 to 12 rad/s in 1.5 seconds.

What is the total acceleration of the hammer?

Given:

t = 1.5 s

r = 1.2 m

ωi = 0 rad/s

ωf = 12 rad/s

To get the total we need both the tangential and the radial accelerations, so this is three calculations and then a Pythagoras.

Ask for a guess at the ratio of the two components before working it. Most will expect them to be comparable. They are not — radial is eighteen times tangential, because ω is squared and ω is large. Ask also which ω to use for the radial part. The final one, 12 rad/s, because the question asks for the acceleration at the end of the wind-up. Using the average is the most common wrong turn here.

Total acceleration

Solution

  • Angular acceleration

α = Δω / t = (12 − 0) / 1.5

α = 8 rad/s²

  • Tangential acceleration

at = αr = (8)(1.2)

at = 9.6 m/s²

  • Radial acceleration

ar = ω²r = (12)²(1.2)

ar = 172.8 m/s²

  • Total, by vector composition

atotal = √(9.6² + 172.8²)

atotal ≈ 173 m/s²

173 m/s² is about 17.6 g. On a 7.26 kg hammer that is roughly 1260 N through the wire and the thrower's arms — which is why the event looks the way it does.

The callout converts the answer into something a body can feel, which is the step that makes the number mean anything. 1260 N is about 1.7 times a thrower's own body weight, applied through two straight arms while spinning. Note how little the tangential part contributed: the total is 173 against a radial of 172.8. Including a_t changed the answer by about a tenth of a percent. That is not a reason to leave it out of the method — it is a reason to understand which term dominates and why.

Part 04

Measuring angular motion

Gyroscopes, the Coriolis effect, and a racing wheelchair

The Coriolis effect

How a sensor with no moving parts detects rotation

  • When a mass m moves with velocity v and an external angular rate Ω is applied…
  • …the Coriolis effect generates a force that pushes the mass perpendicular to its motion
  • The size of that displacement is directly related to the angular rate
  • So measuring the sideways push measures the rotation

FCoriolis = −2mΩ × v

This is the bridge from the physics to the instrument, and it is the part of the lecture students find genuinely surprising: a solid-state chip with nothing spinning in it can measure rotation. The cross product in the equation means the force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the rotation axis, which is what makes the effect usable — the sideways displacement is clean, separate from the driven motion, and proportional to Ω.

Inside a MEMS gyroscope

Four masses, kept permanently vibrating

  • A proof mass in four parts (M1–M4) is held in continuous oscillation, moving inward and outward in the horizontal plane
  • Rotate the structure and the Coriolis force changes that vibration
  • Two masses oscillating in opposite directions get pushed in opposite directions, so they move apart
  • That changes the capacitance between them — the system's ability to store charge — and the change is converted to a voltage
The chain is worth stating as a chain: rotation causes a Coriolis force, which moves masses, which changes a capacitance, which changes a voltage, which is what the chip reports. Four conversions between the physical thing and the number. That is a theme from Sensors and Data Acquisition earlier in the course — nothing measures what you want directly, it measures something that varies with it.

Three axes, three modes

Roll, pitch and yaw come from which masses move

RollAngular rate along X. M1 and M3 move up and down out of the plane
PitchAngular rate along Y. M2 and M4 move up and down out of the plane
YawAngular rate along Z. M2 and M4 move horizontally in opposite directions

Whenever the Coriolis effect is detected, the constant motion of the driving mass causes a change in capacitance (∆C) that the sensing structure converts into a voltage signal.

aCoriolis = −2 Ω × v

The pair that responds is always the pair whose drive velocity runs across the rotation axis. Everything in the table is that cross product, worked out three times.

Students do not need to memorise which masses do what. What they need is that one chip resolves rotation about three independent axes, which is why a single sensor on a wheel can tell you about the wheel's spin and separately about the chair's turning and tilting. Mounting orientation therefore matters enormously in the lab. A gyroscope mounted at the wrong angle reports a mixture of axes and there is no way to unmix it afterwards without knowing the orientation.

Roll, pitch and yaw inside the chip

Turn it and watch which pair gets pushed

Start on "no rotation" and press play. All four masses oscillate in and out together, nothing is pushed off its line, and the capacitance trace is flat. That is a gyroscope reading zero — not a gyroscope doing nothing. Then take the three axes in turn and let them predict before you click. The rule is in the cross product and it is on the readout each time: the pair that responds is the pair whose drive velocity is across the rotation axis. Roll pushes M1 and M3 out of the plane. Pitch pushes M2 and M4 out of the plane. Yaw pushes M2 and M4 sideways, in opposite directions. Now the rotation-rate slider, which is the whole measurement. The deflection grows with Ω and so does the capacitance wobble. Take Ω negative and the wobble flips phase — which is how the chip knows the direction as well as the rate. The thing to leave them with: nothing in here spins. A mechanical gyroscope resists being turned; this one does not resist anything. It just lets the Coriolis term push some very small masses a very small distance, and measures the gap.

A wheel-mounted sensor

Wheelchair racing metrics from an IMU on the wheel

  • The gyroscope reports the wheel's angular velocity, in degrees per second
  • The trace shows a start, a build, a plateau at top speed, and the slowing at the end
  • Every push is visible as a step in the rise
  • Nothing here is a speed yet — turning it into one needs the radius
Point at the staircase in the rising part of the trace. Each step is one push; between pushes the wheel coasts and the angular velocity flattens or decays slightly. You can count pushes straight off the trace, and the spacing gives push frequency. This is the measurement the lab works with, and the next slide is the arithmetic that makes it interpretable.

This is the payoff slide for the whole lecture. The thing that makes a wheel-mounted gyroscope useful as a speedometer is v = rω, which is three slides of theory ago. Worth flagging the assumption buried in it: this only gives ground speed if the wheel is not slipping. On a track that is safe. On a treadmill or a loose surface it is not, and the same arithmetic would quietly give the wrong answer.

The conversion chain

Move the sensor reading and watch it propagate

Walk the three chips, which are read off the trace on the next slide. A first push clears about 200 deg/s, which is only 4 km/h — the chair is barely moving. Ten seconds later it is through 1000, about 21 km/h. Top speed is just under 1900, which is a shade under 40 km/h. Then change the wheel radius and watch everything downstream of the second box move while the first two boxes do not. The gyroscope reading and the rad/s are properties of the wheel's rotation; only the speed depends on how big the wheel is. That is a practical point for the lab: get the radius wrong and every speed is wrong in proportion, and nothing in the data will look suspicious.

The measured trace

Scrub the cursor and run the chain on real data

This is the measured trace from the wheel-mounted sensor, digitised from the recording. Nothing on the figure is drawn in: every number in the four boxes is computed from the array at whatever time the cursor sits on. Park the cursor in three places. Around 12 s, just after the first push, the wheel is near 200 deg/s — about 4 km/h, and the chair is barely moving. Around 22 s it is through 1000. On the plateau after 40 s it is just under 1900 deg/s, which is close to 40 km/h. Then switch the unit buttons and watch the trace keep its shape while the axis changes. That is the point: the sensor measured one thing, and all four of these are the same measurement in different clothes. The ripples are worth naming. Each one is a push — the wheel speeds up under the hand and coasts slightly between contacts. Count them across ten seconds and you have push frequency, from a sensor that never measured a push. Finally drag the wheel radius and watch only the last two boxes move. If you get the radius wrong, your speeds are wrong by exactly that ratio, and nothing in the data will tell you.

What you can get from it

Velocity over time is the raw material for everything else

  • Differentiate the velocity and you have acceleration, and with mass, force
  • Plot force against velocity across a sprint and you recover a force–velocity profile — F0 and v0 for the athlete in their chair
  • Force times velocity gives power, peaking at an intermediate speed
  • Push-by-push metrics — frequency, contact time, impulse per push — come from the same trace
This closes the loop back to Work, Energy and Power and forward to Muscle Mechanics: the force–velocity relationship that was a property of a muscle fibre there appears here as a property of a whole athlete-plus-chair system, measured on a track with one sensor. The shapes are the same because the underlying constraint is the same. That is worth saying explicitly — it is one of the few places in the course where a reductionist property shows up intact at the whole-body level.

A force–velocity profile from one sprint

Fit the rise, differentiate it, multiply by mass

This is the sprint-profiling method, applied to the trace on the previous slide. The recipe is four steps and each one is something this lecture has already done. One: fit a rising exponential to the velocity. Two: differentiate it, which gives acceleration. Three: multiply by the system mass — athlete plus chair — which gives force. Four: plot that force against the velocity it occurred at, and the relationship comes out as a straight line. The two intercepts are the profile. F₀ is the force the athlete could apply if the chair were held still. v₀ is the speed at which no force is left over. Maximum power sits exactly halfway between them, at F₀v₀/4, and that is a result worth deriving on the board. Use the fit-window slider deliberately. Start it at 11 s and the fit is poor, because the first several seconds are a rolling start rather than a maximal effort and the exponential cannot describe both. Move it to 18 s and the residual drops to about 0.1 m/s — one per cent. Knowing which part of a trial to throw away is most of applied biomechanics. Then the mass slider. v₀ and τ do not move, because they are kinematics. F₀ and Pmax scale directly with mass. Ask what that means for comparing two athletes in different chairs.

Summary

ANGLES

Absolute angles are measured from a fixed reference, relative angles between two segments. Distance φ is a scalar, displacement θ a vector. Calculations use radians, where θ = l/r.

RATES

ω = Δθ/Δt and α = Δω/Δt. Every angular quantity has a linear partner through the radius: l = rθ, vt = rω, at = rα.

TURNING

Circular motion needs a centre-seeking acceleration ar = ω²r, even at constant speed. With at it gives the total linear acceleration by Pythagoras.

If you remember one thing: the angle is shared, the distance is not. Every point on a rotating body turns through the same θ, and the radius decides what that means in metres, metres per second, and metres per second squared.

Close on the callout — it is the sentence that generates all six equations in the summary table. Flag what carries forward: ω and α return immediately in Angular Kinetics, where they meet moments and moment of inertia; the sign convention returns there too; and v = rω is already in use in the lab.
