Static Analysis

Two conditions, three equations, and the forces inside a body that nobody can measure.

This is the lecture where the course pays out. For eighteen lectures we have been building up the language — kinematics to describe motion, kinetics to describe what causes it, Newton's laws to connect them. Today that language does something a student can use: it tells you how hard a muscle is pulling, from measurements taken entirely outside the body. Say the aim out loud at the start. By the end of this hour you will be able to take a photograph of someone holding still, four measurements off it, and compute a tendon force. That is a real skill and it is on the final.

Static Analysis

Where this fits

Through kinematics and kinetics we have been working toward being able to quantify human activity, both in motion and at rest.

With those concepts and the relationships Newton’s laws give us, we can solve for unknown variables — which is the whole point, because the variables we most want are the ones we cannot measure.

You cannot put a load cell on a tendon in a living person. You can put a force plate under their foot and a camera in front of them. Static analysis is the bridge between those two facts.

Everything in red is a force nobody has ever directly measured in a walking human being.

Set the stakes before the mechanics. Internal forces — tendon forces, joint contact forces, ligament forces — matter enormously for injury, surgery, prosthesis design and training, and essentially none of them have ever been measured directly in a living person doing anything interesting. There are a handful of instrumented-implant studies with a few subjects. So almost everything the field knows about what the inside of a body is doing comes from calculations like today's. That is worth saying plainly, because it makes the method feel like a tool rather than an exercise.

Static Analysis

Back to the first law

The ability to solve for unknowns begins with Newton’s laws.

Linear. An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in uniform motion stays in uniform motion, unless acted upon by a net external force.

Angular. A body maintains a constant state of angular motion unless acted on by a net external torque.

The operative word is net. We have to look at all the forces and all the moments acting on the system at once.

Which gives two cases, and only two:

  • ΣF and ΣM are zero → no acceleration. The system is in equilibrium.
  • ΣF or ΣM is not zero → the state of motion is changing. There is an acceleration.

Today is the first case. The next lecture is the second one, and the equations barely change — the right-hand sides stop being zero.

Spend the time on "net", because it is the word that makes the method work. Any single force in a static problem is unbalanced; it is only the sum that vanishes. Students write ΣF = 0 and then reason about one force at a time, and that is where errors come from. Flag the forward link explicitly. Everything today is ΣF = 0. In dynamic analysis it becomes ΣF = ma, and that is the only change. If they master the bookkeeping now, next lecture is a one-line extension.

Static Analysis

Two conditions, and neither one implies the other

Here is the thing that catches people. Press the middle case. Two equal forces, opposite directions, so sigma F is exactly zero — and the bar is spinning, because the forces are not in line with each other. Forces balanced, moments not. That is not equilibrium. Press the third. Now the two forces are both upward and placed symmetrically, so their moments about the pivot cancel — and the whole thing accelerates away, because sigma F is three hundred newtons. Moments balanced, forces not. Also not equilibrium. Only the first case is equilibrium, and it satisfies both. Drag the forces around and try to break it; every time you move one you have to move another to restore both sums. The practical consequence for the exam: you get three equations, and you need all three, and a problem with three unknowns is solvable while a problem with four is not.

Static Analysis

The equations of static equilibrium

When the sum of all forces and all moments is zero, the object has zero acceleration and is said to be in equilibrium. These are the equations we use to solve for unknown quantities:

ΣF = 0    ΣM = 0

They hold on every axis:

ΣFx = 0
ΣFy = 0
ΣFz = 0

ΣMx = 0
ΣMy = 0
ΣMz = 0

In this course we work in two dimensions, which leaves three:

ΣFx = 0   ΣFy = 0   ΣM = 0

Three equations is the number to remember, because it sets what is solvable. Three equations, three unknowns. In today's example the three unknowns are the muscle force and the two components of the joint reaction, and that is not a coincidence — the problem was built to be solvable. Mention the six-equation version once and move on. Real three-dimensional biomechanics uses all six, and it is also where you discover that the body has far more muscles than equations, which is the indeterminacy problem. Not today.

Static Analysis

The joint reaction force

Any applied force is accompanied by a joint reaction force. His slide says that and it is true, but the interesting part is how big it is. Work it in his order with the sliders. Fifty newtons in the hand, a third of a metre from the elbow, makes sixteen newton metres of moment. The biceps inserts about four centimetres out, so to cancel sixteen newton metres it has to pull with roughly four hundred newtons — eight times the load. And then the joint has to carry both of them, because the elbow is what is holding the whole arrangement together. Now move the third slider, the insertion distance, inward. The load has not changed. The moment it makes has not changed. But the muscle force and the joint force both climb steeply, because the muscle is being asked to do the same job from a worse position. That is the single most important idea in musculoskeletal loading, and it is why joint forces in ordinary activities are multiples of body weight rather than fractions of it.

Static Analysis

Where the force acts matters — but only for the moment

For the force equations, it does not matter where on the body a force acts. Forces just add:

ΣF = Fapplied + Fjoint reaction = 0

And in components, which is how we will actually use them:

ΣFx = Fax + Frx = 0

ΣFy = Fay + Fry = 0

For the moment equation it matters completely. The applied force tends to rotate the segment about the joint, and that moment has to be countered if the limb is to stay still:

ΣM = Mapplied + M? = 0

The question mark is the muscle, and solving for it is the entire exercise.

A force applied at the joint makes no moment about that joint — its moment arm is zero. That is why we take moments about the joint: the two unknown reactions vanish from the equation and the muscle force is left on its own.

The callout is the single most useful tactical point in the lecture and it is worth writing on the board. You have three unknowns: M, Rx and Ry. You have three equations. You could solve the system simultaneously and it would be miserable. Instead, take moments about the joint. Both reactions act at the joint, so both have a moment arm of zero, so both drop out of the moment equation entirely — and you are left with one equation in one unknown, which is M. Solve that, then put M into the two force equations and read off Rx and Ry one at a time. Three equations, solved in sequence, never simultaneously. Every static problem in this course is built to work that way.

The method

A free body diagram, a force table, three equations. Always in that order.

Static Analysis

Example problem — the iron cross

Using the diagram of the right arm of the gymnast, find the magnitude of the muscle force (M) and the joint reaction forces at the shoulder.

Given

  • the arm is held horizontal
  • force of the rings = 400 N, located 0.88 m from the shoulder
  • the ring force acts at 80° above the negative X axis
  • arm mass = 3.4 kg, located 0.39 m from the shoulder
  • the muscle force acts 0.08 m from the shoulder
  • the muscle force acts at 40° below the negative X axis
Read the given list out loud and make them notice its shape: six lines, and every one is either a magnitude, a distance or an angle. That is all a static problem ever gives you. Two of them are easy to get backwards and they are the two that matter. Eighty degrees above the NEGATIVE x axis — not the positive one. Forty degrees BELOW the negative x axis. Get either wrong and the answer changes by thousands of newtons, which the figure two slides from now will let you check. Also note what is not given: the muscle force, and the two reactions. Three unknowns, three equations.

Static Analysis

Step one: draw a free body diagram

The first step of every static equilibrium solution, and the step with the highest failure rate. Press through his five. Step one is the one that is not in his slides and should be: cut the arm free of the gymnast. The arm is now an object on its own, and everything the rest of the body was doing to it has to be replaced by forces at the cut. That is what a free body diagram is — not a picture of the situation, a picture of one object with every force on it and nothing else. Then his four. Reaction forces at the shoulder, horizontal and vertical, magnitudes unknown — which is fine, you know they are there. The muscle, at its angle and its insertion point, magnitude unknown. Gravity, three point four kilograms times nine point eight one, at the arm's centre of gravity. And the rings, the only force on the whole diagram whose magnitude you actually know. Count what is on the diagram at the end: four forces, two of them with unknown magnitudes and one of them with two unknown components. Three unknowns.

Static Analysis

Step two: build a force table

To keep track of the equations and the variables in them, build a force table. One row per force; three columns for what that force contributes.

ForceFx FyM
F1F1xF1yM1
F2F2xF2yM2
RxRx00
Ry0Ry0
Total0 00

The first column lists every force acting on the system. The three remaining columns are what each force contributes in x, in y, and as a moment.

Because of the laws of static equilibrium, the sum of each column is zero. So each column becomes an equation:

  • column two → ΣFx = 0
  • column three → ΣFy = 0
  • column four → ΣM = 0

Look at the last two rows. The reactions act at the joint, so their moment arm is zero and their moment column entry is zero. That is the whole trick, written into the shape of the table.

The table is a bookkeeping device and it is a good one. Its real value is that it makes it impossible to forget a force: every force gets a row, and every row gets three entries, and if a cell is empty you have not finished. Point at the two zeros in the bottom right again. Students take moments about an arbitrary point, get a messy simultaneous system, and conclude the method is hard. The table makes the right choice obvious, because it is the choice that fills two cells with zeros.

Static Analysis

Why the answer was so large

Six and a half thousand newtons is roughly two-thirds of a tonne, hanging off one shoulder of a gymnast who weighs maybe seventy kilograms. His slide twenty-eight calls it "extremely large" and leaves it there. Here is why it is that large. The rings pull from eighty-eight centimetres out. The muscle pulls from eight. Eleven to one, and the muscle is on the wrong side of the ratio — it has the short arm. So to cancel the ring's moment it has to pull about eleven times as hard, and then a bit more again because the two forces point in different directions, which is where sixteen times comes from. Slide the insertion point and watch the curve. It is a hyperbola, so the loss is not linear: going from eight centimetres to four doubles the force, and going from four to two doubles it again. The last centimetre closest to the joint is worth more than the first ten. That has a clinical consequence worth a sentence. A tendon reattached a centimetre closer to the joint than it was is a permanent loss of strength that no amount of training recovers, because the lever is gone. And the design question, which is the good one to leave them with: if short moment arms are so expensive, why is every muscle in the body built that way? Because a short moment arm means a small amount of muscle shortening produces a large, fast movement at the hand. You are buying range and speed, and paying in force.

Static Analysis

The three equations, written out

ΣM = 0 about the shoulder — the reactions have no moment there, so this equation has one unknown:

400(0.88 sin80°) − 3.4(9.81)(0.39) − M(0.08 sin40°) = 0

346.65 − 13.01 = M(0.05142)

M = 6488 N

ΣFx = 0, now that M is known:

−400cos80° − 6488cos40° + Rx = 0

Rx = 5040 N

ΣFy = 0:

400sin80° − 3.4(9.81) − 6488sin40° + Ry = 0

Ry = 3810 N

Three equations, solved in sequence and never simultaneously, because the first one was arranged to have only one unknown in it.

Put the answers in human terms before moving on.

6488 N of muscle force is about 195 times the weight of the arm it is holding up, and 16 times what the rings are pulling with.

The shoulder joint is carrying a resultant of about 6300 N — roughly nine times the gymnast’s whole body weight, through one shoulder, while he holds perfectly still.

Two rings at 400 N support 800 N in total, which is an 81 kg gymnast — so the given is self-consistent, and worth checking that way.

Work the three in order on the board at the pace of the slide, and stress the sequencing: the moment equation first, because you arranged it to have one unknown; then each force equation gives one reaction directly. Nobody should be solving simultaneous equations in this course. The callout is where the lecture lands. Nine times body weight through one shoulder, holding still. That is why an iron cross takes years to train and why gymnasts' shoulders fail the way they do. And do the consistency check out loud — two rings at four hundred newtons is eight hundred newtons of support, which is an eighty-one kilogram person. His given is a real gymnast. Checking a problem's givens against the world is a habit worth building.

Static Analysis

Why static analysis matters

It is practically impossible to measure internal forces directly. The ability to solve static equilibrium problems yields an estimate of them — and for most of what biomechanics knows about the inside of a living body, an estimate like this is all there has ever been.

In the gymnast, the muscle force needed to hold that position was extremely large, and that in turn means a very large stress inside the shoulder joint.

Where it gets used

  • Ergonomics — how much spinal compression a lift costs, and where to put the limit
  • Static postures — holding, carrying, standing at a bench
  • Sport — any held position, and the instant of peak load in a moving one

The honest caveat, from the last figure: a static analysis ignores the segment’s own weight and acceleration. On a real walking stance that made it about 15% high. It is an estimate, and knowing the size of its error is part of using it.

Close by connecting the two halves. Same three equations, same free body diagram, same force table, once on a gymnast from a textbook and once on a woman walking down a corridor. One gave nine times body weight through a shoulder and the other gave five times body weight through an ankle. Neither number could be measured; both can be computed from outside. The ergonomics application is the one most of them will meet professionally. Lifting guidelines are built on exactly this calculation done at the lumbar spine, where the erector spinae has a moment arm of about five centimetres and the load in your hands is half a metre out. The ten-to-one ratio you have seen all lecture is why a twenty-kilogram box can put several hundred kilograms of compression through L5-S1. Static Analysis

Signs, and the two mistakes everybody makes

1. Components. Resolve every force the same way, from the same axes, every time:

Fx = F cosθ    Fy = F sinθ

with θ measured anticlockwise from the positive x axis, always.

His givens are phrased relative to the negative x axis, so convert them once, at the start, and write the converted number down:

  • 80° above the negative x axis → θ = 100°
  • 40° below the negative x axis → θ = 220°

Then cos and sin give you the signs for free, and you never have to decide whether something is “negative” by looking at the picture.

2. Moments. Pick a sense — anticlockwise positive is standard — and keep it for the whole problem:

M = F d⊥ = F d sinθ

where θ is the angle between the force and the segment — not between the force and the horizontal.

When the segment is horizontal, d sinθ is just the vertical component times the distance, which is why the horizontal cases feel easy.

When it is not, that shortcut silently fails. The Tutor’s tilted-forearm problem is in the deck for exactly this reason.

This slide exists because it is where the marks are lost, and it is worth being blunt about that. The first fix is mechanical: convert every angle to a standard-position angle before you touch the problem, write the converted numbers next to the givens, and then just use cos and sin. The signs come out right automatically. Students who try to reason about signs from the picture get maybe three out of four right, and three out of four is a wrong answer. The second is conceptual: the moment arm is perpendicular distance, and sin of the angle between is how you get it. It feels unnecessary while everything is horizontal, and then it bites the moment something tilts.

Static Analysis

What to take away

The two conditions

  • ΣF = 0 stops it going anywhere; ΣM = 0 stops it turning. Neither implies the other.
  • In 2D that is three equations, which solves three unknowns — and biomechanics problems are built with exactly three.

The method, in order

  1. Cut the segment free and draw every force on it — the free body diagram.
  2. Build the force table: a row per force, three columns.
  3. Moments about the joint first, because the reactions have no moment there and drop out, leaving one unknown.
  4. Then ΣFx and ΣFy, one reaction each.

What it tells you

  • Muscles have short moment arms and loads have long ones, so muscle forces are always large multiples of the load — 16× in the gymnast.
  • The joint carries the load and the muscle, so joint forces are larger still — 9× body weight through one shoulder, 4.9× through an ankle in ordinary walking.
  • None of those numbers can be measured. All of them can be computed, from a force plate and a camera.

And the honest limit: a static analysis ignores the segment’s own weight and acceleration. Measured against a full inverse-dynamics solution of the same stride, it ran about 15% high. Next lecture the zeros become ma, and that error goes away.
