General Kinematics

By the end of this lecture you should be able to…

  • Explain why the velocity of a segment depends on the frame of reference you measure it from
  • Write the general kinematics equation VA/G = VA/B + VB/G
  • Calculate the tangential velocity of a segment as VA/B = ωr
  • Resolve each velocity into x and y components and add them component by component
  • Solve for the magnitude and direction of the resultant velocity
  • Extend the equation along a chain of joints, and use it on a real stride
General kinematics is where the last two lectures meet. Linear kinematics gave us velocities of points. Angular kinematics gave us ωr. This lecture does the one thing neither could do on its own: the velocity of a point on a segment that is rotating about a joint which is itself moving. That is every segment in the human body. The hand is rotating about a shoulder that is translating; the foot is rotating about a knee that is rotating about a hip that is translating. Nothing in a walking person rotates about a fixed axis. The whole lecture is one equation with more and more terms added to it.

General Kinematics

A combination of angular and linear motion

  • General kinematics is a combination of angular and linear motion
  • It combines
    • angular and linear displacement
    • angular and linear velocity
    • angular and linear acceleration

This lecture will help you determine the kinematics of a segment rotating about a joint that is itself moving through space.

Look at the thrower. The javelin leaves the hand at around 28 metres per second. His hand is not moving at 28 metres per second about his shoulder, and he is not running at 28 metres per second either. The release speed is the sum of several things happening at once, and that sum is what we are about to learn to write down.

General Kinematics

Why the frame of reference matters

  • General kinematics demonstrates the importance of frame of reference for segmental velocity
  • Angular kinematics taught us how to solve for the velocity of a segment rotating about a joint — but if the joint is moving, the velocity of the joint will change the velocity of the segment relative to a reference point
  • Since there is both translation and angular motion in a human system, general kinematics is an important tool
"Relative to what?" is the question that has to be asked before any velocity means anything. Relative to his own shoulder, the thrower's hand is doing one thing. Relative to the ground — which is what the javelin cares about — it is doing something quite different, because the shoulder is travelling across the runway at the same time. Neither answer is wrong. They answer different questions. The equation we are about to write is how you convert between them.

General Kinematics

The same segment, seen from two places

Same arm, same rotation, two cameras. The left panel is bolted to the shoulder, so all it ever sees is a segment going round in a circle. The right panel is bolted to the ground, and sees the looping path the hand actually traces out. Turn the shoulder speed up and watch the right-hand path stretch out into a cycloid. At the top of each turn the hand is moving fastest, because the two velocities point the same way; at the bottom they oppose and it slows right down. The reading at the bottom is the whole lecture in two numbers: the hand's speed about the shoulder never changes, and its speed over the ground never stops changing.

General Kinematics

What we already know, and what is missing

  • From angular kinematics we learned how to determine the velocity of A relative to B in both angular and linear terms
  • But what if the whole system was moving?
  • That is, if A is moving relative to B, and B were moving relative to some other joint or the ground
  • How would we determine the velocity of A relative to the ground?
This is the figure from angular kinematics, and until now B has been nailed down. Everything we did with ωr assumed the axis stayed put. Ask the room where that assumption holds in a human being. Pretty much nowhere — the only joint that is genuinely fixed is the one you are standing on, and only while you are standing still.

General Kinematics

The general kinematics equation

The velocity of a segment relative to the ground (VA/G) is the vector sum of the segment's velocity relative to the joint (VA/B) and the velocity of that joint relative to the ground (VB/G).

Written in equation form:

VA/G = VA/B + VB/G

Read the subscripts out loud: "A with respect to G equals A with respect to B, plus B with respect to G." The pattern is worth pointing at explicitly. On the right the inner subscripts — B and B — cancel, and the outer ones, A and G, are what you are left with. That cancellation rule is how you will check every equation you write for the rest of the lecture, and it is how you will know where to put the terms when the chain gets longer.

General Kinematics

Where…

VA/G

is the linear velocity of A with respect to the ground

VA/B

is the tangential velocity of A with respect to B

VB/G

is the linear velocity of B with respect to the ground

Only the middle one is tangential — it is the term that comes from rotation, and it is always perpendicular to the segment. The other two are ordinary linear velocities.

The middle term is the one students lose marks on. It is tangential, which means two things: its size is ωr, and its direction is at right angles to the segment, not along it. If you ever find yourself drawing V A-on-B pointing down the length of the segment, you have made an error — a rigid segment cannot get longer, so there is nothing to move along it.

General Kinematics

The tangential term is the one we have to build

The equation requires the addition of linear velocity vectors:

VA/G = VA/B + VB/G

Assuming that we know VB/G and the angular velocity of A about B, then we need to determine VA/B, which is the tangential velocity of A about B:

VA/B = ωr

where ω is the angular velocity of A about B and r is the radial length from B to A.

ωr is the bridge back to last week. Nothing new in it at all — it is the same v equals r omega we used for the wheelchair wheel, and the same one we will use for the bicycle in a few slides. What is new is that it is now only one of two terms, and that it has a direction we have to respect when we add it to the other one. However, before we simply add the vectors, we need to break them apart into x and y components.

General Kinematics

One equation for each directional component

This means that we need an equation for each directional component:

VA/Gx = VA/Bx + VB/Gx

VA/Gy = VA/By + VB/Gy

Then we need to use trigonometry to break up VA/B and VB/G into x and y components, and solve for the resultant velocity VA/G:

VA/G2 = VA/Gx2 + VA/Gy2

This is the part that is not new either — it is vector addition from lecture 4, applied to velocities instead of forces. The rule is the same as it always was: you may only add components to components. You cannot add a 0.68 and a 10 unless they point the same way. In the bicycle problem coming up they happen to, which is why that problem is the easy one; in the pitcher problem they do not, and that is why it takes six slides.

General Kinematics

Adding the two vectors

Build the sum by hand. The blue arrow is the tangential term and it stays perpendicular to the segment no matter where you put the segment — drag θ around and watch it stay at right angles. The green arrow is the joint's own velocity and it does not care about the segment at all. Put them tip to tail and the red arrow closes the triangle. Two things are worth pausing on. Set θ so the two arrows line up and the resultant is simply the sum of the two numbers. Set them opposite and it is the difference — and if ωr equals V B-on-G the point is momentarily stationary over the ground, which is exactly what the bottom of a rolling wheel does.

General Kinematics

Breaking each vector into x and y

Same two vectors, now resolved. The left panel does the trigonometry; the right one stacks the components and adds them. The arithmetic to narrate: x components add to x components, y to y, and only then do you go back to Pythagoras for the resultant. Students who try to add the magnitudes directly get a number that is too big, every time, unless the vectors happen to be parallel. Note the angle readout at the bottom. The direction of the resultant is an answer in its own right — in the pitcher problem the marker is as interested in the 23 degrees as in the 3.99.

General Kinematics

Example Problem

What is the linear velocity of a point on the top of a bicycle wheel (radius = 34 cm) if the wheel has an angular velocity of 2 rad/s and the bicycle has a velocity of 10 m/s?

Set it up before solving it. A is the point on the top of the tyre, B is the hub. The hub is the moving joint; the point on the rim is the thing rotating about it. Worth saying out loud that these two numbers are given independently — this wheel is not necessarily rolling without slipping. Treat ω and the bicycle's speed as two separate pieces of information, which is how the problem is written.

General Kinematics

Solution

First make an equation for the velocity of A with respect to the ground:

VA/G = VA/B + VB/G

Then solve for VA/B:

VA/B = rω

VA/B = (0.34 m)(2 rad/s)

VA/B = 0.68 m/s

Radians are dimensionless, so rad/s times metres gives metres per second and the units come out right without any conversion. 0.68 m/s is the speed of that point about the hub. Not over the ground — about the hub. That distinction is the whole reason the next slide exists.

General Kinematics

Solution (continued)

Then add the velocities to solve for VA/G:

VA/G = VA/B + VB/G

VA/G = 0.68 m/s + 10 m/s

VA/G = 10.68 m/s

At the top of the wheel the tangential velocity points forward, the same way the bicycle is going — so the two simply add. No trigonometry is needed because both vectors lie along x.

This is the easy case and it is easy for one reason only: both vectors are horizontal, so the vector addition collapses into ordinary arithmetic. Ask the room what the answer would be for the point at the bottom of the wheel before moving on. 10 minus 0.68 is 9.32. Then ask about the point at the front, where the tangential velocity is straight down — now you genuinely do need Pythagoras, and the next slide lets you check it.

General Kinematics

Every point on the wheel at once

Drag the point round the rim. At the top you get his 10.68. At the bottom, 9.32. At the front and back the tangential velocity is vertical and the resultant is 10.02 at a small angle — the arithmetic he set up, now answered everywhere at once. Then press roll. Rolling without slipping ties ω to the speed: omega equals v over r, which for 10 m/s and a 0.34 m wheel is 29.4 rad/s, not 2. Now the bottom of the wheel is exactly stationary and the top is doing twice the bike's speed. That is why the spokes blur at the top of a moving wheel and look sharp at the bottom, and it is worth getting the class to look at a photograph of a cyclist to see it. Say clearly that his problem does not assume rolling — the two numbers are given separately and 2 rad/s is a slipping wheel. Both are legitimate; they are different questions.

General Kinematics

Example Problem

A baseball pitcher's shoulder is translating forward as his arm rotates about the shoulder. The objective of the motion is to deliver the required velocity (magnitude and direction) to the ball before it leaves his hand. Assume that the radial distance of the hand from the shoulder (r) is 0.58 m, θ is 50 degrees and ω is 4.2 rad/s. Assume also that the shoulder has a velocity of 1.8 m/s acting horizontally with respect to the ground. What is the velocity of the hand with respect to the ground (i.e. VH/G)?

Same equation, harder geometry. Here the two vectors are not parallel, so this is the one that teaches the method. Note the letters change — H for hand, S for shoulder — but the structure does not. H with respect to G equals H with respect to S plus S with respect to G. Inner subscripts cancel.

General Kinematics

Draw a diagram

For simplicity, let us draw another diagram with all of the variables labelled on a rigid segment representing the arm.

The arm becomes one straight segment from shoulder to hand. The shoulder is the moving joint B; the hand is the rotating point A.

Drawing the free-body-style diagram is not an optional tidying-up step. Every piece of trigonometry later in this problem is read off this picture, and if θ is put in the wrong corner the answer is wrong in a way that no amount of careful arithmetic will catch.

General Kinematics

Develop an equation to describe the motion of the arm

The velocity is measured relative to the ground and is the vector sum of the ball's velocity relative to the shoulder and the shoulder relative to the ground.

The velocity of the Hand with respect to the ground (VH/G) is equal to the velocity of the Hand with respect to the Shoulder (VH/S) plus the velocity of the Shoulder with respect to the Ground (VS/G).

VH/G = VH/S + VS/G

Everything needed is now on one picture: r, θ, ω and the shoulder's velocity. Four numbers, and the rest is method.

General Kinematics

Separate the equation into vertical (y) and horizontal (x) components

We also know that VH/G is equal to

VH/G = VH/S + VS/G

Therefore it follows that

VH/Gx = VH/Sx + VS/Gx

VH/Gy = VH/Sy + VS/Gy

One vector equation becomes two scalar equations. That is always the move, and it is always legal, because the x and y directions are independent of each other.

General Kinematics

Separate the equation into vertical (y) and horizontal (x) components

  • As you can see VS/G only has an x-component
  • VH/S has both x and y components
  • To determine the angle to use, draw a dotted horizontal line through the hand. It should now be obvious that the angle of VH/S with respect to the horizontal is 40° because of the z-pattern (trig)

θ = 50° is the angle of the segment. VH/S is perpendicular to the segment, so its angle to the horizontal is 90° − 50° = 40°.

This is the single most error-prone step in the lecture and it is worth slowing right down. The segment is at 50 degrees. The velocity is at right angles to the segment. So the velocity is at 40 degrees to the horizontal, not 50. Draw the dotted line, point at the two angles, and name the z-pattern — alternate angles between parallel lines. Every year a good number of people use 50 and get 2.34 and 2.87 instead of 3.67 and 1.57. The answer still looks plausible, which is what makes it dangerous.

General Kinematics

Why 40° and not 50°

Turn the segment and watch both angles move together, always ninety degrees apart. The tangential velocity is locked at right angles to the segment because the segment cannot stretch. The two answers are side by side so the cost of the mistake is visible: with 40 degrees the x-component is 3.67, with 50 it would be 3.28 and the resultant lands in the wrong place. Leave it on the slide for a moment.

General Kinematics

Sub in known values and solve for component velocities

Now use the equations for the x and y components of VH/G:

X component: VH/Gx = VH/Sx + VS/Gx

= rω cos 40° + 1.8

= 3.67 m/s

Y component: VH/Gy = VH/Sy + VS/Gy

= rω sin 40° + 0

= 1.566 m/s

rω is 0.58 times 4.2, which is 2.436 metres per second. That is the whole tangential velocity before it is split up. Times cos 40 gives 1.866, plus the shoulder's 1.8, gives 3.67 in x. Times sin 40 gives 1.566, plus nothing, gives 1.566 in y. The zero in the y equation is worth pointing at. The shoulder is moving horizontally, so it contributes nothing vertical — but write the term down anyway, because in the next problem it will not be zero.

General Kinematics

Solve for the resultant velocity

Now we need to solve for the magnitude and direction of VH/G.

Magnitude:

VH/G2 = VH/Gx2 + VH/Gy2

VH/G = 3.99 m/s

Direction:

tan−1(1.566 / 3.67) = 23.1°

3.99 metres per second at 23 degrees above the horizontal. Two sanity checks worth doing out loud. First, the answer is bigger than either piece on its own — 2.44 and 1.8 — but smaller than their sum of 4.24, which is what has to happen when two vectors are neither parallel nor opposed. Second, the direction has come out shallower than the 40 degrees of the tangential velocity, pulled down by the shoulder's purely horizontal contribution. Both are the kind of check you can do in your head in an exam.

General Kinematics

The whole problem, live

His four numbers are the starting position, and the readout reproduces his 3.67, 1.566, 3.99 and 23.1 exactly. Now use it to ask the questions the static slide cannot. Take the shoulder's velocity to zero and you are back in angular kinematics — 2.44 at 40 degrees. Take ω to zero and the hand simply travels with the shoulder at 1.8 horizontally. Then the coaching question: with r and ω fixed, which θ gives the greatest release speed? Drag it and find out. The answer is zero degrees — arm horizontal, tangential velocity straight forward — and that is of course an unreachable position for a real shoulder, which is a good way into why pitching mechanics are a compromise.

General Kinematics

… but what if we found that B was connected to another joint?

If we found that B was rotating about another joint centre (C) we would have to expand our equation to account for the tangential velocity of B about C, and no longer require the velocity of B relative to the ground:

VA/G = VA/B + VB/C + VC/G

The subscript rule does all the work. A-to-B, B-to-C, C-to-G. Inner subscripts cancel in pairs and you are left with A-to-G. That is the general form, and it is why this lecture is called general kinematics. You can chain as many segments as you have joints, and the pattern never changes.

General Kinematics

Where…

VC/G is the linear velocity of C with respect to the ground

VB/C is the tangential velocity of B rotating about C

VA/G is the linear velocity of A with respect to the ground

VA/B is the tangential velocity of A rotating about B

Two of the four are tangential — one per rotating segment — and each is ωr for its own segment, with its own ω and its own r. Only the last term, the one touching the ground, is a plain linear velocity.

Count the terms against the joints. Two rotating segments give two tangential terms; one translating base gives one linear term. A three-segment leg would give three tangential terms and the same single base.

General Kinematics

Two segments, one chain

Now the terms are stacked tip to tail: the base velocity first, then B about C, then A about B, and the red arrow from start to finish is the answer. The point to make is how quickly the end of the chain outruns the base. With both segments turning, the tip can easily be going three or four times the speed of the base — and that is before you add a third segment. This is the whole mechanical story of throwing and kicking, and it is why coaches talk about sequencing: each joint adds its ωr on top of whatever the one before it has already built up. Set the two angular velocities opposite in sign and watch the tip slow down instead. Timing is everything.

General Kinematics

Why this matters

  • Understanding general kinematics is important in many scenarios related to human locomotion
  • We are often wanting to track the movement of the whole body and the segments' velocity and displacement

Walking, running, wheeling, throwing — in every one of them the joint you are rotating about is itself on the move.

Nothing in this picture is rotating about a fixed axis. Every hip is travelling forward at something near the runner's speed while every thigh rotates about it.

General Kinematics

Feet, and what their velocity tells us

  • Understanding the velocity and displacement of the feet during walking in relation to the whole body can give us insight into differences in walking gait related to
    • injury
    • aging
    • sex differences

We could use one IMU on each of the pelvis, thigh and shank … how could we get foot velocity?

This is the exam question in disguise, and the answer is the equation we have just spent the lecture building. An IMU gives you angular velocity directly — that is what a gyroscope measures. So the thigh IMU gives you ω for the thigh and the shank IMU gives you ω for the shank. Multiply each by its own segment length and you have the two tangential terms. Add the pelvis velocity and you have the foot. V foot over ground equals V foot about knee, plus V knee about hip, plus V hip over ground. Three terms, three sensors.

General Kinematics

One real stride, built from the chain

This is a measured stride — motion capture at 90 Hz, one participant walking at 1.35 metres per second, 111 steps a minute. The three coloured arrows are the three terms. Scrub through the stride and watch them trade off. Through stance the thigh and shank terms very nearly cancel the hip's forward motion, and the foot sits almost still on the ground — that is what the dark blue trace flattening to near zero means, and it is the thing a foot has to do if it is not to drag. Then swing. Both segments swing forward together, the terms line up with the pelvis instead of opposing it, and the foot reaches just over four metres per second — three times the speed of the body it belongs to. The honest line at the bottom: adding the three terms reproduces the directly measured foot velocity to within about three per cent of the peak. The small residual is the rigid-segment assumption — the markers say the thigh changes length by about a centimetre over the stride, and a rigid segment cannot do that.

General Kinematics

What to take away

  • Velocity is always relative to something. Name the frame before you give a number
  • VA/G = VA/B + VB/G — inner subscripts cancel, outer ones survive
  • The rotating term is tangential: size ωr, direction perpendicular to the segment
  • Resolve, add, then recombine. Components add; magnitudes do not
  • The angle of VA/B to the horizontal is 90° − θ, not θ
  • Chain it for as many joints as you have — each rotating segment adds its own ωr
If one line survives the week, make it the second one. Writing the subscripts down and checking that the inner ones cancel catches most of the mistakes before any arithmetic happens. And the fifth line catches most of the rest.
